Lesson 190—Physical and Emotional Health

# Building Healthy Relationships

Creating Strong Connections with Family and Friends

Heavenly Father wants us to love Him and those around us. As we develop close connections with family and friends, we can be blessed with needed comfort, strength, and support during both calm and challenging times. This lesson can help students seek the Lord’s help to establish relationships that contribute to their overall health and well-being.

## Possible Learning Activities

### What matters most?

President Dieter F. Uchtdorf, then of the First Presidency, taught about things that matter most:

  

  As we turn to our Heavenly Father and seek His wisdom regarding the things that matter most, we learn over and over again the importance of four key relationships: with our God, with our families, with our fellowman, and with ourselves. (Dieter F. Uchtdorf, “Of Things That Matter Most,” Ensign or Liahona, Nov. 2010, 21)

- What do you learn from President Uchtdorf’s statement?

### Our relationships

- What have you learned about the value of developing meaningful relationships with others in your life?

- How can building and strengthening relationships with others affect our relationship with God as well as our overall health and well-being?

### Relationship building

- Look for common interests.

- Learn and use people’s names.

- Smile.

- Carefully listen while others talk.

- Sincerely praise and compliment people.

- Avoid judging or fault finding.

- Show genuine interest.

- Pray for people by name.

- Seek charity.

- Be patient.

- Look for service opportunities.

### Personal application

Think about how the Lord may want to help you strengthen an existing relationship or develop a new one. Using what you learned today, make a plan to deepen your connection with this person. Include how you will turn to the Lord for help in this process.

If students could benefit from practicing skills to help them avoid judging, consider the following activity:</p> <p data-aid="156942302" id="aside2_p3">Organize students into small groups and encourage them to think of misconceptions or judgments teenagers might make about others. This can happen because of differences in values, opinions, perceptions, motivations, desires, and ideas. Learning to address these differences in a healthy way can strengthen your relationships with others. …</p> <p data-aid="156942327" id="aside2_p15">When personal differences occur, you may have difficulty clearly communicating your side without escalating the conflict. Using “I” messages can help you state your concerns, feelings, and needs in a manner that is easier for the listener to hear and understand. An “I” message focuses on your own feelings and experiences rather than your perspective of what the other person has done or failed to do.</p> <p data-aid="156942329" id="aside2_p16">The first part of an “I” message identifies and expresses your own feelings, which is critical in addressing conflict or disagreement. It helps to lessen defensive feelings and makes it easier to listen to one another. (<cite>Finding Strength in the Lord: Emotional Resilience</cite> [2021], <a class="cross-ref" href="/study/manual/emotional-resilience-for-self-reliance/8-building-healthy-relationships/8-learn?lang=eng&amp;id=figure10_p1-figure10_p3#figure10_p1">136</a>)</p> </blockquote> <p data-aid="156942330" id="aside2_p17">To illustrate this principle, consider sharing the following examples or inviting students to think of their own:</p> <table> <thead> <tr> <th> <p class="label" data-aid="156942331" id="aside2_p18">Examples of “You” Messages</p> </th> <th> <p class="label" data-aid="156942332" id="aside2_p19">Examples of “I” Messages</p> </th> </tr> </thead> <tbody> <tr> <td> <p data-aid="156942333" id="aside2_p20">“You never listen or try to understand me.”</p> </td> <td> <p data-aid="156942334" id="aside2_p21">“I feel like I’m not being heard or understood.”</p> </td> </tr> <tr> <td> <p data-aid="156942335" id="aside2_p22">“You never put any thought or feeling into your gifts or gestures.”</p> </td> <td> <p data-aid="156942336" id="aside2_p23">“I feel unimportant and neglected.”</p> </td> </tr> </tbody> </table> <p data-aid="156942337" id="aside2_p24">Organize students into pairs and display the following activity:</p> <ul class="no-marker"> <li> <p data-aid="156942338" id="aside2_p25"><strong>Step 1:</strong> Decide who will be partner 1 and who will be partner 2. Partner 1 will start by thinking of a few common “you” messages he or she has heard. Partner 2 will respond by translating those “you” messages into “I” messages.</p> </li> <li> <p data-aid="156942339" id="aside2_p26"><strong>Step 2:</strong> Switch partners 1 and 2, and repeat step 1.</p> </li> <li> <p data-aid="156942340" id="aside2_p27"><strong>Step 3:</strong> Together, think of some loving actions to take when an “I” message is not received as expected.</p> </li> </ul> <p data-aid="156942341" id="aside2_p28">To conclude, invite students to share what they learned from the activity and how they might use some of these skills with their friends and family members.</p> </section> [/SIDEBAR]
