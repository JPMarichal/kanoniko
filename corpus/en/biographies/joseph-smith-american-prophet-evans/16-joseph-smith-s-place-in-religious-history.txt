John Henry Evans, Joseph Smith, an American Prophet ( 1946)

CHAPTER XIV

Joseph Smith's Place in Religious History

If you can look into the seeds of time, And say which grain will grow and which will not, Speak then to me, who neither beg nor fear Your favors nor your hate. —SHAKESPEARE.

There can be little doubt that the name of Joseph Smith, the Mormon prophet, will go down in history, and that with the lapse of time his figure will bulk larger and larger against the religious background.

Already the church he founded has entered upon its second century; its membership has grown steadily from six persons to upwards of a million; and, as Mormonism expands, there will necessarily be an increasingly large number of men and women who will look back upon the founder of their faith with deepening reverence. And, as time passes, the creative aspects of the Prophet's mind will come more and more into the foreground of the picture. Three reasons lead to this conclusion:

First, Joseph Smith was a man of ideas, and ideas have a way of persisting—if there is any vitality in them to begin with. The reader who has gone over the preceding pages already knows what these ideas are. They all center in man; they place an almost unprecedented estimate on the worth of the human personality; and they point the way to an unending progress in the next world, through obedience to spiritual law.

Second, Joseph Smith was one of the great leaders of men. The manner in which he "governed" so large and heterogeneous a body of people as had gathered in Nauvoo, frequently drew comment from visitors. His influence over disciples was exerted partly through a unique personality, partly through ideas, and partly through an impression in them that he was a prophet and seer. Few characters in history were so devotedly loved, and few possessed so great power to shape men's lives.

Third, Joseph Smith somehow had the ability to energize people, to imbue them with the thought of a great destiny for them, not only collectively, but individually as well. His dispensation of the divine will, he taught, was the last and greatest of all dispensations; and his work, and the work of his followers, was to prepare the world, through a widespread propagation of the True Faith, for the Second Coming of Christ and the thousand-year period of universal peace of which the Scriptures speak.

In the three sections that follow we shall amplify these points in their bearing on the Prophet.

Psychologists have revealed to us an interesting law of human behavior, which may be used to good effect in the handling of men. It is this: If one would win the attachment of others, one must impress them with the idea of their importance. This is called "raising their ego."

People are always interested in themselves. This interest is deeply fundamental. It can always be counted on. No one likes to be made to feel "small." Every one likes to feel that he is "somebody." Great leaders all know this, and use the fact to their own advantage.

Benjamin Franklin once got the lasting friendship of an enemy by borrowing a book from him, which no one else in Philadelphia was fortunate enough to own. Theodore Roosevelt. always obtained as much information as possible about the man whom he expected to meet, so as to have common ground on which to stand. And it is said that once Lincoln saved a prominent man not a little embarrassment by his quick-wittedness. The two were engaged in conversation just as a cheering crowd called for a speech, and an aide requested the visitor to step aside. "You see, Mr. Brand," Lincoln explained, "they might not know which is president."

These three men knew how to raise the ego in others. Sometimes this is done consciously, as when Napoleon would ask the name of one of the soldiers in the line just before speaking to him; but often it is done through a sort of instinct, as in Lincoln's case. The fundamental basis of it, however, is a natural love of people, as in the case of Joseph Smith.

The Mormon prophet, too, built up the ego of others, but he did so in an altogether different way from the men whom we have mentioned. He worked (not perhaps consciously) through ideas.

At the time Joseph Smith lived religious creeds uniformly made people feel "small" in the sight of God, and preachers helped along this minimizing process not a little.

Man was but a poor worm of the dust. By nature and disposition he was a sinner, doomed to eternal punishment. That was the norm. "In Adam's fall," said the old rhymester, "we sinned all." Not only was man "lower than the angels," but he was inherently unworthy of going higher. It was only by grace that he was saved, and his only real worth in the eyes of God lay in his giving up. And then, even in heaven, the race was subjected to a leveling process, socially, intellectually, and spiritually; for they all went to exactly the same place and did exactly the same thing. If men were Christians those days, it was in spite of what they were taught.

The Prophet had different ideas from these. In effect he said this to his disciples: You are eternal, just as God is eternal. Your ego is as old as God's. But spiritually you are children of God, begotten of him, not made. You have the will to order your own life, and the Lord will not compel you in any way. He will persuade you, show you the path, and help you to walk in it; but he will not force you, either in your act or in your thought. More than that. He is anxious above all things for your welfare. In truth, his glory is tied up with your exaltation in the Next World.

And then, in addition, your personality is very sacred. The forces of the universe center in you. You are to be developed, perfected, made happy both here and hereafter. It was never intended that all men should be alike, except in opportunity for growth. Mankind is not inherently bad. Rather mankind is inherently good. Only wrong ideas and influences make men bad, and these are to be displaced by right ideas and influences. Individual differences will be perpetuated in heaven. So far, then, from being a mere "worm," man is a God in embryo.

This was a stimulating philosophy. It raised man's ego to the nth power, even though, for the most part, unconsciously. Moreover, while mankind were not to be leveled in the fundamentals of character, yet they were to be so in the accidentals.

Joseph Smith knew, as everybody knows, that the difference in the amount of material wealth people have in this world, has been the cause of a great lowering of the ego in the masses. Time out of mind, the few who were rich have received the homage (and the envy) of the crowd who were poor. This was very damaging to the ego of the many. It should not be so in the New Movement. Here there should be a leveling process at work. Under the United Order the ego of the poor should be lifted to the plane occupied by that of the rich. They should have "all things common."

Another inequality suffered a similar fate at the hands of the Mormon leader—the difference, namely, between the priest and the people. Among all nations, modern as well as ancient, Christian no less than heathen, the priest has occupied a place by himself. Everywhere he has been isolated through his vocation in life. And all the while people have looked up to him as the representative of the deity in a peculiar sense, as God's spokesman, as an intermediary between God and the people. No mere layman ever even hoped to have so easy and sure access to God as the priest had. In a sense, perhaps unconscious, but none the less effective on that account, the result was a lowering of the ego in the mass of mankind.

But that was not the Prophet's idea.

Perhaps not in the history of the world has a religious leader, other than Jesus, been so looked up to as Joseph Smith. His disciples believed him to be the greatest of the prophets and seers. And in proof of this they pointed out that no man before him, so far as the records show, had been so honored of God. This modern prophet had seen both the Father and the Son, had been tutored for seven years by a holy angel, had been visited by other resurrected beings, and been guided constantly by the spirit of revelation. In a special sense, then, he was God's chosen. Besides, he had been selected to open the greatest of the dispensations, wherein preparations were to be made for the "winding up scene." That was the feeling of all the disciples, far and near, for the Prophet. And he knew this.

Yet Joseph Smith, far from being puffed up in pride and wishing to monopolize the prophetic function, sought assiduously to bring every man up to his own level spiritually. This is extraordinary.

We have already called attention to the Prophet's conception of religious knowledge. In a word, it was that every individual, male and female alike, was entitled to know spiritual truth for himself, instead of taking some one else's word for it. The "testimony of Jesus" was "the spirit of prophecy," and the Holy Ghost manifested its presence in certain "gifts"—healing the sick, speaking in unknown tongues, prophecy, as well as faith, wisdom, and knowledge. Here, in line with this conception, is his rendering of Second Peter, chapter 1, verse 19: "We have a more sure word of knowledge." (The King James version gives it "prophecy.")

The level to which the Prophet sought to raise the ego of every Latter-day Saint is expressed in this remarkable passage, uttered in 1839:

Now, what is this other Comforter? It is no more nor less than the Lord Jesus Christ himself. This is the sum and substance of the whole matter:

When a man obtains this last Comforter, he will have the personage of Jesus Christ to attend him, or appear to him from time to time, and even he will manifest the Father unto him; they will take up their abode with him, and the visions of the heavens will be opened unto him; the Lord will teach him face to face, and he may have a perfect knowledge of the mysteries of the kingdom of God.

This is the state and place the ancient saints arrived at when they had such glorious visions—Isaiah, Ezekiel, John upon the Isle of Patmos, St. Paul in the three heavens, and all the saints who held communion with the general assembly and the Church of the First Born.

The spirit of revelation is in connection with these blessings. A person may profit by noticing the first intimation of the spirit of revelation. For instance, when you feel pure intelligence flowing into you, it may give you sudden strokes of ideas, so that by noticing it, you may find it fulfilled the same day, or soon. That is, those things that were presented unto your minds by the Spirit of God, will come to pass. Thus, by learning the Spirit of God and understanding it, you may grow into the principle of revelation, until you become perfect in Christ Jesus.

In this way Joseph Smith hoped to raise all his people to the point where they would be led in their thought and conduct by the same means by which he professed to have been guided in his religious work.

Joseph Smith never did violence to the ego in his disciples through any coercive measures. The case of Pelatiah Brown serves to illustrate his attitude in this matter. In 1843 he said:

I will endeavor to instruct you in relation to the meaning of the beasts and figures spoken of. I should not have called up the subject had it not been for this circumstance:

Elder Pelatiah Brown, one of the wisest old heads we have among us, and whom I now see before me, has been preaching concerning the beast which was full of eyes before and behind; and for this he was hauled up for trial before the high council.

I do not like the old man being called up for erring in doctrine. It looks too much like the Methodist, and not like the Latter-day Saints. Methodists have creeds which a man must believe or be asked out of their church. I want the liberty of thinking and believing as I please. It feels so good not to be trammelled. It does not prove that a man is not a good man because he errs in doctrine.

The high council undertook to censure Elder Brown, because of his teachings in relation to the beasts. Whether they actually corrected him or not, I am a little doubtful, but don't care. Father Brown came to me to know what he should do about it.

Father Brown has been to work and confounded all Christendom by making out that the four beasts (Rev. 5:8) represented the different kingdoms of God on the earth. The wise men of the day could not do anything with him. Why should we find fault? A club is better than no weapon for a poor man to fight with.

Thereupon the Prophet told the congregation the true interpretation of the passage about the beasts. It will be observed that the Prophet here corrects the erring brother, but in such a way as not to hurt his ego. It was a skillful handling of a delicate situation.

As a rule, the influence of the religious leader is limited to his life-time, or to a short time after his death. The only exception to this is in the case of one who, in addition to having an exceptional personality, leaves a body of ideas grounded in human fundamentals.

Take such men, for instance, as George Whitefield end Jonathan Edwards. Wherever Whitefteld went, he was followed by great crowds, who hung upon his every word, look, and gesture. And the religious ideas of Edwards were popular, even though somewhat terrifying, in his day. But these two men now have only historical importance. Whitefield's influence ended with his death—at least, with the death of his contemporaries; and the ideas for which the New England divine is famous, have long since become shelf-worn.

The case is different with Joseph Smith. "For a man to be great," he said once, "he must not dwell on small things, though he may enjoy them." Accordingly, he endeavored all his life to connect his ideas with fundamental things, the things that endure. Perhaps the most conspicuous instance of this deeper aspect of his mind is the point we are here discussing—the selection of such particulars as appeal to the deeper nature of man. So long, therefore, as human nature remains essentially what it is to-day and what it has been since the beginning of time, just so long will this egoistic philosophy of the Mormon prophet find disciples.

You cannot harm a man by putting into his mind the idea that he has infinite potentialities.

One reason, then, why the Mormon prophet continues to intrigue our interest, and will probably do so increasingly as the years pass and he is better understood, is that he taught religious ideas that are intimately bound up with the hopes and aspirations of mankind everywhere, and that he put these ideas in such a form as can be readily understood by the average man. In a word, he raises our ego through ideas.

But another reason is that the Mormon leader was an exceptional personality; and personality is at once the most mysterious, the most fascinating, and the most baffling thing in the universe.

It is not, however, the whole personality of the Prophet, if one may say so, that is so alluring. Rather it is his psychic nature. Were his visions real, imaginary, or a fabrication?—that is the question that immediately comes to the surface when Joseph Smith's name is mentioned. Or, to put it another way, was he himself deceived in respect to his visions, or did he, for purposes of his own, concoct them to deceive the world? For, in certain quarters, the alternative seems never to be taken into consideration, that his visions may, after all, have been objective realities. If this question could be definitely got out of the way, preferably by a negative answer, the Mormon prophet would have no more interest for us to-day than any other of a hundred founders of churches. But as it is, he will doubtless hold attention as long as men and women seek to pierce the veil that hides the future.

Joseph Smith's case is that of Saint Paul's over again.

Ever since the higher critics took hold of the Bible, especially the miracles of the Bible, Paul's vision on the road to Damascus has been the subject of controversy among the scholars. Miracles are so pivotal in Christianity anyway that they always become the focus of attack by opponents; but this particular miracle was so decisive an event in the apostle's career as to challenge explanation. And so, for decades now, it has been hotly debated by theological students.

It was stated by Landerer at Bauer's tomb in 1860 that the great German's central purpose in life had failed. For Bauer, who had devoted his entire life to explaining away the miraculous element in the religion of Jesus, had admitted this miracle of Paul's, thus opening the way to a belief in miracles generally. Bauer confessed in the end that "the conversion of Paul resists all historical, logical, and psychological analysis."

And yet other scholars have supposed they were successful in their assault on the vision. Pfleiderer, setting two counter-tendencies in Saul's mind against each other, actually did away with the vision altogether. Holsten suggested a line of deductions that, he believed, had led the apostle, not indeed to a vision, but to an hallucination. But, as Baumann says, "no set of theorems will lead a man to a vision which he will believe until his death." Renan's theory that Paul had "inflammation of the eyes, perhaps ophthalmia," provoked only scorn and ridicule. And Loisy's explanation, a compound of all the others, gets us nowhere. After half a century of talk about the apostle's vision from the modern scholar's viewpoint, we are now exactly where we began.

Joseph Smith's case, however, is somewhat different from Paul's. For one thing, it is nearer to us in time, and, for another, there are more details to serve as a basis for reasoning. But at heart the two are one—they are both visions; they both assume the objectivity of religious knowledge. At all events, this modern experience affords us an opportunity to trace the development of one who professes to be a seer and prophet.

The religion of Joseph Smith assumes that every one is a psychic to a degree. That is to say, whoever wishes to contact the spiritual forces in the universe, may do so through faith. One must, in Emerson's phrase, learn to watch the gleam of intelligence in one's own mind, with a view, as the Prophet tells us in a quotation already given, to detecting "the first intimations of revelation," and the "sudden strokes of ideas" that come from without. Practice in this sort of detection, coupled with faith, will isolate this stream of thought from the Unknown, and will also increase the size of the inflow. Thus one may at last come to live by the higher Will of God.

Before his fourteenth year Joseph Smith, to all appearance, was a perfectly normal boy. Even his neighbors of the affidavits do not appear to have observed anything unusual about him prior to 1820. It was only after what is known in Mormonism as the "first vision" that any difference became noticeable to them, and that only because he displayed imaginative qualities, as they thought, and obstinately maintained his "illusions" to be real. Otherwise he might have done his chores around the home, worked on the farm of his father and of the Knights, hauled wood to Palmyra, dug wells, and even mined for silver, without attracting any more attention to himself than was given to other youths in his own class in the neighborhood. Even in respect to "human corruption" he was probably conventional.

Indeed, this "normalcy" in everything except his visionary propensities characterized his entire life, so far as outward appearance went. He chopped wood out of necessity sometimes and sometimes for exercise; he tanned hides for utilitarian ends; he relaxed in the athletic sports common to his part of the country and his times; he was considered good company by both women and men, especially men; and he was a persuasive talker, particularly in a small group of persons. In a word, he exhibited about the same traits of character as the men with whom he associated. It was only in respect to his psychic nature that he was regarded as in any way different from them; and, if he had been asked his opinion on the matter, he would very likely have attributed the difference to his having paid more attention to those "intimations" than they had. His spiritual sensitivity had been developed more than theirs.

It would seem that the Prophet required to be in a certain mood before he attained his highest state of receptivity spiritually. David Whitmer tells an incident to the point.

One morning at the Whitmer home the Prophet took his accustomed place at the table prior to undertaking the work of translating the Book of Mormon from the gold plates—so the story goes. It was an attic room, which was divided into two by a curtain. Joseph was on one side of this partition, and Cowdery was on the other.

"Oliver," said the Prophet, "what's the matter; I can do nothing; all is dark."

David explains that the Prophet had had "some words" that morning with Emma, who was also staying with the Whitmers.

Presently Joseph descended the narrow stairway, went out by the back door, and disappeared into a woods near the house, presumably to pray. In a little while he returned, asked his wife's forgiveness, and then went upstairs again to work. Everything was now as usual. It was always that way, David adds; if anything went wrong with the young prophet, he could do nothing till the situation was righted.

Prayer, it seems, was his customary way to spiritual peace and certitude. He prayed, as we may remember, just before his first religious experience; prayer also preceded his second religious experience; and it prepared the way to subsequent experiences. This brought about the necessary calm, subdued the earthly tendencies, and released the distinctively spiritual forces within. It was equivalent to the East Indian habit of contemplation and relaxation, as well as the few minutes' restfulness and meditation so often recommended nowadays by spiritual teachers as a preparation for a strenuous day.

Always with the Prophet, it seems, spiritual effort resulted in physical exhaustion, sometimes in actual physical collapse.

On the occasion of the "first vision" he found himself at the end of it "lying on his back, looking up into heaven" at the ascending figures who had appeared to him. The morning after the three visitations of Moroni, on the night of September 21, 1823, his "strength entirely failed him, and he fell helpless on the ground, and for a time was quite unconscious of anything." Partly, however, that was due to loss of sleep and the excitement over the appearance of Moroni.

Sometimes the divine afflatus, when it came upon him, brought a change in his countenance. We have already quoted Orson Pratt's statement about a paleness and a light that came into his face on such occasions. This was, it appears, more noticeable at one time than at others. The same testimony comes to us from several sources.

And then, too, the matter of making spiritual contacts in the Prophet's case became easier with practice—if one may put it so. That is, there was a development, a growth. At first, as Pratt tells us, Joseph required the aid of some physical means for this purpose, as in the case of translating the characters on the gold plates from which the Book of Mormon is supposed to have come. That was the function of the urim and thummim. There is a tradition to the effect that, as he progressed with the work of translating the Nephite Record, he could dispense with the interpreters. At all events, when he came to the task of revising the Bible, he did not, as Pratt assures us, feel the need of the urim and thummim. And on the occasions afterwards when we are told by eye and ear witnesses that he spoke "in the name of the Lord," he did so after he had remained silent for a few moments with bowed head.

There is a notable thing about Joseph Smith's record of his visions. It is the utter absence of imaginative language in his descriptions of them. If the Mormon prophet had the superabundance of imagination that his Palmyra neighbors say he had, what became of it when he set down the details of his visions? The documents recording these visions contain not the slightest trace of imaginative language. They are the baldest narratives of religious experiences to be found in the literature of the subject.

From whatever angle we view the Mormon prophet we always come back to the groundwork of his religious philosophy—that spiritual knowledge is objective, not subjective; that it comes from without, not from within; that it is inductive, rather than deductive; that, in short, it comes through experience, like every other kind of knowledge.

For the last five or six decades now there has been a growing tendency, chiefly among the scientifically trained, to look upon religious leaders in general and the prophets of the Hebrews in particular as wholly the product of their times. It comes as a further development of the doctrines taught by Darwin, Spencer, Huxley, and their followers of a later period. Specifically, it is that the prophets were inspired, not by a power above, outside, and beyond them, but by their groups.

This explanation encounters some special difficulties when applied to Joseph Smith.

The defense of this theory rests on the broad assumption that the method of authority found in religion is incompatible with the method of induction found in science and life generally. This may be true of Christianity as it has come to be in our times, but it is not true at all of the religion of Joseph Smith. Nor is it true, as a matter of fact, of the kind of Christianity we find in the New Testament, especially in the four Gospels.

As we have had occasion to remark more than once, the Prophet never asked any one to accept his word on spiritual things. Always his position was that the way was open to everybody to obtain religious knowledge from the same source as he had obtained it. That is, whoever wished to do so might know whether Joseph Smith's religious ideas were true, by himself going to the fountain of all spiritual knowledge. His method was exactly the same as the scientist's. If a pupil in, say, chemistry, wishes to verify a statement in a text-book or by a professor, all he has to do to test it out in the laboratory. The difficulty is to get people to do that with religion.

To be sure, one might deny that there is such a spiritual phenomenon as a vision, in which event Joseph Smith's formula for obtaining religious knowledge would be of no avail; just as the scientist's theory of verification would be useless to one who denied the dependability of the five senses or the fact of material phenomena.

As for the notion that all inspiration is from the group, the case of the Mormon prophet would prove particularly troublesome.

In general one might ask how it is that a prophet can get something from the group as a whole which is not in any one of its individual members. The statement that he does is as dogmatic as any that is to be found in theology, since it is without foundation in the facts of history. If any particular idea were in the group to begin with, how does it happen that the group does not receive it when it is advanced by the prophet? A poet has often been known to crystallize what is in the air of his community. But in that case the community always hails the poet and honors him. But this is not the case with the prophet. More often than not he is put to death. That has been the fate of nearly all of them. Usually they have gone in the opposite direction from that taken by the group to which they originally belonged. Thus if the prophet goes with his age, he is not a prophet; if he goes counter to his time, he is crucified.

How does this theory of inspiration fare in the case of the Book of Mormon? The plates were tangible things—if they existed at all. They weighed about fifty pounds. The Book of Mormon is supposed to have come from them through a translation made by the Prophet by means of the urim and thummim. The urim and thummim, too, was a material object. So also was the breast-plate. In what sense did these objects belong to the group, taken singly or collectively? The question is an affront to our intelligence.

But the plates did not exist? Very well. Then the man who said they did was an egregious liar, and could not have been a prophet. For see what we must believe if we suppose that he only fancied the plates to be real. We must believe that he imagined he took them out of the hill; that he imagined he carried their fifty pounds home from the hill; that he imagined he was attacked three times on the way home; that he imagined he had to hide them here and there, to keep them from being stolen; that he imagined he had them before him for months, while he worked on them every day; that he not only imagined he showed them to eight men, but that these eight men also imagined they saw and felt the plates. If that does not tax one's credulity, it would be extremely hard to find anything that would.

Besides, the Book of Mormon contains all sorts of ideas, in addition to a story, that were the very opposite of those entertained by any one in Palmyra, or by any one elsewhere, for that matter, in the United States—let alone by any group-mind anywhere. The reader knows what these ideas were. If Joseph Smith received his inspiration from his group, how does it come about that the group did not accept his book with joy? The pathetic fact is that they not only rejected with scorn and anger the ideas he taught, but sought to do away with the author of them. Which might at least suggest that we look otherwhere for their source than to any group-mind.

The assumption in the whole theory of course is that everything must be explained, and explained, too, in terms of science. Not everything can be explained. Love, for example, is the commonest and the most potent influence in human life; yet who would be so venturesome as to suggest an ultimate explanation of its mystic power? Indeed, there are even material phenomena that up to the present have never been accounted for—the radio, for instance, the transmission of the voice over the air from the speaker to the listener, for thousands of miles without apparent means of communication.

Thus the Mormon prophet, in his character of revealer and restorer of truth, as his followers believe, will always be the subject of study, because he forms a sort of medium between the world of matter and the world of spirit. In his capacity of seer and prophet, rather than as the founder of a religion, he will continue to pique curiosity and to challenge explanation for many generations yet to come, not only in America, but throughout the world.

More than one American historian has called attention to a general feeling in the colonists that the government and the nation they were forming were destined to do great things for the world. This feeling existed in every one who came to the new land, it would seem, but it was especially strong in the men who took a leading part in the founding of the New Republic.

How did this idea of a destiny for the new nation find its way into the colonial mind? No historian has ventured to say. That it was there, is certain. The atmosphere was charged with it. Immigrants breathed it with the air, with the exhalations of the fresh, virgin earth, the great forests, and the clear streams that flowed down from the mountains. Almost at once they came to feel that somehow the political hopes of mankind were bound up with what they were doing in the new home. It was an energizing belief.

Not only so. The average American of to-day, after a century and a half, thinks almost the same thing. He, too, believes in the destiny of his nation and government, and you cannot tell him anything to the contrary. That nation and that government, in his opinion, have already had a share, and a large, controlling share, too, in shaping the growing political ideals of the race.

Well, almost exactly the same thing happened in the minds of the men and women who constituted the following of Joseph Smith. Only, in this case the idea was far more definite, both in its form and in its sources, than in the case of the colonists or their descendants. And it persists with gathering momentum in the membership of the organization which he established.

The Mormon prophet, as we have seen, fell in with the idea and feeling of his fellow Americans that the nation had a destiny in the world, and it is quite conceivable that he had caught the inspiration from the group in which he found himself. But he carried this idea and feeling, not only to lengths that he did not find in either the group-mind or the individual mind of his age, but to lengths to which they would not have been willing to follow him, even if they had understood his thought.

We have already called attention to this phase of the Prophet's teachings in another connection, but it may be mentioned again in this place as having special bearing.

The Mormon leader taught, as stated, that America is a choice land, choice above all other lands on earth; that it was dedicated, while the earth was forming, to be the cradle of liberty; that, in consequence, God hid it from the nations of the Old World, with their wrong-headed notions of government and religion, till people with ideas resembling the approved pattern could be planted here; and that, when those people did come here, with their fierce passion for political and religious liberty, Jehovah fought their battles, not indeed for them but with them, in order that their ideals of freedom might prevail.

More than that. The thing that was in the mind of God all the time, according to Joseph Smith, was the setting up of the True Church here. And so, with this purpose in view, the Spirit of God "wrought upon" the minds of men for hundreds of years, each great event bringing the consummation nearer. Thus this modern prophet accounted for the Revival of Learning, the Protestant Reformation, the Discovery of America by Columbus, the Settlement of the New World by the Pilgrims and others, the ultimate Success of the American Arms, and the Setting up of the American Government and Nation. All this was but the prologue to the Drama that was to be enacted here in the "last days."

When, therefore, Joseph Smith's followers noted how political thought and practices throughout the world were being gradually changed through the influence of American political ideals, they saw more than met the eye. To them there was an invisible hand moving the checkers on the board, with a view to end the game. So, too, when they beheld their country developing into a world power, and then into the leading nation on the globe. And they would be proud to see the United States take the lead in ushering in that Era of which the ancient prophets spoke, when men "shall beat their swords into plowshares and their spears into pruning hooks."

Now, according to Joseph Smith some important world-events are to take place in the "last days." We have mentioned this belief in another place, but it deserves amplification here.

One of these is the Second Coming of Christ. Says the Prophet on this point, speaking for the Master:

Verily, verily, I say unto you, the time is soon at hand that I shall come in a cloud with power and great glory. And it shall be a great day at the time of my coming, for all nations shall tremble.

But before that great day shall come, the sun shall be darkened, and the moon be turned into blood; the stars shall refuse their shining, and some shall fall, and great destructions await the wicked.

Prepare ye, prepare ye for that which is to come, for the Lord is nigh. His anger is kindled, and his sword is bathed in heaven, and it shall fall upon the inhabitants of the earth. The arm of the Lord is revealed. The day cometh that they who will not hear the voice of the Lord, neither the voice of his servants, neither give heed to the words of the prophets and apostles, shall be cut off from among the people.

For they have strayed from mine ordinances, and have broken mine everlasting covenant. They seek not the Lord to establish his righteousness, but every man walketh in his own way, whose image is in the likeness of the world and whose substance is that of an idol, which waxeth old and shall perish in Babylon, even Babylon the Great, which shall fall.

This event will immediately precede the ushering in of the millennial reign of Christ—which is the next important world-event in the Mormon eschatology. Says the Prophet, speaking again in the name of the Lord:

I will reveal myself from heaven with power and great glory, with all the hosts thereof, and dwell in righteousness with men on earth a thousand years, and the wicked shall not stand.

For a trump shall sound, and all the earth shall quake; and the dead shall come forth—yea, even the dead which died in me.

And every corruptible thing, both of man and of beasts, of the fowls and of the fish, shall be consumed. Also that of element shall melt with fervent heat; and all things shall become new, that the knowledge of me and my glory shall be on the earth.

In that day the enmity of man and the enmity of beasts, yea, the enmity of all flesh, shall cease from before my face. And in that day whatsoever any man shall ask, it shall be given unto him. Satan shall not have power to tempt any man; there shall be no sorrow because there is no death; an infant shall not die until he is old, his life shall be as a tree, and when he dies he shall not sleep, but be changed in the twinkling of an eye.

In that day the Lord shall reveal all things—things that have passed, things that no man knew; things of the earth by which it was made, and the purpose and the end thereof; things most precious; things that are above, and things that are beneath, things that are in the earth, upon the earth, and in heaven.

The third world-event foretold in the Prophet's revelations is the "end of the world." Of this he says, also speaking in the name of God:

And again, I say unto you, when the thousand years are ended, and men begin to deny their God, then I will spare the earth but for a little season. Then shall the end come.

The heaven and the earth shall be consumed and pass away, and there shall be a new heaven and a new earth. For old things shall pass away, and all things shall become new, and all the fulness thereof—men and beasts, the fowls of the air and the fishes of the sea. And not one hair, neither mote, shall be lost; for it is the workmanship of mine hand.

But, behold, before the earth shall pass away, Michael, mine arch-angel, shall sound his trump, and then shall the dead awake, for their graves shall be opened, and they shall come forth—yea, even all.

The righteous shall be gathered on my right hand unto eternal life; and the wicked on my left hand will I be ashamed to own before the Father. Wherefore I will say unto them: "Depart from me into everlasting fire, prepared for the devil and his angels."

These ideas have been given here in the Prophet's own words, partly to show how glowingly the Mormon leader could speak of the future of his people, but mainly to show the nature of the thing that could capture the imagination of his followers, not only of his own day, but of ours. Perhaps, however, there ought to be added a most illuminating phrase, which he puts into the mouth of Jehovah. It is to the effect that God spoke men in "their weakness, after the manner of their own language, that they might come to understanding."

Before any of these events occurred, though, the New Movement was to be proclaimed to "every nation, kindred, tongue, and people." For Joseph Smith not only pointed the eyes of the Mormons forward to events of world-importance in which they should take a prominent part, but he put into their hands a program to follow in the prosecution of the task he required of them. Their Golden Age was ahead of them, but in order to attain it they must exert themselves to the utmost.

One of the early Mormon writers, an obscure man, once compared the Holy Ghost with the spoonful of sugar which is used to sweeten a cup of tea. His point, not intended to be irreverent, was that religion and life were properly as inseparable as the tea and the sugar in a cupful of hot water. He got the idea, though not the figure, from Joseph Smith; for the Prophet had it in mind, as we have seen, to set up, not a mere sect, nor yet a religion, but a complete society on the basis of religion. That society in its social structure, in its government, in its education, in its industries even, should be saturated with the spirit of religion, as thoroughly as a cupful of tea is with sugar. It was a new idea then. At no point of Mormonism was there a more sudden and complete divergence from the ideals of the times.

This fundamental idea that religion and life are one, the followers of Joseph Smith were to promulgate throughout the world, till it should take root in every land and clime. And he left them the social machinery in an elastic organization with which to accomplish this task. Not since the first century of the Christian Era has there been a people so imbued with the missionary spirit as the followers of Joseph Smith. Every year they spend a million dollars in actual money to carry his message into every home.

As it happens (the Mormons would not use the word "happens" in this connection), modern facilities of communication have come to the rescue of the Saints. The steamship, the railway, the paved road, the automobile, the airplane, the newspaper, and especially the radio—these make possible the carrying out of the Prophet's commission to his people in a way he never dlearned of. This, in the Mormon view, is what God meant when he said that he would "cut his work short in righteousness." It was all in "the plan."

Thus the Mormons since Joseph Smith's day have taken high inspiration from the belief that, through promulgating his ideas, they are helping to shape the destiny of nations, that they are preparing the way for a coming world-event of the greatest magnitude. And this inspiration, it is safe to say, will broaden and deepen as time passes and as Mormonism grows.
