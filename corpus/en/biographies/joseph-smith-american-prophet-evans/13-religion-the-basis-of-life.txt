John Henry Evans, Joseph Smith, an American Prophet ( 1946)

CHAPTER XI

Religion the Basis of Life

Great changes are not caused by ideas alone; but they are not effected without ideas. The passions of men must be aroused if the frost of custom is to be broken or the claims of authority burst; but passion of itself is blind, and its world chaotic. —L. T. HOBHOUSE.

To Joseph Smith religion meant power, spiritual energy. Perhaps there has been no one bit of scripture more often quoted among his followers than Paul's statement about the gospel being "the power of God unto salvation." Only, the stress is always, not on the word "salvation," but on the word "power." The emphasis came from the founder of the faith.

The Prophet was not unacquainted with what religion had done among the Chosen People. In the home his imagination had been kindled by the story of Moses, of Joseph, his namesake, of Elijah, of Isaiah, of Jeremiah, of John the Baptist, of Peter, and of Paul. Religion had made all these men think high thoughts and do great deeds. And it is not at all improbable that stories had floated into Palmyra of the early Christian martyrs, of Simeon the Stylite and his thirty-foot pillar, and of the strenuous Luther. Whatever had come into the youth's mind in those days in connection with religion, had been associated with a power that energized.

Moreover, the stirrings of religion in his own soul had been the stirrings of power. For one thing, it had changed him so that he saw life with different eyes. That probably was the whole purpose of religion, after all—to change your outlook on life, to make you see and think and act differently. And then, it had helped him with the Book. If it had not been for religion that volume would never have made its appearance in print. Religion had motivated the story of the Jaredites and of the Lehites. The founders of both those nations had left their homes and gone across the seas because of the impelling force of the spiritual, and had established themselves in the Wilderness of what is now the New World. And their peoples had won their victories, through the same power.

God was, then, a God of power. He was not an idea merely, but an actual Being, a Person, who loved mankind because they were his children. His love surpassed anything known among men. It was like a powerful magnet. Matthew had left his counting desk, and Peter and John their nets to follow Christ. All the apostles (except the Beloved Disciple) had lost their lives one way or another because they had a taste of this Power, this Love of Jehovah. And Paul had traversed the known globe and been all things to all men because he had seen the Light and heard the Voice.

It should be the same in the New Dispensation. Men and women, drinking deep of this Fountain, should come from the ends of the earth, leaving behind them father and mother, sister and brother, husband and wife and sweetheart, for the new gospel's sake. Missionaries should penetrate into every nook and corner of the world in search of "the honest in heart." It should be in verity "a marvelous work and a wonder," wherein "the wisdom of the wise should perish and the understanding of the prudent be hid."

No religion could exist without God. Not that one ought to have God always on the lips, but one ought always to have him in the heart. Besides, man must not only have faith in God; he must have faith in himself, that he is a child of God, and therefore immortal. Nor was that all. There was such a thing as sin and righteousness and heaven and prayer. God, immortality, salvation, prayer—these were spiritual realities.

That was the dream and the prophecy of a fourteen-year-old boy in Palmyra.

What would have been his astonishment had he been able to foresee in detail the actual working of that Power he dlearned about in the lives of his disciples?

There was the case of the Reverend Orson Spencer, for instance. Orson Spencer, as we have seen, was a man with two degrees, a Baptist clergyman of wide influence throughout New England. In 1842, in answer to a request by the Rev. William Crowell, editor of the Christian Watchman in Boston, as to why he had changed his faith, Spencer wrote a series of letters, which were afterwards published in book form. In one of these letters the new convert to Mormonism describes his mental and moral struggle in the situation. Here is part of the story:

You give me credit for a conscientious regard for the will of God. It was this that gave me the victory where many others, I fear, are vanquished. The Spirit of God wrought mightily in me, commending the ancient gospel to my conscience.

I contemplated it with peaceful serenity and joy in believing. But when I allowed my selfish propensities to speak, I cursed Mormonism, in my heart, and regretted being in possession of as much light and knowledge as had flowed into my mind from that source.

When I preached or conversed according to my best convictions, peace reigned in my heart, and truth enlarged my understanding. Conviction and reverence for the truth, at such times, seemed to reign in the hearts of those that heard me; at times, however, some were ready to gnash their teeth, for the truth they would not receive and could not resist.

I counted the cost, to myself and family, of embracing such views, until I could read it like the child his alphabet, either upward or downward. The expense I viewed through unavoidable tears, both in public and private, by night and by day. I said, however, the Lord he is God, I can, I will embrace the truth.

When I considered the weakness of the human mind, and its liability to be deceived, I re-examined and held converse with the most able opposers to Mormonism, in a meek and teachable spirit; but the ease with which many, wearing a high profession of piety, turned aside the force of palpable truth, or leaned on tradition or inextricable difficulties, that they could not solve into harmony with their professions, was very far from dissuading me from my new views.

What could I do? Truth had taken possession of my mind—plain, simple, Bible truth.

It might be asked if I could not expel it from my door. Yes, I could do it; but how would that harmonize with a sincere profession to preach and practice the truth, by way of example to others?

It was a crisis I never shall, I never can forget. I remember it as an exodus from parents, kindred, denomination, and temporal support. Has any one ever passed such a crisis, they will say, be careful of Brother Spencer's character and feelings.

Little as I supposed I cared about popularity, competence, or the fellowship of those who were sincerely in error, when I came to be stretched upon the altar of sacrifice, and the unsheathed blade that was to exscind from all these hung over me with perpendicular exactness, then, then, Brother, I cried unto the Lord to strengthen me to pass through the scene with his approbation.

Moreover, there was the integrating power of the New movement—the power that was greater than any melting pot to be found elsewhere in America.

Mormonism is made up of people from all the Christian churches, nearly, in the world—Catholics, Episcopalians, Methodists, Presbyterians, Baptists, Quakers, Universalists—and many who never belonged to any church. Yet in this organization they are not Catholics, Episcopalians, Methodists, Presbyterians, Baptists, Quakers, Universalists, any more. They are all Latter-day Saints. Any one who has ever tried to uproot an old tradition will know what it takes to do it. But the religion of Joseph Smith has done just that in hundreds of thousands of cases.

Also Mormonism has been accepted by men and women from almost every civilized nation on the globe—by the English, the Welsh, the Scotch, the Irish; the Danish, the Norwegian, the Swedish, the Icelandic; the French, the Dutch; the German, the Swiss; and many other lesser nationalities. Yet these people from thirty nations have all sunk their fatherlands in the country of their adoption, and they have done so more completely perhaps than in the United States generally—which is the crucible of the world. In no entirely Mormon city, even though it were as large as Chicago or New York, could there be found small colonies of foreigners, mixing only within themselves and speaking only their own native tongue. National lines, as well as religious, have been almost obliterated by the religion of Joseph Smith. It would be impossible anywhere else in America to come upon such a thorough fusion of heterogeneous elements as is to be seen in the Mormon communities of Utah and Southern Idaho, where the Saints are the most numerous.

Finally, there is that historical trek of the Mormons from Nauvoo to the West under Brigham Young. Only the exodus of the Israelites under Moses and the flight of a Tartar tribe, made famous by De Quincey, are comparable to it, and in some respects the movement of the Mormons was unique.

The Israelites were incited to their journey by the hope of a promised land, which flowed with milk and honey; the Tartars by fear of what might happen to them if they did not flee. But the Mormons were going to what they all knew was set down on the maps as the "Great American Desert." It is an actual fact that, if they had chosen to renounce their faith, they might have been left free to enjoy the same security and prosperity as their neighbors in the communities they were leaving. While there is no telling what lies at the bottom of mass movements, especially if the individuals are ignorant, as the Israelites were, for example, this mass movement of the Mormons is complicated by the fact that the people on the whole were intelligent and used to the amenities of cultivated life, and by the further fact that the only hope held out to them, if they escaped death by starvation or by the tomahawk, was that they would be free in the new home to work out their destiny in their own way.

There must, therefore, have been no little power in a faith that could induce intelligent men and women to do the things that the Mormons did for their faith, individually and collectively. How it would have heartened Joseph Smith to have seen this later manifestation of invisible power!

In his "Intimations of Immortality" the poet Wordsworth gives the first Christian hint, outside the New Testament, of a possible pre-earth life of the human spirit. Here is the passage:

Our birth is but a sleep and a forgetting: The soul that rises with us, our life's star, Hath had elsewhere its setting, And cometh from afar: Not in entire forgetfulness, And not in utter nakedness, But trailing clouds of glory do we come From God, who is our home.

The poem was written between 1803 and 1806. When it first appeared in print, "some good and pious persons," as the poet himself tells us, were "pained" that he should have inculcated "a belief in a prior state of existence." He took alarm, and hastened to set himself right. "It is far too shadowy a notion," he apologized, "to be recommended to faith, as more than an element in our instincts of immortality. But let us bear in mind that, though the idea is not advanced in revelation, there is nothing there to contradict it, and the fall of man presents an analogy in its favor."

Wordsworth must have been familiar with such biblical passages as this from John: "And now, O Father, glorify thou me with thine own self with the glory which I had with thee before the world was." But this refers only to a prior existence for Jesus, and does not even suggest one for mankind in general. If there is any hint in the Bible of a pre-earth life for any one else, the poet considered it too vague to use as a basis for his poem.

He does, however mention the Platonic philosophy. In Plato's PhÃ¦do Socrates is represented as saying to Simmias, "Our souls existed formerly, apart from our bodies, and possessed intelligence before they came into man's shape."

With the single exception of Joseph Smith no one for more than a hundred years after Wordsworth ventured to suggest a pre-earth life for all men. Maeterlinck, however, in 1913, bravely advocated the idea in his book, Our Eternity. He says:

"If you maintain that you will always exist, you are bound to admit that you have always existed; for we cannot imagine the one without having to imagine the other. If nothing ends, nothing begins, for any such beginning is the end of something."

The Prophet, though, was less hesitating than the poet and more certain than either the Greek or the Belgian philosopher. With true Miltonic imagination, Joseph Smith constructs for us a pre-earth spiritual world, and peoples it with spirit-children—ourselves, before we were born in the flesh. But he does this, not as a poet nor as a philosopher, but as a seer.

More than one theologian has been puzzled by the two accounts of the creation in Genesis. They were no riddle at all, it seems, to Joseph Smith. The first chapter, he says, tells about the spiritual creation, and the second chapter about the physical creation. This interesting fact he indicated by sandwiching the following passage between the third and the fourth verse of the second chapter:

I, the Lord God, made the heaven and the earth, and every plant of the field before it was in the earth, and every herb of the field before it grew.

For I, the Lord God, created all things of which I have spoken, spiritually, before they were naturally upon the face of the earth. For I, the Lord God, had not caused it to rain upon the face of the earth. And I, the Lord God, had created all the children of men; and not yet a man to till the ground; for in heaven created I them; and there was not yet flesh upon the earth, neither in the water, neither in the air.

In that spiritual realm — wherever it was — we lived. Not alone those who inhabited this planet before Joseph Smith's time, but those who were alive then and those who were to live here thereafter. And this regardless of the part of the earth in which their lot happened to be cast, the color of their skin, or the degree of their intelligence and moral sense.

But we were all spirits there. "Spirit" the Prophet defined as "matter," only "more fine and pure" than that with which we are now familiar; and "it can be discerned only by purer eyes" than those of the flesh. And he added dogmatically: "We cannot see it; but when our bodies are purified, we shall see that it is all matter. There is no such thing as immaterial matter." This last was a thrust at the theologians of his day,

As to the form in which we existed on that spirit-earth, we bore what is now known as the human shape. That is, we had hands and feet, legs and arms, a head and a trunk, and other bodily organs, just as we have in our mortal life—only spiritual, not the grosser material. And we were intelligent there—at least as intelligent as we seem to be here. We were capable of knowing, feeling, and willing, within about the same limits as in the flesh. Not that we were equally intelligent. In intelligence we differed infinitely, from the lowest moron up to God. Wherever two spirits exist, one is bound to be more intelligent than the other. That was the case in "heaven."

How did we come to be thus? Joseph Smith had an answer. It is the one given, by implication, in the phrase "Our Father which art in heaven." Only the Prophet was not held back by the timidity of the modern Christian generally, when it came to talking about what is clearly implied in the doctrine.

God is our Father by spiritual generation. He "begot" our spirit. The idea involves a divine Mother, as well as a divine Father—and Joseph Smith taught that. Thus Jesus, who is God in the theology of the Mormon leader, becomes literally, and not figuratively, our elder Brother. He was spiritually the "first-born" of the Father, and the "only Begotten" in the flesh. Not necessarily that Christ and man were begotten by the same identical Being in heaven, any more than Adam and Eve, who are our common parents, begot our fleshly bodies. Nor were the spirits all begotten at the same time. But all men are brothers in the spirit, and God is the universal Father.

But Joseph Smith, with startling boldness, goes even farther back than that. He would penetrate to the spiritual cosmos. Man is co-eval with God—that is, in his ultimate being, his ego. Not withstanding all the spirits in that primeval state differed in intelligence, yet they were all equally eternal. "They existed before." Thus we come down to something fundamental.

The body of flesh, which was begotten on this earth of earthly parents is the "tabernacle" of what we now call the "spirit"; the "spirit," in turn which was also begotten, but in "heaven" and of divine parentage, is but the "tabernacle" of something else in us—which we might call the "ego."

The word "eternal," therefore, so far as it relates to man's essential being, means, not only "without end," but also "without beginning." In Nauvoo once the Prophet said, with more concreteness than Maeterlinck: "I take this ring off my finger. It has neither beginning nor end; it is eternal. But, if I cut it at any point, I have made both a beginning and an end; it is eternal no more. There is no consistency in saying the soul has a beginning, but no end."

Whether these ultimate entities always existed as individual "intelligences," or whether they existed in the mass of original stuff and were at some time or other flung off from it, the Prophet left to his disciples to discuss. While he did not say so, there is a clear inference from what he did say that all life has the same base.

When were these spirit-beings begotten, and how long did they stay in that pre-earth world? Joseph Smith did not say. Probably the astronomer's light-years or the geologist's period, even, would have to be stretched to cover that spiritual Ã¦on.

At all events, they remained there developing till they reached the limits afforded by an unembodied state. For the Prophet taught that only when "spirit and element" are "inseparably connected," can there be "a fulness of joy."

Accordingly a "plan" was formed in "heaven." A physical earth was to be made, in which these spiritual beings were to dwell, with "their free agency," to work out their "salvation." The Prophet's supplement to Genesis puts it this way (Jehovah is speaking):

We will go down, for there is space there, and we will take of these materials, and will make an earth whereon these may dwell. And we will prove them herewith, to see if they will do all things whatsoever the Lord their God shall command them.

They who keep their first estate shall be added upon; and they who keep not their first estate shall not have glory in the same kingdom with those who keep their first estate. And they who keep their second estate shall have glory added upon their heads for ever and ever.

It was known beforehand that the first man and woman so placed would "transgress" and, through their transgression, bring death (a separation of the spirit and the body) into the world. This would call for "an infinite atonement." A redeemer must therefore be provided to "save" man—that is, in part, to bring about a reunion of the spirit and the body. The Prophet goes on:

The Lord said: "Whom shall I send?"

One answered like unto the Son of Man: "Here am I, send me. I will be thy son. Father, thy will be done, and thine be the glory forever."

And another said: "Behold, here am I, send me. I will be thy son, and I will redeem all mankind, that not one soul shall be lost; and surely I will do it. But give me thine honor."

And the Lord said: "I will send the first."

The second was angry, and kept not his first estate; and at that day many followed him. He became Satan, the Devil, the Father of lies. He was cast down because he sought to destroy the agency of man, which I, the Lord, had given him.

Then there was a "rebellion" in heaven. That is, some of the spirits refused to abide by the plan agreed upon. It is as if mortals were to fly in the face of a well-established law of physics or education. The "punishment," which was that they should remain outside the plan, was not therefore arbitrary at all. It was that, having refused to take up an earthly life with personal autonomy, their further progress was inhibited. Perhaps most of them did not see that at the time. But that would make no difference as to the fact.

A new earth having been "created," the spirits, thus "begotten" began gradually, a few millions at a time, to inhabit the planet, arriving here through the channel of natural birth.

In Joseph Smith's day it was taught that the earth was made out of nothing. No doubt the idea originated in a desire to enhance the "glory of God"—as if that might not be trusted to take care of itself. The Mormon prophet had other notions of "creation." He asserted dogmatically that the earth was made out of preÃ«xisting materials.

As for the glory of God, that consisted in something vastly more worthwhile than in bringing matter into existence. Indeed, even God could not do that. The divine glory consisted in exactly the same thing that the real glory of man does. It consisted in service. Here is what the Prophet puts in the mouth of God: "This is my work and my glory—to bring to pass the immortality and eternal life of man." That was said more than a hundred years ago.

The Prophet's views on the "creation" of man were equally unorthodox. Parley P. Pratt, who got his inspiration from his "file leader," ridiculed the notion that man was "moulded" out of earth like an adobe and that woman was "manufactured" from a rib. To him the whole story was of a piece with that of the genesis of life here which we tell our children. And a disciple of a later period, when asked how Adam and Eve came into being physically, replied, "In exactly the same way everybody else did—they were born."

Personal autonomy was a fact. There is nothing deeper in the religious philosophy of Joseph Smith. No progress or responsibility was possible without "free agency." It was the great point in dispute in that pre-earth conference of the spirits. Progress led through the operation of autonomy; stagnation, even retrogression, without it—in all stages of human life. That is why Joseph Smith was always averse to force, compulsion.

Evil was "necessary" in the world. In the Book of Mormon one of the characters says this, which is the most abstract statement in the Prophet's writings: "It must needs be that there is an opposition in all things. If not so, righteousness could not be brought to pass, neither wickedness, neither good nor bad. Wherefore, all things must needs be a compound in one, and remain dead, having no life nor death, nor corruption nor incorruption, neither sense nor insensibility."

Into the midst of this primal conflict, one force making for righteousness and joy and the other for unrighteousness and misery, these embodied spirits were flung, with the advice to learn to distinguish between the two powers and to choose which of them they would serve, always bearing in mind, however, that they must abide the consequences, both here and hereafter.

Eternal joy was the end to be sought—joy as distinguished from pleasure or even happiness. Says Joseph Smith: "Adam fell that men might be; and men are, that they may have joy."

Man on the earth was not to be bothered overmuch by the Lord. His way was not to be hedged about, so that he could move only in one direction. That was Lucifer's way, not God's. Rather he was to be left free to choose. God would not do for him what he could do for himself. No doubt this groping would result in pain and suffering. But that would be good for him—in the end. Even Jesus, God though he was, "learned obedience by the things which he suffered." Why shouldn't man?

It will be remembered that in that pre-earth world of spirits there were some "noble and great ones," who were to be made, "rulers" in the earth-life. These, according to the Prophet, would be the teachers of mankind, and their coming to earth would be timed, so as to cover particular generations and peoples. This is the way in which the idea is put in the Book of Mormon: "The Lord doth grant unto all peoples, men of their own nation and tongue, to teach his word in wisdom, all that he seeth fit they should have."

In this way Joseph Smith accounted for such men as Abraham, Moses, Isaiah, Paul, Gautama, Confucius, Plato, Galileo, Alfred the Great, Washington, Lincoln, Edison. And this explanation he made when he was twenty-four.

In death the spirit and the body are separated. The one enters a "spirit world," which is an intermediate stage between death and the resurrection; the other, the body, goes back to the earth. Says Alma in the Book of Mormon:

It has been made known unto me by an angel that the spirits of all men, as soon as they are departed from this mortal body, whether they be good or evil, are taken home to that God who gave them life.

The spirits of those who are righteous are received into a state of happiness, which is called Paradise—a state of rest, a state of peace, where they shall rest from all their troubles and from all care and sorrow.

The spirits of the wicked, those who have done evil, shall be cast into outer darkness. There shall be weeping, and wailing, and gnashing of teeth; and this because of their own iniquity. The wicked remain in this state, as well as the righteous in Paradise, until the time of their resurrection.

This intermediate world of disembodied spirits is not without its opportunities. For here, as in the earth-state, the spirits retain their power to think, to feel, and to will, with their freedom of choice. But the "plan of life," based on fundamental laws of truth as formulated before the "creation" of the earth, is essential to the progress of the soul everywhere. Hence, if for some reason the "wicked" spirits are unhappy because of neglected opportunities in the mortal state, they have another chance to accept whatever truth they are able in the disembodied condition to receive and to apply. In another chapter we shall see the implications of this thought.

In the course of events these disembodied spirits "come forth" in the resurrection. The resurrection, in Mormon theology, is not a mystical rising from the dead. Rather it is literal. The spirit actually takes up its physical body again. Not every particle of matter that went into the making of the body, but the essential elements. It is stated thus in the Book of Mormon: "The soul shall be restored to the body, and the body to the soul. Every limb and joint shall be restored to its body. Even a hair of the head shall not be lost. But all things shall be restored to their proper and perfect frame." Joseph Smith took the resurrection of Jesus as an example of what human beings generally may expect in this matter.

The resurrection is universal. But not all are raised at the same time. In general there are three periods of resurrection. The first began at the time when Jesus rose from the dead, with some persons whom Matthew calls "saints" and who "appeared" to those in the city of Jerusalem, and extends down to the beginning of the millennium. During that period of a thousand years others will be raised from the grave—which is the second resurrection. And the third period of resurrection will occur after the millennium, when the rest of mankind will arise.

Thereupon, after the "judgment," men will live on for ever in their reincarnated state—each in the sphere that his previous life entitles him to.

Such, then, according to the modern prophet, is the natural history of the soul. Man exists, first, as an individual ego, in which he is as eternal as God; secondly, as embodied in a tabernacle of spirit, which was "begotten" of a heavenly Father and a heavenly Mother; thirdly, as embodied in a fleshly body, born of an earthly father and mother; fourthly, as a discarnate spirit, awaiting the resurrection; and, finally, as a reincarnate being, clothed with an immortal body of flesh and bone, and with spirit taking the place of blood.

Meantime, in every stage of this unending, beginningless life, there is conscious intelligence, with the power to think, to feel, and to choose. And each separate stage is an advance over the stage just preceding—an evolution that dwarfs the evolution of Darwin, of whom Joseph Smith never even heard.

When Joseph Smith was a boy in Palmyra, he was taught some things about salvation that he came to disbelieve; and he substituted therefore a theory which, although set forth many years before the modern scientific method was applied to religion, yet strikes hands in its fundamentals with our present way of looking at things.

In those days salvation was supposed to be a sudden acquisition. People used to say to themselves and to one another, "Yesterday I was among the damned, but to-day I am saved." And they meant it. Moreover, confession of Christ and baptism wiped out the past. Hence one who had lived a life of sin, even a murderer, might at the last moment repent and be admitted not only into the company of those who had steadily and consistently lived a life of good deeds as we regard them, but into the company also of God and the holy angels.

Besides, when people got to heaven, they found that all their mental and moral differences were obliterated, so that the Edwardses and the Jewkeses (provided the Jewkeses had had the sense to "believe") mingled freely together, and, although in their mentalities they were poles apart, yet they did pretty much the same thing—sang psalms and hymns in praise of the great Egoist, who sat demurely on the White Throne. Also those who were in heaven found themselves, for all practical purposes, quite sexless. Whoever wishes to verify these details has need only to dip into the sermons and other theological treatises of the period.

Evidently the Mormon prophet gave the matter considerable thought. He put two and two together, theologically speaking, and got the astounding product four—an intellectual feat which the most astute theologian of the old school had apparently been unable to perform. For he said to himself, in effect: "If God rewarded every man according to his works, did it not follow, since mankind varied so much in the character of their works, that in heaven there were many 'kingdoms' or 'degrees of glory'?"

The question itself, coming into the mind of an untrained backwoods youth in those dull theological times, was proof of no small mentality. But the manner in which this same youth elaborated upon his answer to this inquiry shows a rare imaginative power. His query now took on a double-headed question: First, was heaven an achievement or a gift? And, second, where and what sort of place is heaven? Not, of course, that he reasoned himself into a solution of his problem. That was not his way. He announced his answers in fragments, as usual—which we are putting together here and amplifying.

The Mormon leader's theory of salvation was in striking contrast with the two theories of his times.

One of these was the Catholic. The Roman Catholics taught that a man is saved through "works," and by works they meant, not what we now understand by the term, but whatever the church requires one to do. While the moral virtues were necessary, there was nothing peculiarly saving in them. Otherwise one could be saved without the church, without Christ even—which was unthinkable. But the forms and ceremonials of the church were spiritual and saving; they were especially designed to remit sin and put one into contact with God.

There were seven of these requirements—baptism, confirmation, the eucharist, penance, extreme unction, orders, and matrimony. These, it will be noted, were all something to do, as contradistinguished from something to think and feel—something which derived its significance, not from the nature of the thing done, but from the fact that the church commands it. The Catholic was not supposed to read, to study things in religion out for himself. That was the business of the church. What he was expected to do was to obey the injunctions of the vicar of Christ. And the vicar of Christ pointed to the seven "sacraments." If he faithfully adhered to them, he would be saved.

The second theory of salvation current in the Prophet's day was the Protestant. According to Protestantism, one was saved, not through works in the Catholic sense, but through the grace of God. There was no salvation to be got out of forms, as such, even though the church required them. It was possible to go through religious ceremonials in a perfunctory manner—which resulted in no good to any one. Just as in the Catholic belief the moral virtues were of no avail to save, so in the Protestant belief neither the moralities nor the ritual would bring salvation of themselves. There was something else needed.

That something else was grace. The law required that a man serve the Lord with all his heart, with all his soul, and with all his mind. But whoever had done that satisfactorily? So there was something to overlook. Moreover, man was enjoined to be "perfect," even as God was perfect. But who was perfect? Here again man fell down. It was only the grace of God that could lift him up. "By grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves; it is the gift of God: not of works, lest any man should boast. For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus unto good works, which God hath before ordained that we should walk in them."

These theories of salvation had become the warp and woof of men's thinking religiously, both in the Catholic church and in the Protestant churches. It was difficult for the men of that generation to think anything different. What shall we say, then, of a young man just past his twenty-sixth birthday, but without education or theological training in the accepted sense, who rose above the traditional atmosphere with a theory of salvation, not only new to his times, but in harmony as to its essentials with a method of thought two generations in the then future?

For Joseph Smith advanced an entirely different conception of salvation. According to the Prophet we are saved, not through religious forms, although something in the way of forms is necessary, nor yet through grace, although grace is indispensable, but rather through knowledge. It is understood of course that the knowledge acquired must be applied intelligently to the problems of life. And, since knowledge increases but gradually, we are therefore saved gradually, instead of all at once, notwithstanding there may have been a point, a precious moment, when we were put in the way of being saved.

Furthermore, salvation is not a matter of being rescued from "eternal punishment" in the next world, as was universally taught and believed in the first half of the nineteenth century, but rather a putting one's self "beyond the power of evil," both in the world to come and in this world. We have already quoted in another connection that illuminating sentence from the late president of the Mormon church, Joseph F. Smith, of the insecurity of a church that talks only of the life hereafter and can do nothing for us here. The Prophet himself, in 1843, put the matter thus:

The principle of knowledge is the principle of salvation. This principle can be comprehended by the faithful and diligent; and every one that does not obtain knowledge sufficient to be saved, will be condemned. The principle of salvation is given us through the knowledge of Jesus Christ.

Salvation is nothing more nor less than to triumph over all our enemies and put them under our feet. And when we have power to put all enemies under our feet in this world, and a knowledge to triumph over all evil spirits in the world to come, then we are saved, as in the case of Jesus, who was to reign until he had put all enemies under his feet, and the last enemy was death.

Knowledge through our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ is the grand key that unlocks the glories and mysteries of the kingdom of heaven.

Obviously the Prophet in this passage does not refer to personal enemies—of whom, as we have seen, he had an abundance. The word, to him as to us, would take in a wider scope than that. Whatever hinders the individual development, here or hereafter, is an enemy. There can be no doubt that he would concur with our modern notion, as there is ample evidence to show, that our principal enemies to-day are, and perhaps always have been, poverty, ignorance, and war; and that the way to get rid of these enemies is to apply knowledge to the solution of the problem. It is only faith to say, however, that Joseph Smith had in mind spiritual enemies principally—enemies that prevented us from growth in religious knowledge; although, as we have shown in a preceding chapter, he advocated measures looking to the overthrow of both poverty and ignorance, and we know he was opposed to war and anticipated a time when there would be universal peace.

Such, then, in general, was the Mormon prophet's conception of salvation. It was a process, not an end; an achievement, not a sudden acquisition. In the long evolution of the soul there had been no jumps, but only slow development, a going from a lower stage to a higher, a battle of the Ã¦ons, a continual triumphing over "enemies." This was an immense leap upward out of the traditional, stagnating theological atmosphere of his time.

But this conception of growth in the process of salvation applied not only to the short span of mortal life, but also, and especially, to the infinitude of the future.

In the next world there were in general two classes of people, the "obedient" and the "disobedient," and these were distributed over three "kingdoms"—the "telestial," the "terrestrial," and the "celestial."

Joseph Smith never looked upon God as an arbitrary Being—in the way, for instance, that a pupil often regards his teacher. On the contrary, he viewed him as a kindly Guide, an interested Teacher, a loving Father. It is doubtful whether any man was on more intimate relations with God than the Mormon prophet. The word "friend" in connection with deity frequently dropped from his pen, and he often spoke of God as always a refuge in times of trouble and perplexity.

More than that. Joseph Smith looked on the "commandments" of God (a word he was fond of using) as the expression of deeply fundamental laws, not arbitrary pronouncements. God himself had become God through obedience to law. It was a daring thought. And God was "progressing." The Prophet's universe therefore, like William James's and Einstein's, it would seem, was a "limited," a "finite" universe. At all events, when God issued a commandment, it was founded in something inherently necessary—something He had found to "work."

Besides, any commandment of God's was "spiritual," not "temporal." That is, it was given with a view to the growth of that spiritual entity in man that was co-eval with God, without regard particularly to the body. "All things unto me are spiritual," the Prophet represents Jehovah as saying, "and not at any time have I given unto you a law which was temporal; neither to any man, nor to the children of men. My commandments are all spiritual."

And so, whenever the Mormon leader spoke of people as obedient or disobedient, he meant that they were or were not con forming to the fundamental laws of life in the sense in which he conceived of immortality. Hence those persons in heaven who might be in the class of "disobedient" were simply those who had not lived on earth in accordance with the principles that God had made known—the principles of spiritual growth. And those who were in the "obedient" group had made a successful attempt to live according to those principles. There was nothing arbitrary about it. The nature of man being such as it is, he can be "saved" only through universal law. Joseph Smith thus anticipated Henry Drummond by a full generation.

The Prophet, however, used the word "saved" in two separate meanings.

First, there was a salvation in the general sense. "In Adam all die." Something that Adam did, whatever that was, brought "death" into the world. Now death, as Joseph Smith conceived it, was nothing more nor less than a separation of the spirit and the body. The spirit went to a spirit world; the body in its particles returned to earth. It all came about through Adam's "transgression." Other human beings had had nothing whatever to do with it, although it was in the "plan." Hence justice demanded that they be redeemed from the consequences of a sin in which they had no part. For Joseph Smith did not share the theological notions of his contemporaries about "original sin." Therefore the atonement of Christ, without conditions, brought forth all men from the grave, restored their spirits and their bodies. Thus all men became immortal beings; immortality was theirs without even the asking.

Secondly, there was a salvation which, though free to all, was limited to those who were willing to conform to the conditions laid down. These would not only be raised from the dead into immortality, but also "exalted" in the next world. But they must believe, repent, be baptized, receive the Holy Ghost, and afterwards struggle for general righteousness in their lives. The Prophet often used the phrase "immortality and eternal life," as if to imply that the one was merely a going on, but the other a being, in heaven.

Thus there were necessary distinctions in the hereafter as numerous and as great as on earth, and the Edwardses and the Jewkeses need not be together—unless they wanted to.

For the human personality was essentially the same there as here. Intelligence, with its primary attributes, was inherent in the spirit, not in the flesh. The flesh was but a house, and, though it cramped and restricted the spirit in many ways, yet it was but a medium through which the spirit made contacts in the physical world. Just as the human spirit had been able to think and feel and choose in the pre-earth life, and just as it is able to do those things in the earth-life, so it will continue that ability in the post-earth life, with all that spirit-qualities mean in the way of incentives, of overcoming, of enjoyment.

Most Christians believed, where they had any ideas on the subject at all, that the incidents of death and the resurrection brought on sudden changes in the tendencies of the soul, that one automatically sluffed one's undesirable qualities. Such was not the case. "That which breaketh a law and abideth not by law, but seeketh to become a law unto itself, and willeth to abide in sin, and altogether abideth in sin," said the Prophet once, "cannot be sanctified by law, neither by mercy, justice, nor judgment. Therefore it must remain filthy still." In other words, the mere passing from one stage of life to another, as from mortality to immortality, does not change one from a bad man to a good man. That comes only through individual effort and the grace of God.

Nor did men and women lose their sex differences and functions on passing from earth to heaven. Why should they? Sex, too, was something of the spirit as well as of the body of flesh. Sex was a sacred thing, not something to be ashamed of. Shame came only from its perversion and abuse. In itself it was holy. It was the nearest thing to the creative power of God that man had. And love, the holiest of all human emotions, was inextricably involved in sex. Out of love arose the greatest joy of life—the joy of intimate human companionship. Heaven would be no heaven to a husband without his wife, or to the wife without the husband, or to parents without their children. Moreover, there would be procreation in the hereafter, as there had been here; only, the offspring would be spirit children, as in the case of God and those who came to the earth—life.

As already stated, there were three "kingdoms" in the next world—the telestial, the terrestrial, and the celestial. Those who inhabit the telestial kingdom will find themselves in hell. For here are "the liars, the sorcerers, the adulterers, and the whore-mongers," whether in the church or out of it. In the terrestrial kingdom are those who "died without law" and those also who "were blinded by the craftiness of men." The celestial kingdom was made up of people who "received the testimony of Jesus" and who "overcame by faith." It is the human pyramid, with the largest number at the base. But in each kingdom there were varying degrees of intelligence and values, with the possibility of progress—"eternal progress," as the Mormons phrase it. Thus will it be in the celestial kingdom—the highest glory.

In a word, then, the Hereafter of Joseph Smith is but a projection, an extension, of the Here and the Now, with this difference: People will live on for ever.

But where and what are Heaven and Hell, according to the Mormon prophet? Joseph Smith believed that the heaven of the Hereafter will be on this earth. He himself, true to form, did not go into details in the matter. He was content with the general statement. Here is his most extended pronouncement on the subject:

They that are wise and have received the truth, and have taken the Holy Spirit for their guide, and have not been deceived, they shall not be hewn down and cast into the fire, but shall abide the day.

And the earth shall be given them for an inheritance; and they shall multiply and wax strong, and their children shall grow up without sin unto salvation. For the Lord shall be in their midst, and his glory shall be upon them, and he will be their king and their law-giver.

This "inheriting" of the earth, was not to take place till after the planet had been "changed" into a fit abode of immortal beings. The Prophet lifted his movement to an importance beyond anything in eighteen hundred years, by making it a prologue to the last great scenes in the human drama—the Second coming of Christ, the Millennium, the "end of the world," and the transfiguration of the earth prior to the time when the Saints of all ages would "inherit" the globe. It was an ambitious program.

Orson Pratt, as was his wont, gave some details. He even went so far as to figure out the probable number of people who would make the earth their permanent home and to parcel out the available land to heads of families. He says in part:

This is our heaven. We have a title to it by promise. It will be redeemed through the faith and prayers of the Saints, and we shall get a title from God to a portion of it as our inheritance.

Are the wicked to receive the earth as an inheritance? No; for Jesus did not say, Blessed are the wicked, for they shall inherit the earth. This promise was made only to the meek. Who are the meek? None but those who receive the ordinances of the gospel, and live according to them.

That is, the inhabitants of this earth after it has become sanctified and after they have been raised from the dead in immortality, will be those who are entitled to the glories of the celestial kingdom.

What is to become of those who are not thus entitled to the celestial kingdom? Neither the Prophet nor Orson Pratt left any record of their thoughts on the subject so far as the place of their abode is concerned. If they are not on this planet, enjoying the prospects that the others are, it is their own fault.

It was stated in a previous section that there are three "kingdoms" in the Hereafter, according to the teachings of the Mormon prophet—the "telestial," the "terrestrial," and the "celestial." It was also stated that the first two of these kingdoms have no admission requirements whatever, but that the third, which is the highest in glory, has certain definite pre-requisites.

These pre-requisites are to be found in what is known in Christian literature as the "gospel." The "gospel" is a set of principles corresponding to the "church." In fact, one might say that the gospel is to the church what the spirit is to the physical body in man. Hence, in order to gain admission to the "celestial kingdom," or the "kingdom of God," as Jesus expressed it (from the fact presumably that God favors it with his presence), one must, perforce, be a member of the church on earth. The purpose, therefore, of the church is to win recruits from those who would otherwise go to the lower kingdoms for the highest kingdom of glory, by inducing them to join the church and to live according to its higher laws. That is the function of the church of Christ. It is a new slant on salvation.

Joseph Smith was not, therefore, one of those who think we can get along without the church. The church, in this view, is necessary because it supplies a means by which men may live and work together; and a highly organized group-life, in turn, furnishes a means of developing the human personality. But a church is impossible without both officers and a body of rites. No organization, whether a church, a club, or a nation, can either come into being or exist without them.

It is a good thing to have many officers, or executives, in the church—the more, the better, up to a given point. This gives people something to do. Presently we shall see how this ideal was actually worked out by the Prophet into a church. It is not to be expected that these officers, great or small, shall be perfect. Perfect officers do not exist, any more than do perfect men. If the Lord waited till he could get perfect men to officer his church, there would be no church. But one qualification stood out as essential in every officer. He must have a passion for service, for human welfare. That was primary.

The Mormon leader was never a stickler for ceremonials as such. Mormonism, therefore, has few forms. Baptism, confirmation, the Lord's Supper, matrimony, and the laying on of hands in ordination to the priesthood, in healing the sick, and in blessing—these are the general ordinances in the Mormon church. Apparently the Prophet's maxim here was, the fewer, the better.

Baptism and confirmation let one into the church. As we have seen, this ordinance is by immersion. At the same time it remits sins. Immersion is considered better by the Saints than sprinkling or pouring, because it is more symbolical. As in physical birth, so in spiritual birth, one comes out of water into air. Then, too, it typifies the burial and the resurrection of Christ, on the one hand, and, on the other hand, sluffing off the "old man" and putting on the "new man." In baptism, therefore, one gets a fresh start on the spiritual highway. Confirmation, being a physical contact, is a tangible means of conveying the Holy Ghost through one who is supposed to be already in possession of that power.

The Lord's Supper is emblematical of brotherhood—like getting one's feet under the same table with others at a solemn feast. It signifies that a fellow feeling exists among those who eat the bread and drink the wine. As for the other ordinances, they are efficacious partly by reason of the physical contact.

The ordinances of religion, too, Joseph Smith puts on the plane of fundamental law. Forms are "signs." He specifically mentions baptism as a "sign." Forms in religion have value, not merely as such, but as symbols. Signs are no more necessary in religion than they are in life generally. They conserve life. Without them (language, gestures, spoken words) no two persons can come together in thought. It is equally so in religion, in the church.

Not all these details were expressed by the Prophet. Usually he did not deal in particulars. But they are implicit in his teachings on the subject of the church.

Professor Richard T. Ely, of the University of Wisconsin, has this to say of the church set up by Joseph Smith: "So far as I can judge from what I have seen, the organization of the Mormons is the most nearly perfect piece of social mechanism with which I have ever in any way come in contact, excepting alone the German army." That was said before the Great War. Professor Ely made a visit to Utah in 1903 for the purpose of investigating Mormonism on its economic side. His article—"Economic Aspects of Mormonism"—appeared in Harper's Magazine for April of that year.

At the head of the church are twenty-six men, arranged in groups of three presidents, twelve apostles, seven presiding seventies, three presiding bishops, and a presiding patriarch. These constitute the governing body of the Mormons "in all the world."

The "first presidency," as the three presidents are termed, have jurisdiction over the whole church. In reality, however, this body consists of a president and two counselors. The apostles, also general officers, operate under the immediate direction of the first presidency, and have charge of all missionary work everywhere. The senior member of this council always becomes president of the church. The "presidents of seventy" act under the apostles; their duties are restricted to preaching Mormonism "to the world." The presiding bishopric are the fiscal agents of the church. And the presiding patriarch, who is not always an old man, gives "patriarchal blessings," as Jacob did.

Territorially the church membership is divided into "stakes" and "missions." The stakes are "at home," and the missions "abroad." The missions are usually presided over by one man. He serves all the way from two years to as much as twenty or more years, and at present [1933] receives a salary. Each mission is subdivided into "conferences," and each conference into "branches." In the former case the presiding officer is a missionary from "home," in the latter case he is often a local elder. Thus there are the Southern States mission, the British mission, the African mission, and so on.

Similarly, in places where the Mormons are permanently located, as in Utah, Idaho, and the adjoining states, there are "stakes," with their sub-divisions into "wards." A stake is presided over by a president, with two counselors and a body of twelve men known as the "high council." The high council is a part of the church judiciary. Its cases usually are such as come up from the "bishop's court" by appeal, and there may be an appeal from the high council to the first presidency. At present there are more than a hundred stakes. Of "wards" there are perhaps a thousand. These correspond to the parish in other churches, as the stakes answer to the division known as the "diocese." The ward is presided over by a bishop and two counselors, who are directly responsible to the stake president. The ward is divided into "blocks," each of which has two ward "teachers," who are supposed to visit each family in their block once a month and to make reports to the bishop.

Thus the president of the Mormon church, with our modern facilities of communication, can, in twenty-four or forty-eight hours, get in touch with every man, woman, and child in the organization throughout the world. This "social mechanism" is equally applicable to one thousand and to one billion. It was devised by the Prophet at a time when the church had perhaps fewer than six thousand members. With an organization like that and an aggressive leader such as the founder of Mormonism was, the world could be turned upside-down in a short time—as Joseph Smith confidently expected it would be some day.

This organization has worked both ways—upward and downward. That is, ideas have sometimes gone from the head of the church to the members and also from the members to the head of the church. General policies are fixed upon and promulgated by the "general authorities." No one can get anywhere religiously in the Mormon church unless what he has to advocate has the "approval" of the leaders. But it has often happened that movements have sprung up in the stakes or even the wards, and these, upon receiving the stamp of the authorities, have become general. This double-headed leadership has a distinct advantage: it makes for socialization, initiative, and interest in the entire membership. Thus group-mindedness becomes creative.

The Mormon church is financed through its system of tithing. This idea was announced as a "law" by the Prophet, in 1838, after the failure of the United Order. It may be remembered that the "law of consecration," as the system of collectivism was named, required that all the "surplus" earnings of church members be turned into the community "storehouse"—everything, that is, above what was necessary to maintain a family. This "law of tithing" is considered by the Mormons as a "lesser law" than consecration. It requires that every gainfully employed person in the organization "shall pay one-tenth of all his interest annually" to the church. Thus the income of the Society runs up into the millions. These millions, however, trickle back into the stakes and wards and missions in the form of chapels, temples, charity funds for the poor, education, what not.

Only those who spend their entire time (perhaps fewer than fifty men in all) draw salaries. These would be the general authorities and the mission presidents. The presidents of stakes are allowed a pittance for traveling expenses, and the bishops a trifle monthly for clerical service. In the Mormon church there is an enormous amount of free service. What with stake and ward officers, Sunday school workers, and workers in the young people's organizations, tens of thousands of men and women, young and old, devote many hours gratis every week for the benefit of the church. If, as Professor James says, one ought to do something gratuitous each day in order to keep up the spirit of service in us, then, on that assumption, the Mormon organization should be ideal.

The work of Mormon propaganda is in keeping with its effective organization. We have mentioned this on more than one occasion, but it deserves special attention here. This, too, as indeed every fundamental idea in Mormonism, originated in Joseph Smith's time.

In the early days of the church it was mature men, as a rule, who preached the Mormon gospel. Nowadays, owing to the fact that the older men are needed to take care of their families, this missionary work is done by young men. For many years now the church has kept upwards of two thousand missionaries out in all parts of the world—the United States, Canada, South America, Europe, South Africa, and the Pacific Islands.

These men go at their own expense or the expense of relatives, and they keep themselves in the "field" for about two years—rarely less. While the cost of a mission varies with different countries, the average approaches fifty dollars a month, not counting the amount required in going and coming. In two years this would mean the expenditure by each missionary of twelve hundred dollars, or, by all of them together, of a million dollars, in round numbers, for a single year. This money comes out of the community in which the missionary has his home. In almost every Mormon family the father and all the sons have either been on missions or are looking forward to taking one. When the family is large, as is generally the case among the Saints, this cost of missionary service is great, especially since the church adopted the policy of sending women on missions. But the money involved is not considered lost, by any means. It is put down against education. In uplift to the individual missionary and to the community, the amount thus laid out, despite its huge economic drain, is always considered well spent.

In the case of the missionary, his knowledge of his own religion and of other religions is greatly increased; he contacts people with different modes of thought and habits of life from those under which he was reared; in short, he sees the world at a time in his life when he is very impressionable—the late adolescence. So he comes home laden with new ideas—religious, social, economic. As for the community, there grows, very gradually and imperceptibly, but none the less surely, a sort of world-feeling that is very wholesome. In every Mormon town are to be found men that have been on one or two missions. If the number be, say, twenty, then there are perhaps fifteen different parts of the world-represented. Their knowledge becomes public property, for they tell their experiences in the religious meetings of the ward. Thus, not only do various ideas become known generally in the community, but there grows up a feeling of kinship for the peoples whom these missionaries have visited and with whom they have made intimate contacts. Hence the Mormons, having come themselves from many different nations, learn to have a fellow feeling for all the world. Of course it is impossible to estimate these individual and social benefits in dollars and cents.

Three positive ideas operate in Mormonism with great effectiveness.

One of these is faith. Faith was looked upon by Joseph Smith, not as a mere intellectual assent to something, but as a source of spiritual power. It brought you into communion with God, for one thing, and, for another, it made you do something, not merely made you feel and think. The standard definition of faith among the Mormons is that it is "the assurance of things hoped for."

Once in Nauvoo the Prophet commented upon that which Peter says about "the more sure word of prophecy." The apostle exhorts his readers to "add to their faith, virtue, knowledge, temperance, and so on," the Prophet quotes, but he also urges them to "make their calling and election sure." The "more sure word," then, meant that one was "sealed up in the heavens" and that one was given a "promise of eternal life in the kingdom of God." And when any one received that, it would prove "an anchor to the soul, sure and steadfast." In which event the rolling of thunders, the flashing of lightnings, the bellowing of earthquakes, and the thickest war clouds, would not shake the soul "in hours of trial, trouble, and tribulation."

This cryptic saying, in which belief becomes faith and faith dissolves into knowledge, has taken deep root in the Mormon mind and heart. This faith, as it was passing over into knowledge, gave a sense of spiritual security, which produced power. The disciples of Joseph Smith, not only in his own time but since, were thus enabled to lay hold on immortality at this end. The phrase, "I know," whether it stood for a genuine personal experience or not, became a commonplace on the lips of the Latter-day Saint. If in reality he did not know, he at least thought he did—which often amounted to the same thing.

It was this "assurance" that supplied the spiritual energy in Mormonism. That, in fact, was back of the Reverend Orson Spencer's change of faith, as also of the change of faith in tens of thousands of others; it was back of the "gathering" movement, first drawing together, as by a magnet, people from all parts of the world, and then welding them into one and making them group-minded; and it lay at the root of the large expenditure of money for missionary work, for chapels, and for temples. Without that "assurance," whether in fact or in thought, Mormonism would disintegrate.

Another principle is repentance. The Mormons do not look upon repentance as a sign of weakness of character. Rather it is a sign of strength, of plain honesty with one's self, of progressive-mindedness. With Joseph Smith and his followers it has been what we know today in education as a "solution of problems."

As already stated, the Mormon prophet denied that "salvation" was an end in itself. With him it was rather a continuous process, an evolution of character. It consisted in finding yourself—picking out your objective, ascertaining where you were with respect to that objective, and then selecting the best means by which to get from where you were to where you wanted to be. Thus the problem in religion did not differ in essentials from problems in other fields of human life.

There was, on the one hand, the standard of life set up in the gospel, the church. Joseph Smith reaffirmed the Decalogue and the Sermon on the Mount, and added other "commandments" from his own source of wisdom. And then, on the other hand, there was frail human character. One's duty was to compare the two. That would give one the situation. After that it only remained to make the two meet at every point. If one fell down at any point, one was to get up where the fall occurred. Since repentance was so vital in character-growth, it must come daily to the rescue.

The third principle is prayer. Joseph Smith never imbibed any of those ridiculous notions current in his time about prayer; or, if he did so, he quickly got rid of them. Prayer was, first of all, a means by which the soul was put into a spiritual attitude toward life. It was also a means of getting in tune with the Infinite, so that the spiritual forces might work freely in one's redemption. Since sin, which was a deep fact, must be overcome, prayer would help in the process of overcoming.

Are prayers ever answered? Of course they are. "Pray always," the Prophet urged upon his disciples, "that you come off conquerors, and faint not; pray lest you fall into temptation; pray unto the Lord, in the name of Jesus Christ." The Mormons figure that, as long as a man prays, especially in secret, he is on the safe spiritual highway; the danger line is reached, when he leaves off praying.

But always answers to prayer come in accordance with law, not in violation of it. Joseph Smith insisted that law reigns in the spiritual world as inexorably as it does in the physical world. But one law can counteract another, in the one realm as in the other. As water may be made to run uphill, or as an object may be made to stay in the air indefinitely, so a spiritual force can overcome, say, the effects of destructive germs in the human body by setting to work the healing process. Of the miraculous element in Mormonism we shall speak in another section.

If the church of Christ is necessary at all, it should be necessary to every one. That ought to go without saying. Moreover, on the theory that God is just and no respecter of persons, every one should have the opportunity of accepting or rejecting the gospel. Then he would accept also the responsibility—each in his own case.

But there have been long periods in the history of the world when the church of Christ has not been accessible to all the people. There have been long periods, specifically before the Christian Era began, when the church was not even in existence; and even when it was among men, there have been whole nations where the gospel was not preached.

Very naturally the question arose, What becomes of those who, for either of these reasons, did not receive the Word? Christian ministers had no answer. They were deeply perplexed. God apparently had formulated a plan of salvation that failed to reach more than a small fraction of those for whom it was intended! Jehovah was, after all, a respecter of persons, a partial deity, and rather futile as a redemptive architect.

To the Mormon prophet, however, this was evidently not a knotty theological problem. And so he may be said to have rescued the Christian God from the charge of partiality, injustice, short-sightedness, and inefficiency.

It was the spirit of man, not his physical body, that chose to accept or to reject truth. Doubtless it was the duty of man to exercise his freedom of choice on earth. That is what he was sent here for, in the first place, from the pre-earth sphere. But if he did not even hear the gospel, how could he be condemned? On the other hand, though, he could not receive any benefits from a law which he had not obeyed. For, obviously, if the spiritual world is governed by law, as the Prophet averred it is, then he could not expect to reap where he had not sown. Otherwise where was the good of any law?

The fact is, that according to Joseph Smith, the gospel was among men during periods before Christ, as well as afterwards, and many took advantage of the situation as did Enoch and his people. The ante-deluvians were as precious to God as those who came after them. Indeed, one of the striking things in the Book of Mormon is the idea that the Lord was as concerned with the inhabitants of the American continent as he was with those of Palestine. It was a conception that threw the spotlight upon God. And so, at various periods in history, God has dispensed his Word; but, through the propensity of man to forget, the doctrines of the gospel became overlaid with ideas foreign to the divine intent. Even among the East Indians the followers of Gautama, after a while, came to think more of the tree under which the wise man received his vision than of the eight paths to spiritual peace.

But the earth as we see it with the eye of the head, is not the only place where man may accept or reject the plan of salvation. The spirit world, the world into which every one goes when he dies, is such a place. For, as the attributes of thinking, feeling, and willing are things of the spirit, so, too, is organization. And so one may become a member of the church of Christ in the spirit world. The same men who preached here preach there. The Mormons are fond of quoting Peter on the subject. According to the first of apostles, Christ "went and preached to the spirits in prison." And he did so, the apostle adds, that they "might be judged according to men in the flesh."

Since, however, such ordinances as baptism, confirmation, ordination to the priesthood, and marriage for eternity are matters of the flesh-world, these ordinances are performed for the "dead" by the living. It is a revival of the doctrine of "baptism for the dead," spoken of by Paul as having been practiced anciently. The "work" for the "dead" is done in temples—of which there are seven: four in Utah, one in Canada, one in Arizona, and one in the Hawaiian Islands.

Whether such "proxy" rites have any efficacy is of course a matter of opinion, depending on whether or not the claims of the Mormons are accepted. It is certain, though, that there is a high social value in the idea.

Old people, as a rule, find life rather empty. This is particularly true of those who are poor as well as old. The tendency is to give up, to look backward, to consider themselves a burden in the world, useless. There is nothing to look forward to.

There is a good deal less of that among the Mormons than among any other class of people. The reason lies in temple work. For one thing, they look on their "work" as a service, not to the "dead," but rather to the living; only, they are gone into "another room" for a time. And then, for another thing, these old people are forever looking forward. Some day they, too, will go into that "other room." There they will see those whom they served. Besides, they earn a little money this way. For people who can afford the means but not the time, are willing to make small donations for this purpose.

Thus we come to the end of our consideration of the teachings of Joseph Smith, the Mormon prophet. And now it is necessary to take a bird's-eye view of these doctrines, to summarize them, and to look at them by and large, so as to see their general drift.

Mormonism, to begin with, is a "positive" religion in the sense in which Compte used this term. That is to say, it faces the facts of life frankly and honestly, without blinking, and attempts to solve human problems in plain sight of these facts. Only, in the view of the founder of Mormonism, there are spiritual facts, as well as physical and social facts, which the French philosopher would probably not have admitted—at least, in the sense in which the Prophet understood the phrase.

When, however, we say that Mormonism is a "positive" religion, we are speaking only of the religion of Joseph Smith, not that of some of his followers, past or present.

Disciples, as a rule, have different conceptions in some respects from those entertained by the founders of their faiths.

The case of Jesus is an instance. His followers, adhering to the traditional idea, persisted in the belief that he was a king in the old sense; and even Paul appears to have expected the Second advent of Christ in his own times. As it is, however, many centuries have come and gone, and that event has not happened yet.

Moreover, the "Christianity" of the nineteenth and the twentieth century bears little resemblance, in its inner heart, to the religion of the Gospels. If that faith had preserved the doctrine of human values and had been aggressive in maintaining it, we should not now be in the grip of the competitive system—which shows signs of breaking down at last under the oppressive load of selfishness and gross inequality.

Similarly the Prophet, for instance, believed in a personal devil. Satan, with his compeers, male and female, is a brother to man, is a brother also to the angels, is in fact a son of God, though an erring one; and he, and they, to the extent that we give them leeway, still go about "up and down the earth," endeavoring to tempt mortals. Yet some of the early Mormons, as well as some later ones, attributed "all diseases" to the influence of the devil—for which the leader called them to task. And there were other differences between what the Prophet and what some of his disciples had in mind.

Furthermore, the Mormonism of to-day varies somewhat from the pattern laid down by its founder. Neither plural marriage nor the law of consecration, for example, is now in effect among the Saints. That, however, as we have seen, is attributable to causes over which the Mormons had little or no control; for, in the one case, the Federal government, urged on by popular sentiment, raised a prohibitive finger, and, in the other case, eternal conditions, mainly, brought the movement to an abortive end. The Mormons, though, still accept both polygamy and the United Order "in principle," as the diplomat might say.

And so it becomes necessary for the biographer of Joseph Smith to make what may appear an invidious distinction between the fundamentals of the faith as they were taught by its founder and the inferences and implications of his disciples. The non-recognition of this distinction has often brought Mormonism into more or less ill repute, not only with the outsider, but with the disciple as well.

Mormonism, then, to come back to our original thought, does not play the ostrich with facts. It is not of the contemplative, pillar-saint variety of religion. Joseph Smith felt even more concern over the prevailing social, economic, and political situation than he did over the future state of the soul. For he knew that, if mankind could but be made prosperous, happy, and decent here, his strictly spiritual ideas—those about God, immortality, and human values—might be left to take care of themselves.

However wrong-headed and anachronistic polygamy may have been thought by a generation that had grown romantic rather than realistic in its ideals of marriage, the practice of that doctrine by the Mormons was at least a brave attempt to face the distressing fact of prostitution, secret and open, a fact which a "Christian civilization" has persistently and inconsistently shut its eyes to, and then to apply to the situation, not without heroism, the only remedy that has ever held out any hope.

The United Order was an even braver, and far more difficult attempt to face and to solve the bread-and-butter problem. No religion, in the Prophet's view, was of any value unless it tried to do something here. And it was equally futile under the circumstances, because, in the Mormon leader's own phrase in another connection, the time was not ripe to attack economic greed and selfishness at their root.

For always Joseph Smith attempted to apply remedies by removing the cause of disease. He never attacked any problems otherwise. Thus plural marriage would remedy the social evil by giving every normal woman a husband, so as to allow expression to the maternal instinct by legal means, instead of leaving her a prey to the hawk-like propensities of the male; the "law of consecration" would solve the economic problem by removing the incentives to greed, acquisitiveness, and the piling up of huge surpluses; and the political remedies proposed aimed to substitute group-mindedness for individual-mindedness. All along the line, Joseph Smith was laying the axe at the root of the tree.

Second, in Mormonism everything takes its beginning and acquires meaning and direction from religion.

It would be hard to find anywhere in the world a community-life so dominated by the religious spirit as the one established by Joseph Smith. As a matter of fact, not even primitive society was more influenced by the religious spirit—only, of course, not in quite the same way. Very early in his career the Prophet came to know that religion furnishes the only working hypothesis in human affairs; and this idea is pretty well accepted to-day by the thoughtful, now that the world is going awry at so many points.

What is it that gives us our idea of human values? and where do we get our notion of what is of most worth in human life? Joseph Smith would answer, "Religion." And the Mormon leader added something to what Jesus said on the subject-namely, the idea of the continuity of the human spirit and the idea of development. For, as we have seen, he taught that man, in his ultimate being, had no beginning, that he will have no end, and that "salvation" is a gradual process of growth toward a definite, although moving, goal.

Moreover, man is a god in embryo, as God is a perfected man. Right or wrong, sacred or blasphemous, this audacious belief has always motivated conduct in the Mormons. It has worked two ways: It has made the idea of God a little less incomprehensible to the average man, and it has enhanced the value of man in his own eyes by revealing his potentialities. These beliefs, centering in the human personality, have exerted a tremendous influence in those who have accepted them.

Religion also has affected the ideal of the family-group among the Mormons. It has made for large, rather than small, families, because in "heaven," the pre-earth world where we all were once, spirits are waiting to come here for bodies, and these spirits must be well born and well brought up.

This idea sounds strange in the second quarter of the twentieth century, with our new methods of ascertaining truth. Yet it is believed by all orthodox Mormons. As we shall see in another section of this book, the Prophet drew a sharp distinction between the things which the human mind can find out of itself and the things which are beyond its utmost reach.

Children being of primary importance, it is essential that they have a good home. The spirit of religion must predominate there—faith in God, repentance from sin in the sense already explained, and prayer. Furthermore, the Mormon family does not so easily break up through divorce, since the parents, looking upon their marital agreement as "eternal," make a conscious effort to adjust their difficulties, partly for the sake of the children, and partly because each of them is apt to take the view that no one else can really make him happy but that he must do that himself.

The way in which the earlier men among the Mormons—and to a decreasing extent the present generation of Mormons—looked forward to establishing a line of descendants was truly patriarchal. Jacob did not excel them in this respect. There is no place on earth, it is safe to say, where family reunions are so common as among the followers of Joseph Smith. The descendants of the first Mormon to bear any particular name are inclined to look after one another, no matter how they may have become scattered by circumstances. The "head of the house" will continue to be at the head in the next world, where his "glory" will be, to some extent, in proportion to the number of his posterity. Hence the family name must not be tarnished by crime or apostasy.

Similarly the group-life is influenced by the spirit of religion. It is religion that brings Mormon families together, as a rule, especially in the smaller communities. Every Sabbath day the children and the young people come together in Sunday school, where they have classes in religion; and in the afternoon or evening the older folk meet in a religious service. On a week day (or evening) the women assemble in the Relief Society, and study theology, literature, or social work; the young men and women in an "Improvement Association," where they study secular subjects and sometimes dance; and the children between the ages of six and twelve years in a junior seminary.

In addition to all this, the members of each ward come together in a "stake conference" every three months; and the members of each stake in a "general conference" twice a year. It has been said humorously that the whole Mormon population in any given community is always either going to or returning from some meeting or other—and there is some truth in the statement. But this frequent meeting is not without its benefits; for the mere coming together with a central purpose, the free exchange of ideas, has done much in producing group-mindedness among the Saints and in creating an attitude of sympathetic understanding.

And then of course religion is responsible for those innumerable streams of ideas that trickle into every Mormon community, by way of the missionary, and that serve to lift the people generally into a cosmopolitanism which cannot be duplicated in other rural districts in America. This motion picture of the world went on the Mormon screen long before our modern transportation facilities and the film and radio came into existence.

Thus Mormonism takes as its field all of life. It does not specialize in theology, although it has, as we have seen, some decided notions on the subject; and it does not concentrate on the forms and ceremonials of religion, although it does not ignore these. The faith of Joseph Smith is not a thing apart from life. Nor is it a wedge driven down into life. It is life. Whatever men and women do in the world, wherever they may live and carry on their thought and work—there Mormonism would be to guide and to inspire. Religion, as the Prophet saw it, must be more than a finger-post to heaven; it must be an affirmative influence saturating life everywhere, giving purpose, energy, and direction.

When the little band of Mormon pioneers entered the Great Salt Lake basin, now called Utah, in 1847, the first thing they did was to kneel down on the dry, alkali soil, and dedicate it to God, praying at the same time that it might be made fruitful under their hand. Later, when the Salt Lake theater was built in the sage-brush, Brigham Young set it apart in a dedicatory prayer, as a place for wholesome amusement. Mormon dances in those days always opened and closed with prayer—as indeed they still do in many wards. Young men have always been advised by the Mormon leaders to ask for divine guidance in the choosing of either a vocation or a wife. And the late George Q. Cannon, who for many years was counselor to the president of the church, whenever he found himself in need of funds for certain purposes, paid tithing on the amount he needed—on the assumption, it would seem, that, if he did his duty by the Lord, the Lord would "open the way" by which he might obtain the required means.

In the early days of Utah the influence of religion was more potent in commercial and political life throughout Mormondom than in later years. As stated elsewhere, a coÃ¶perative movement, a chain-store system, was organized by Brigham Young in Utah, so as to lower prices and divert dividends into the pockets of the people generally. Often the church furnished money for enterprises too great for individuals to carry on. The first sugar factories in Utah were established on the initiative of the head of the church. And political offices were filled, not by packed conventions, but by the local group in quite informal gatherings.

Taking all life as its field, Mormonism remains fluid instead of fixed. It will be recalled that Joseph Smith informed a small group of politicians in Springfield, Illinois, that the difference between his faith and others' was that the Mormon church did not have a creed to hamper thinking and planning. Creeds made a religion rigid and unyielding. Mormonism is, therefore, a religion in flux. When a problem arises in any department of life, it is not necessary first to consult a creed, to see whether any particular measure may be suggested. Instead, the situation is studied, a remedy is looked for, and then it is applied. The only standard of measurement is the one laid down by the Prophet—human values.

Third, the forces working in and through Mormonism tend, if they are let alone, to focus interest on one supreme purpose. Already we have had occasion to note the way in which this religion has become a melting pot of both religions and of nationalities. It could as easily merge the purpose and lives of a hundred million diverse men and women as of a hundred thousand.

And then there is the work of missionaries—which we have mentioned in another connection. This work looks in the same direction; it tends to unity on a large scale. For these men and women go into the towns and cities here and there throughout the world; they mingle with the people there on intimate terms, sometimes learning their language for the purpose; and then they return home, feeling only the kindliest sentiments for those far-off strangers and for their nation. Thus national barriers are broken down. One cannot hate those whom one has come to call by the name of friend.

In addition, Mormonism was designed for all mankind, not for a few in one nation only. This makes for world unity. If ever Joseph Smith becomes a popular hero, as is not at all unlikely, he will be a world hero, not a national hero merely. And this will be due to the fact that his scheme of life has in it some universal elements.

But Mormonism has a unique unifying process in its belief concerning the race as a whole. All men, according to the Prophet, are to be reached by the divine plan of redemption, either in this world of the flesh or in the intermediate world of the spirit, so that they may have the opportunity to say yes or no to that plan. Countless millions have already been given this opportunity in the various dispensations between Adam and Joseph Smith so far as the present world is concerned. But meantime redemption has been going on in the world of the spirit. And from now on, till the "end of the world," in both spheres, there will be preaching of truth, accompanied by adherence to or rejection of that truth. It was necessary, the Prophet said, that all the "dispensation" be "welded" together somehow, because "they without us and we without them" cannot be made perfect. The heart of the fathers must be turned to the children, and the heart of the children must be turned to the fathers. Otherwise the earth would be "smitten."

The "welding link," in this case, is the work called "salvation for the dead." In baptism for the dead (not baptism of the dead, as some have had it) the heart of the children is turned to the fathers, and, presumably, that of the fathers is turned to the children, on account of the latter's advantageous position.

The point is, of course, that the sense of world unity has been created in Mormonism, by reason partly of this belief in "salvation for the dead." A universal bond of sympathy is thus brought into existence in those who believe that this service for the deceased is efficacious. Race, national, and creed boundaries are obliterated by this idea. All men become brothers in a new sense, and every one may thus become a "savior," a Christ in miniature.

But Mormonism would also unify through a supreme purpose in life. It would dissolve in a crucible all the petty aims that confuse and distract humanity, and substitute therefor a new purpose—the development of the human personality.

The trouble with the world, as the Mormon prophet would diagnose it, is that nations are dominated by the nationalistic spirit and their membership by the individualistic spirit, and that the cure in each case is to broaden the outlook by means of the spirit of religion—namely the religion of Jesus. This, to be sure, is not a new remedy, but it is an effective one; and it needs reiteration in every land, with emphasis.

A necessary result of the nationalistic spirit was the frightful waste of money, property, and human life in war, with its consequent higher tax burdens and lower moral standards. The only coÃ¶peration among nations was dictated by selfishness and fear of a common enemy. But this nationalistic spirit resulted from the same spirit in the individual citizen. Nations worked for their own interests because individuals worked for theirs. And this individualism, in turn, arose out of a false "Christianity," which, forgetting its founder's injunction to "love thy neighbor as thyself," embarked upon a sea of ecclesiasticism, instead of human welfare.

The way out was clear to Joseph Smith. Put into every man's heart a sense of his own supreme worth in the sight of God. Teach him, from his childhood up, the ideas that make for character development, so that he shall know the road for himself. Then teach him the religion that gives him power. Finally, surround him with an economic system that makes all men equal, and a social system that makes all women equal.

No doubt it would take time to convert the world. But the world would be converted in the end. You had really but to know and see the truth, and you were converted to it. One thousand, three thousand, ten thousand missionaries, all preaching the higher human values-what could such a body of men not accomplish!

That was the Mormon prophet's dream.
