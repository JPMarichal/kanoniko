John Henry Evans, Joseph Smith, an American Prophet ( 1946)

CHAPTER X

Dignity and Worth of the Human Personality

Remember the worth of souls is great in the sight of God. And if it so be that you should labor all your days in crying repentance, and bring, save it be one soul unto Him, how great shall be your joy with that soul in the Kingdom of Heaven. —JOSEPH SMITH.

If one were asked to lay one's finger on the word that best expresses the essence of the religion founded by Joseph Smith, that word would doubtless be anthropocentric. To the Mormon prophet the human personality, besides being immensely fascinating because so mysterious and elusive, was far and away the most important and precious thing in the whole universe.

Moreover, the founder of Mormonism, while he had something to say about God, immortality, and the hereafter—subjects that are at the other end of man's life, if one may put it that way—nevertheless bore down heavily on the necessity of giving much thought to this world, on the ground apparently that, as it was later expressed by his nephew, Joseph F. Smith, who became head of the church, "a religion which has not the power to save people temporally and make them prosperous and happy here, cannot be depended upon to save them spiritually and to exalt them in the life to come."

The work of Joseph Smith, so far as this point is concerned, was of a piece with that started in the sixteenth century by the Protestant Reformers. Only, the Mormon prophet appears to have gone much farther on the same path.

When Luther and his fellow iconoclasts put the Bible into the hands of the layman in his own tongue, they did not intend, it seems, to raise the question of a man's worth as an individual. That was a by-product of their thought. They sought only to reÃ«stablish the forgotten right of every man and woman to go to God directly with their problems instead of through the church. As soon therefore as the idea of the right of private judgment sank into the consciousness of dissenters generally, their importance as individuals took on a new meaning to them. Thereafter each man counted as one.

But that this idea of human values was not clearly and boldly outlined in the minds of the Reformers is evident from the views held by Christians in the time of Joseph Smith. "A little lower than the angels," who were a distinct species from man, was the highest station allotted to any human being by the churches of his day. But in the eschatology of such austere theologians as Jonathan Edwards man was just a poor worm of the dust, struggling to escape a never ending punishment in the next world—not through his own merits, but through the grace of God.

In the mind of Joseph Smith, however, the dignity and worth of human personality was both clear and conscious. It was not an incident in his theology, but a main theme in his religious teachings, and it received overwhelming emphasis. We shall come to this point again in another section, when we consider the Prophet's theology as a background to his social and economic ideals. For the present it is sufficient to look at the matter in its importance merely.

In Mormonism man is the center. Everything converges in him. All the spiritual forces of the universe find a focus in him, with a view to his development. The earth was created for his benefit. It is to be his permanent home, his heaven of the hereafter. Also man is, literally and not figuratively, the child of God, and as such inherits the nature and potentialities of his Parents of the spirit. God, therefore, is watchfully anxious for the welfare of his child—more so than any earthly parent could be. In a sense God exists for the glory of man, not man for the glory of God, as in the age-old Christian dogma.

One of the things that serve to stress this importance of the individual in Mormonism is the fact that every man, provided he shows himself at all worthy, is given the priesthood. We have mentioned this in another connection, but it has an especial bearing here. Every man is entitled to the priesthood, instead of only a very few. Given the priesthood, a man at once becomes important in his own eyes. He begins to play a new rÃ´le in the world. Indirectly this priesthood has an almost equal influence on the woman; for, although in the Prophet's religious scheme women do not "hold" any priesthood, yet they share, both here and hereafter, in whatever benefits are supposed to come to men through its possession.

Priesthood thus became a great leveler in the religion of Joseph Smith—not by bringing any man down, but by lifting all men up. The psychological potency of this endowment can hardly be over estimated. For, when the man who cleans the spitoons in a bank belongs to the same order or "quorum" as its president (indeed, he may be his presiding officer there), he acquires an importance in his own eyes that is very energizing and that ramifies into everything he does.

This conception of human values—all the more striking since it made its appearance before our "new individualism"—became immediately a determining factor in the life of the men and women who constituted the membership of the Mormon church. It served to decide a thousand and one questions that arose in their daily lives. Everything to be said or done, whether in private or in public life, became meaningful or meaningless in proportion to its contributing or not contributing to human welfare. That is, it did so to all those who really caught the meaning of the new thought. It became a veritable master-key.

Man was, first of all, an isolated being. He was so by reason of his inherent nature. In his body he was shut out from every one else, except to the extent that he wished to reveal himself. Furthermore, man, like God, was a spirit, and he possessed in his present state a physical body of flesh and blood. Both the spirit and the body must be developed to their highest capacity. For Joseph Smith never entertained the notion so prevalent among Christians during the Middle Ages and after, that the "soul" was to be "saved" apart from the body. On the contrary, he taught that it was to be "saved" in the body.

And so Mormonism contemplated, not only that the mind, the spirit, the real personality of man should be disciplined and trained, but also that the physical "tabernacle," the means of contact with the outer world, the instrument of the mind or spirit, the brain and nervous system, ought to be perfected, so as to become as efficient an instrument as possible under the circumstances. We shall see presently by what means the Mormon leader proposed to bring about this desired result in his disciples.

But man did not live alone. He lived in the group and formed a part of it, through which, mainly, he attained his greatest development. There were to be no "pillar saints" in Joseph Smith's scheme of life.

A man's contacts came, first in the home—his parents' to begin with, and then his own. What ideals should prevail there? The answer was to be sought in the value of the human personality. Whatever helped in the development of the whole man, including the body, was "good," and whatever hindered that process was "bad."

Outside the home was the larger group—the industrial, or the group in which man made his living; the political, where he made contact with his fellows in his capacity of citizen; and the religious, in which he came to think and work with others spiritually. This multiform group life naturally gave rise to a thousand problems of the most perplexing nature. How should they be solved? Very simply—by reference to the dominant idea of human values. That economic, social, political, or religious action was best, which measured its effects on the individuals involved. No one knew better than Joseph Smith how the mind can be side-tracked by other considerations than human worth.

In this way the Mormon leader came to decide matters that sprang up in his active life: Whether a city should be large or small; what relation should subsist between the town and the country; just which men one ought to vote for in a political election; whether a man should be left free to choose a vocation, or have it handed out to him by some one; whether there should be rich men and poor men in any commonwealth, and, if not, how the distinction was to be avoided; whether the monogamic or the polygamic system of marriage was the better.

To the Mormon prophet, in short, the final test of any line of action, private or public, industrial, domestic, political, social, economic, or religious was, not whether it contributed to selfish interests, but to the growth of the human personality, human welfare. And this standard is all the more noteworthy because it was thought out between ninety and a hundred years ago.

Of course, all this took for granted that personal autonomy is a fact in human life. And this was a fundamental assumption in the religious philosophy of Joseph Smith.

As already stated, the Prophet never indulged in any fine-spun distinctions between tweedle-dee and tweedle-dum. This is true especially of such questions as the freedom of the will. He assumed that man is free. Hence, as was his habit, he merely announced that every one has his "free agency." Within certain limits all men are at liberty to choose their way of life. And the Mormon leader always respected this right of choice in both disciples and outsiders.

"All persons," he said once in a letter to the Saints scattered abroad, "are entitled to their agency, for God has so ordained it. He has constituted mankind moral agents, and given them power to choose good or evil; to seek after that which is good, by pursuing the pathway of holiness in this life, which brings peace of mind and joy in the Holy Ghost here, and a fulness of joy and happiness hereafter; or to pursue an evil course, going on in sin and rebellion against God, thereby bringing condemnation to their souls in this world and an eternal loss in the world to come. Since the God of heaven has left these things optional with every individual, we do not wish to deprive them of it. We only wish to act the part of a faithful watchman."

On another occasion, in a sermon, he said this: "If I esteem mankind to be in error, shall I bear down on them? No. I will lift them up, and in their own way too, if I cannot persuade them my way is better. I will not seek to compel any man to believe as I do, only by force of reasoning, for truth will cut its own way. Do you believe Jesus Christ and the gospel of salvation which he revealed? So do I. Christians should cease wrangling and contending with each other, and cultivate the principles of union and friendship. I am just as ready to die in defending the rights of a Presbyterian, a Baptist, or a good man of any other denomination. For the same principles which would trample upon the rights of the Latter-day Saints would trample upon the rights of the Roman Catholics, or of any other denomination who may be unpopular and too weak to defend themselves."

And on still another occasion he said this in a religious discourse: "In relation to the power over the minds of mankind which I am said to hold, I would say, It is in consequence of the power of truth in the doctrines which I have been an instrument in the hands of God of presenting unto them, and not because of any compulsion on my part. I wish to ask if I ever got any of it unfairly or if I have not reproved you in the gate? Did I ever exercise any compulsion over any man? Did I not give him the liberty of disbelieving any doctrine I have preached, if he saw fit? Why do not my enemies strike a blow at the doctrine? They cannot do it. It is the truth, and I defy all men to upset it. I am a voice crying in the wilderness."

The standard fixed upon by the Prophet to govern the relations between the controlling body of priesthood in the church and the membership generally is the following, which is quoted to-day among the Mormons more perhaps than any other with a similar import:

It is the nature and disposition of almost all men, that as soon as they get a little authority, as they suppose, they will immediately begin to exercise unrighteous dominion.

The rights of the priesthood are inseparably connected with the powers of heaven, and the powers of heaven cannot be controlled nor handled only upon the principles of righteousness. That they may be conferred upon us, it is true. But when we undertake to cover our sins, or to gratify our pride, our vain ambition, or to exercise control or dominion or compulsion upon the souls of the children of men, in any degree of unrighteousness, behold, the heavens withdraw themselves; the Spirit of the Lord is grieved; and when it is withdrawn, Amen to the priesthood or the authority of that man.

No power or influence can or ought to be maintained by virtue of the priesthood, only by persuasion, by long-suffering, by gentleness and meekness, and by love unfeigned; by kindness and pure knowledge, which shall greatly enlarge the soul without hypocrisy and without guile—reproving betimes with sharpness, when moved upon by the Holy Ghost; and then showing forth an increase of love toward him whom thou hast reproved, lest he esteem thee to be his enemy; that he may know that thy faithfulness is stronger than the cords of death.

This was written to his people while he was a prisoner in Liberty jail. Nowhere in religious literature can there be found a passage that exalts the human personality more than this.

Curious disciples of the Mormon prophet, more than three-quarters of a century after his death, began an inquiry into the sources of his large collection of ideas. It is an engaging subject.

There is no doubt whatever that the first suggestion in at least some cases came from his surroundings. Joseph Smith's mind was of the inquisitive sort. While it might be too much to say that his emphasis on the cultivation of the mind is to be attributed to his own early deficiencies in this respect or that his teachings concerning the care of the body arose out of his fondness for athletic sports, we should easily be within the truth when we say that, in his mind, there was a close connection between these topics and his estimate of the worth of a man, that the mind and the body were to be looked after because they enhanced the value of the human personality, both in this world and in the next.

As early as the end of the year 1832 Joseph Smith urged his following to "teach one another more perfectly in theory, in principle, in doctrine, in the law of the gospel, in all things that pertain to the kingdom of God." That, so far as religion was concerned. But they were to tell one another also "of things in heaven, in the earth, and under the earth; of things which have been, things which are, and things which must shortly come to pass; of things which are at home and things which are abroad; the wars and the perplexities of nations; and also of countries and kingdoms—that they might be prepared to magnify their callings and to go on missions to preach." For Mormonism must be set forth with intelligence and information.

Already a "high school" had been established in Kirtland, and Oliver Cowdery had been delegated to "write books for schools in this church." Also a "school of the prophets," as we have seen, was opened in the same town and another in Missouri. The following year an injunction was given by the head of the church to "study and learn and become acquainted with all good books, and with languages, tongues, and people." And one of the sections in the dedicatory prayer at the completion of the Kirtland temple was to this effect: "Do thou grant, holy Father, that all those who shall worship in this house may be taught words of wisdom out of the best books, and that they may seek learning even by study, and also by faith." The purpose of this was, curiously enough, "that they might grow up in thee and receive a fulness of the Holy Ghost"—a connection that has often been missed, even by faithful disciples of the Prophet.

In Nauvoo, as we have seen, a university was established, with some very able instructors. In addition there were lower schools, equivalent to what we now call the primary grades—although of course not so efficient. This was a good start on the educational road, considering the many obstacles the Mormon people had to surmount.

After the death of the leader, Brigham Young endeavored to carry out the educational plans of his predecessor. For schools were conducted even on the westward trek of the Saints, and the University of Deseret, now the University of Utah, was founded shortly after the pioneers arrived in their new home.

Nor does the Mormon prophet appear to have been slow to take his own advice about cultivating the mind. As we have seen, he studied law under two of the ablest practical lawyers in Missouri—Generals Atchison and Doniphan. Professor Joshua Seixas, a Hebrew scholar, taught him the tongue of the ancient Israelites. That was at Kirtland. In Nauvoo he read German, and in one or two places in his Autobiography he speaks of making a grammar of the Egyptian language. During the last years of his life, also, his writings and sermons bear evidence of his having read not a little of history and political science. On the occasion of his running for President of the United States, a stranger, writing to the local paper, speaks of him as "diving into every subject."

As for what we eat and drink and for other things connected with the care of the body, Joseph Smith was, to say the least, fully abreast of his time, if not actually ahead of it a little.

The body was to be kept fit, not merely because it paid to do so, but because it was the tabernacle of the Holy Spirit. Moreover, the Holy Ghost, which guides into all truth, will not dwell in an unclean tabernacle.

Some of the Prophet's ideas on the subject touch what is considered "not good for the body."

"Strong drinks," by which he meant alcoholic beverages, are "not good, neither meet in the sight of your Father." An exception is made of wine that is used in the Lord's Supper. But even in this case the wine must be "pure grape of the vine, and of your own make." As a matter of fact, the Mormons, from the very beginning, have used water for this purpose, instead of wine, because the wine has had, as a rule, to be purchased from "enemies." With this exception, "strong drinks" are condemned most emphatically as "not for the belly, but for the washing of your bodies."

Similarly tobacco and hot drinks are characterized as "not good for man." In the case of tobacco, the weed is declared to be fit only "for bruises and sick cattle," and even then it must be used with "judgment and skill." The phrase "hot drinks" has always been taken to mean tea and coffee—which "are not for the body or belly."

There is a prohibition also against excessive meat-eating. Although "the flesh of beasts and of the fowls of the air is ordained for the use of man, with thanksgiving, nevertheless they are to be used sparingly." In fact, it would be "pleasing" to God if animal food should be used "only in times of winter or cold or famine."

These are the inhibitions—tobacco, alcohol, tea and coffee, and the over-eating of flesh. Other items in this document are positive.

"All wholesome herbs God has ordained for the constitution, nature, and use of man." It is best, however, that every herb and fruit shall be used "in the season thereof," albeit with "prudence and thanksgiving." While "all grain is good for the food of man," yet "wheat for man, corn for the ox, oats for the horse, rye for the fowls and swine and for all beasts of the field, and barley for all useful animals, and for mild drinks."

This body of dietary doctrines has come down in history as the "Word of Wisdom."

What gave rise to this rather remarkable collection of suggestions is not known. Usually the Prophet "inquired of the Lord." But what brought up the question of food and drink in the first place? That may never be known. It would not be at all surprising if, about this time, he had picked up a medical work by a physician of advanced views on the subject (of which there were some, as we know), or if, on one of his short trips, he had heard of a new theory concerning the body, and then decided to go to the Lord for some thing "more perfect by way of revelation on the subject." That would be characteristic.

But, if so, where did he get the rest of the document in which these ideas are embodied? The document was given by the Prophet to the church "not by way of commandment or constraint," but by way of counsel, with a "promise" attached. It soon became a matter of "constraint," however, for one of the charges against David Whitmer was that he "did not keep the word of wisdom." And the rule was presently promulgated that those who occupied positions of trust in the organization must observe the principles laid down in that code.

The church was not yet three years old when the "Word" was announced. It was not thought difficult to observe, however. For it was declared to be "adapted to the weakest of those who are, or can be called, saints." Obviously the one who dictated this last phrase had never struggled with the drink habit or even with the cigarette habit. But then we come back to a favorite idea of Joseph Smith's, that God was a God of power and would succor man in times of need.

Furthermore, the "word of wisdom" was uttered as a "warning" to the Saints. In "the last days" there would be men who would conspire against those who sought to do what was right. The brewers of intoxicating beverages and the head of the tobacco trusts, according to the Mormons, are among those men who have "evil designs."

Whoever observes the injunctions mentioned in the "word of wisdom" was promised a double blessing. First, he should have "health in his navel and marrow to his bones," he should "run and not be weary and walk and not faint," and the "destroying angel" should "pass by and not slay him," as in the case of the Israelites in Egypt. Second, he should "find wisdom and great treasures of knowledge, even hidden treasures." All of which accords with the idea, elsewhere expressed by the Prophet, that effects are always predicated on the observance of law.

The counsel of the Mormon prophet found in the "word of wisdom" is still observed by his followers. There is probably no community anywhere in which so few men proportionately use tobacco, drink tea and coffee, and indulge in intoxicating liquors.

Outside this pronouncement are some more "commandments" respecting physical matters.

"Cease to be idle," the Prophet says in another place; "cease to be unclean; cease to sleep longer than is needful; retire to your bed early, that you may not be weary; arise early, that your bodies and minds may be invigorated. Let your garments be plain, and their beauty the beauty of your own hands."

He enlarges on some of these items. "The idler shall not have place in the church, except he repent and mend his ways." Again, "Thou shalt not be idle; for he that is idle shall not eat the bread nor wear the garments of the laborer."

The emblem of Utah is the bee-hive—from "deseret," the name in the Book of Mormon for the honey-bee.

One of the profoundest utterances ever made by the Mormon prophet, and probably the most revolutionary, is his dictum that, if men are "not equal in earthly things," they "cannot be equal in obtaining heavenly things." It is a stark proposition, and sets one to thinking.

Among the great teachers of religion three separate types are distinguishable. There is, first of all, the man who attempts to solve his problems through escape. Oppressed by the mystery of life or pained by the corruption and injustice around him, he seeks peace and goodness in retirement and contemplation. A classical example of this is found in the East Indian Gautama.

A good-looking son of wealth, Gautama lived a life of ease and idleness till he was twenty-nine. Then, for the first time, he looked upon poverty, disease, and death, in three different beggars. His eyes were opened. Abandoning his riches, his social position, his wife, and a new born child, he "wandered alone," as Mr. H. G. Wells puts it, "the loneliest figure in history, battling for light." At first he sought that light through extreme asceticism, after the manner of his race. He fasted, he went without sleep, he tortured his body till he fell in a faint. But presently he saw that truth was not to be found that way. Finally, in the shade of a great tree, a new vision came to him, in which life was made plain. After that his fine intellect worked out the Eight Elements of the Path.

Then, there is the man who would remove his doubts and obtain peace through what Professor Carver calls "palaver." Of this type Augustine is a good example.

Augustine was not only the best, but the most influential of the Fathers. Born in the middle of the fourth century, A.D., he lived a varied and strange life. First, he was dissipated; then he experienced much inward struggle; and, finally, he became a celebrated preacher and writer, for which his education had fitted him. With the powerful emotions of his father, a pagan, and the spiritual nature of his mother, a Christian, he was converted, at the age of thirty-three, by the even-tempered Ambrose, bishop of Milan. First as a priest, then as assistant, and later as bishop of Hippo, he exerted wide influence in his time. It is through his writings, however, which are voluminous, that he wielded the greatest power. For a thousand years Christian leaders of thought read his works and were much affected by them—particularly two of the Reformers, Calvin and Luther.

And then, there is the man who answers his questions, not by mere thinking and words, as a door of escape, but by direct action. Lacking the long patience of Jesus to wait, he must perforce do something, and do it now. Joseph Smith was of this type.

Not that the Mormon leader did not meditate and write and talk a great deal. As we have seen elsewhere, he was naturally of a reflective temperament. His was a restive intellect. It was always busy at something or other that might contribute to the welfare of his people. And he also spoke much in public. His sermons, if we had them all, would run into millions of words, without repetition. As for his writings, the Book of Mormon contains more than one hundred and forty thousand words; the Doctrine and Covenants, which is the Mormon bible, has more than one hundred and fifty thousand words; and the History, not counting the "revelations," but only the secular documents, exceeds a million words.

The Prophet, however, was not satisfied with merely having an idea on a given subject; nor was he content with getting that idea across to his first lieutenants, and then waiting until these could act in the matter; but he must himself set going the machinery that would bring about the changes he proposed.

As soon, therefore, as he had conceived the ideal of social justice in his religious organization, he forthwith began to arrange for its application to his disciples. Spirituality must have free play among his followers; the competitive system in material affairs stood in the way; therefore the competitive system must go, and go now.

In this section we shall consider the basic ideas of his proposed economic order, leaving to the next section a discussion of its practical application.

The fundamental concept in Joseph Smith's mind was the spiritual regeneration of mankind, taken one at a time. He would make over men and women, so that the spiritual powers within them would be released to meet the spiritual forces without them in the universe, thus bringing about the "abundant life" of which Jesus spoke. This is the Mormon prophet's religious philosophy in a nutshell.

But how can a man with an empty stomach concentrate on the spiritual? Or a man without a job, or a job not to his liking, or one at which he cannot make a decent living? Or a man with the fear of sickness, old age, and permanent want tugging at his heart strings? Or a little child that has to be taken out of school and put to work in a factory, so as to help with the family budget? Or a widow with two jobs to do, one to set things to rights in the home and the other to provide bread and butter for a large group of children?

Or, for that matter, how could a man live the spiritual life who got his money by grinding the face of the poor? Who saw power only through money values? Or the woman whose only concern was whether her bonnet suited her complexion; what she should serve for tea or dinner; or how she might marry off her daughters well, by which she meant in the right set? Or the young man, just entering upon life, who wished above everything else to get on in the world, by which he understood getting money?

Here is what the Prophet said about the rich and the poor. The first paragraph may be found in the Book of Mormon, which he published when he was twenty-four, and the other paragraphs in his Doctrine and Covenants:

Wo unto those who are rich as to the things of this world. For, because they are rich, they despise the poor, persecute the meek, and set their hearts upon their treasure. Wherefore their treasure shall perish with them also.

Wo unto you rich men, that will not give of your substance to the poor, for your riches will canker your souls; and this shall be your lamentation in the day of visitation, of judgment, and of indignation: The harvest is past, the summer is ended, and my soul is not saved.

Wo unto you poor men, whose hearts are not broken, whose spirits are not contrite, whose bellies are not satisfied, whose hands are not stayed from laying hold upon other men's goods, whose eyes are full of greediness, and who will not labor with your own hands.

But blessed are the poor who are pure in heart, whose hearts are broken, and whose spirits are contrite, for they shall see the kingdom of God coming in power and great glory unto their deliverance.

These woeful pronouncements were followed up with some constructive suggestions, looking to a stable foundation for the Smith Utopia.

First, man had no inherent right to "own" anything material, least of all to snatch and grab things from under the eyes of his fellows, just because he was stronger or more far-sighted than they. That was the method of the jungle. If he "owned" anything at all, it was because the privilege had been granted him by the group, which owned everything. At most, man was but a "steward." He was accountable to the group, as the group was accountable to God and to one another.

It was in accordance with this fundamental idea of the Prophet's that Brigham Young, when he led the Mormons to what is now Utah, said in effect on that first day of their arrival: "No man here has any land to sell. He does not own it. We own it. And every head of a family shall have his land measured out to him—all he needs and can take care of. The water goes with the land. The timber in the mountains, also, belongs to us all, not to one man only, and we shall say when and under what circumstances it may be cut."

Second, every man was entitled to eat, to have clothing, and to live in a house—provided, however, he was able and willing to work for these necessities. God made the earth for man,—not for one man only, nor any set of men, however powerful—but for all men. There was "enough and to spare" of material goods in the world. The trouble lay in their accessibility. Joseph Smith would have been appalled, as indeed we all were, at the spectacle of 1932, of millions in want while the granaries were bursting with wheat, of millions shivering from the cold while the coal mines were idle for lack of buyers with money. It was wicked for a few strong men to monopolize the sources of wealth in the world, and it was wrong for "the poor and the meek" to suffer want by reason of that monopoly.

Third, the collective system that was to displace the individualism that held men in its clutch must not altogether ignore human nature, but work with it to the extent that it did not interfere with human welfare. That is, there should be both public and private ownership in the same economic scheme. Each head of a family should "own" his home, his instruments of production, and other necessities in his domestic and business or professional affairs. Also he was entitled to such part of what he produced as might be necessary for the maintenance of his household and business. A man could, after all, eat only so much, wear only a certain amount of clothing, and make use of only such shelter as would keep him from suffering. Whatever he produced more than that went into the general "storehouse" for the use of the community. This surplus would be necessary for taking care of those who could not work, for expansion purposes, and for other exigencies.

Fourth, each community was to be an independent unit. It would have its own bishop and other officers, its own storehouse, its own vocations, factories, employment agencies, etc. The communities might interchange ideas and goods without stint, but in the main each would make most of the things necessary to maintain itself—farm products, clothing, shoes, hats, and so on. All these separate units, however, would be under one general management—the presiding bishopric. That is, so far as its material affairs were concerned. Spiritually they and the bishopric would be amenable to the presidency of the church.

Fifth, this new collectivism, which the Prophet named the "United Order," was to rest on the secure foundation of religion.

And here we come to a puzzling aspect of Mormonism as founded by Joseph Smith. For Mormonism, in the plan of its founder, was not to be a church, a religion, or a sect, but a complete society. This fact, one suspects, was perhaps the major cause of conflict between Mormons and non-mormons in both Missouri and Illinois. It assuredly was in the Utah of Brigham Young's day. The social, the economic, and the political were inextricably commingled with the religious. To-day, with our enlarging conception of human welfare, this would not be so objectionable to the thoughtful as in the early years of Mormonism, before modern social ideas began to play for attention. Joseph Smith would make everything subordinate to human well-being—govemment, business, art and literature, as well as religion.

For the Mormon prophet, like Jesus, was a pronounced individualist, though not in the narrow accepted sense. Those writers who make the social teachings of Jesus central in his religion egregiously misunderstand the fundamental conception of the Nazarene. "Be ye therefore perfect, even as your Father in heaven is perfect"—that was primary with him. He did not say, "Love others more than yourself"; he said rather, "Love others as yourself." It was largely through service and group-mindedness that human character was to be developed. But the central ideal in the religion of Jesus, though not in the Christianity that succeeded it, was "the abundant life,"—and the "abundant life" is individual, not collective.

So with Joseph Smith. In his theology and religion the one thing of supreme importance in the universe was human personality. This thought will become clearer as we proceed with this discussion. To perfect this personality—that is, to make it like God—everything else must become contributory—the earth, human government, commercialism, other personalities, even the forms of religion. We shall see presently how this central idea ramifies throughout his whole system of thought. If the individualistic system of his day had been more favorable to the development of the human personality than the collective system, Joseph Smith would have been for that. But it was not favorable, it was decidedly inimical to that development. And so he worked out a scheme of collectivism. But the heart of his religion, the motivating power of it, was the gradual unfoldment of the human personality—not that of one man, or a few men, only, but of all men.

The Mormon leader came to know that there is no power in the world of men so great as the power of religion—of faith in God, faith in one's own potentialities and destiny, faith in a future life for glorified man, faith in salvation from sin, faith in a never-ending growth of the human spirit. He had seen this power at work in himself. "I was a rough stone," he says in his frank, picturesque way, "till the Lord took me in hand to polish me." And he had seen this power transform his disciples from men and women dominated by self-interest to men and women dominated by group interest. And he trusted to this power of religion to work in and through the United Order, so as to make it a factor in the development of character.

It is a thousand pities that the unsocial bigots in Western Missouri did not let the Mormons there alone long enough for us to see just what would eventuate from the application of Joseph Smith's economic principles in the United Order.

For all we know, there might have arisen in Jackson county and its environs an economic oasis in the great desert of the competitive and individualistic system in America that would focus the eyes of the world. It was an experiment worth trying. And it should have been let alone. As it was, however, the small attempt put forth in that locality pointed the way to some interesting coÃ¶perative efforts later, especially in early Utah in the time of Brigham Young.

Not in Zion, but in Thompson, a settlement out a little way from Kirtland, Ohio, the experiment of the United Order was first tried by the Prophet.

It may be recalled that, in 1831, the Colesville "branch" of the church moved in a body to Thompson. There were sixty members. It may be further remembered that, before their arrival, Edward Partridge was appointed bishop, the first in the organization, and that he was given a plan of the United Order by the Prophet and told to put it into operation in Thompson. Partridge did so—with what results we do not know. The members of the Order moved to Zion, in Missouri, during the summer, where they were absorbed into a larger group of twelve hundred persons.

At once the Order was set up in Jackson county, under the direction of Bishop Partridge, who probably had the principles and details of the scheme in mind better than any one other than the Prophet himself.

A couple of deeds are extant—relics of those days. They inform us about the legal transactions incident to the establishment of the Order. One is executed by Titus Billings and Edward Partridge, the other by Edward Partridge and Titus Billings. In the former, Billings deeds over to the Bishop "sundry articles of furniture, two beds with extra clothing, farm utensils, two horses, two wagons, two cows, two calves"—to the total value of three hundred sixteen dollars and fifty cents. These articles and animals are straightway deeded back to him, with a "piece and parcel of land," value not given. That was Billings's "stewardship."

This transfer of property was made "for the purpose of purchasing lands in Jackson county, Mo., for the building up of the New Jerusalem, even Zion, and for relieving the wants of the poor and needy." The contract says that Billings had first "paid his just debts."

Billings, on his part, agreed to "release all my right and interest to the above described property." Also in case of "transgression," on account of which he lost his standing in the church and therefore the Order, he was to give back the "lease" and then to pay "the equivalent of the loan" to the Bishop. The most important item, however, from our present point of view, was this clause: "I, the said Titus Billings, do bind myself to pay the taxes and also to pay yearly unto the said Edward Partridge, bishop, or his successor in office, for the benefit of the church, all that I shall make or accumulate more than is needful for the support and comfort of myself and family."

Bishop Partridge, on his part and in behalf of the church, bound himself (1) to allow Billings to "have and to hold the above described property to be used and occupied as tohim shall seem meet and proper"; (2) to "cause the same to be expended for the above mentioned purposes"; (3) to "support" Billings and his family "in case of their inability in consequence of infirmity or old age," the means to come "out of funds in his hands appropriated for that purpose," and, in the event of his death, to "provide for his widow and minor children," and, in case both parents died, to provide for the children till they should become of age. The children, however, were to be "subject to the same conditions yearly as the parents" had been.

Such were the terms on which every one accepted membership in the Order. It was an answer to every economic need of the individual—provided it worked. It removed out of the heart the family of "fears" which haunt the modern man—fear of unemployment, fear of want during sickness and old age, fear of insufficient food and clothing and rent money during years of employment; in a word, the fear of poverty.

Also it minimized economic envy and jealousy. For, while no two heads of families had exactly the same kind of house, the same amount of furniture, or the same equipment for his productive energy, everything being gauged according to the size of the family or actual need, yet every man had enough to eat, enough clothes to wear, and a house to live in, and every man was required to turn into the community chest whatever he produced above what he needed, whether it was much or little, whether it was the bishop or the most insignificant member. The law was uniformly enforced in the Order.

The project, however, failed for two very good reasons. For one thing, it was brought to a period by the expulsion of the whole Mormon community from the country. And, for another thing, the rich and the well-to-do in the church were somewhat reluctant to put their all into the common fund and then to give up all their surplus earnings for the public welfare. Their faith in the New Movement halted at this point. With the poorer class it was different. They had little to lose and everything to gain.

The Mormon leader did not attempt to establish the Order in the Far West region nor in Nauvoo. The plan was officially abandoned soon after the Mormons arrived in Illinois, and was never taken up again till some time in the seventies, in Utah.

Just why Brigham Young did not establish the United Order when he first entered the Salt Lake Valley with his people, is not clear. From our point of view to-day it was a rare opportunity for the pioneer leader.

We can think of reasons, but we are not sure that they were the ones Brigham Young had. Was it because he had heard unfavorable things about the working of the Order in Zion? He was not a member of the community there. Or was it because the burden of carrying on a new economic plan would be too great on top of the immense task of conducting the exodus of a whole people over a practically trackless country? Or was it because Brigham Young was not as social-minded as Joseph Smith and was not confident of his power?

At any rate, Young did not even attempt to set up the United Order among his people till he was too old to give it the personal attention it needed in order to succeed, although from the beginning of the Utah colony there had been a high degree of coÃ¶perative effort put forth in every economic endeavor. Even in the practice of the Order there were some notable achievements.

In the year 1883 Edward Bellamy, author of Looking Backward, visited Utah for the purpose of studying the Mormon economic system. He had a conversation with Lorenzo Snow, one of the apostles, which extended over three days. Snow, who had been a student in Oberlin College in Ohio, was an intelligent business man, as well as a high ecclesiast. The two went over the details of the new economic scheme and also into the coÃ¶perative efforts of the Utah saints. Bellamy went away, not only with considerable inspiration, but with some fundamental ideas, which he incorporated in his book. It is stated that Looking Backward had large influence in the making of Soviet Russia. If so, to this Mormon idea originally is to be given the credit (or the blame) for what has been going on there of late years. However that may be, it is certain that Bellamy's book was greatly influenced by what Snow told its author.

Three different forms of the United Order were practiced in early Utah.

One of these was a coÃ¶perative organization. It was created by Brigham Young when he saw the huge fortunes piled up by private enterprises at the expense of the people. Merchants brought goods into the Territory by the train-load (trains of wagons, that is), and sold them at exorbitant prices. He therefore organized a system of chain stores owned by the masses. There was a "coÃ¶p store" in every settlement, however small, with three main stores in as many sections of the Territory, the principal feeder being in Salt Lake City. Every head of a family was supposed to own stock in the organization. The system went by the resounding name of "Zion's CoÃ¶perative Mercantile Institution," abridged to "Z. C. M. I."

Prior to this Lorenzo Snow had put into effect a coÃ¶perative plan in his home town of Brigham City, in the northern part of the Territory. Only the strictly public institutions, however, were community-owned—the butter and cheese factory, the woollen mills, the stores, and most of the cattle. The community also owned and operated a cotton farm in the southern part of the Territory, where they got their raw materials for fabrics.

Both of these movements were highly successful till the coming of the railroad, in 1869, when the Snow enterprise failed because of the higher cost of production there.

Another form of the United Order existed in a little settlement in Southern Utah, Orderville. It was wholly communistic. The only individual property was wearing apparel, and all the people ate at a common table. It did not work out very satisfactorily, notwithstanding it was in a small town, a sort of large family, although those who are old now but who were children then recall with pleasure their life in the Order—which is only natural to a memory that usually buries the ill and remembers only the pleasant. In a larger community it would probably have failed long before it did.

By far the most successful effort ever put forth at any time in Mormondom was to be found in the settlement, also in the southern part of the Territory, which went by the name of Glenwood. The Order there answered perfectly to what Joseph Smith must have had in mind when he instituted the movement. It shows the idea at its best.

The community numbered about five hundred persons, all of whom belonged to the Mormon church. Nestling in an arm of low mountains, the town overlooked a pleasant valley to the west, while, to the southeast, it had access to one of the richest uplands of grazing land to be found anywhere. A bishop presided over the "ward," as such a group is known by the Saints. There was no political government.

Each head of a family (the Mormons always went by heads of families in those days) owned his home, the town lot on which his house stood, and the furniture in his home. That was a part of his "stewardship." Also he had his own vocation—which was whatever he felt himself best fitted to do. It might be farming, dairying, taking care of cattle or horses, running a flour or woollen mill, tanning hides, making shoes or mending them, building fences or houses, tailoring, serving in the store, bookkeeping, or school teaching. No lawyers were needed in that community, for the bishop settled all the disputes for nothing, and the midwife not only brought babies into the world, but saw to sick people and those who had met with accidents. Thus division of labor was carried to its most practical ideal.

Household necessities were supplied in a unique way. Every so often the farmer came to the door with flour and potatoes, the butcher with meat and lard, the poultry-grower with chickens and eggs, the dairyman with butter and cheese and milk, and so on. Each asked, not "How much of this can you afford to-day, ma'am?" but rather "How much of this do you need?" Whenever the children required new shoes or the old ones mended, they were sent to the shoemaker. Or, in case they needed a new frock or a pair of overalls, they went to the store.

No mention was made of money. Nobody there had money, or needed it. All the men and boys got their pay in "credits." What they bought went down on the debit side of their account, and what they earned on the credit side. At the end of the year the books were balanced, and the men knew where they stood.

Every able-bodied man worked. He had to or be expelled from the Order. The idler was not to eat the bread nor wear the clothes of the laborer. That was the law. If any one played sick under suspicious circumstances, he was waited upon by a committee, who looked into his case. If the committee's suspicions were well founded, he went back to work; if not, he was taken care of and fared as well as if he were at work.

Boys, too, had to work, if they were able, and also young men who were not yet "heads of families." During the summer months, when there was no school, the boys weeded the gardens, the sidewalks, and the streets, and kept the town looking spick and span. They worked under a supervisor, usually an aged man who could do nothing else, and received "credits" against the time when they should themselves be married. The girls helped their mothers with the housework.

As soon as the boy got to the stage when his voice was fully changed, he was expected to do two things: To select a vocation and to look around for a wife. Having first trained at a job and then secured the consent of his "girl," he took her to St. George (a settlement still farther south, in a warmer climate) and there they were married in "The Temple"—the most sacred place to the Mormon. Generally two or more couples went together, with a load of articles produced in Glenwood to be exchanged for articles produced in St. George.

When the couple returned home, they found a house to live in, built in their absence on a lot they had selected, and furnished with home-made tables, chairs, and other necessary things. After that the farmer, the butcher, the dairyman, and the rest had another customer, and the bookkeeper at the store another balance-sheet to draw up.

On Sundays everybody went to the church—Sunday school in the morning and a religious service in the evening. And once a week men, women, and children went to the village dance, where only "square" dances were allowed.

If Glenwood were to-day under the Order, it is quite probable that the town would have a laundry and a bakery, and that every home-owner would have a telephone, a radio, and an automobile—every one or no one.

Thus through the "Word of Wisdom" and the "United Order" the Mormon prophet took care of the primary human necessity—the food urge. We shall see, in later chapters, just how he provided for the second greatest human instinct—the sex urge. Instead of ignoring the human body with its physical needs or of deploring its existence, after the manner of his times, he frankly recognized it and tried to shift attention from negations concerning it to some positive fundamentals looking toward the betterment of conditions under which man had lived time out of mind.

With what success his economic plans would have worked under different conditions than those under which he lived, or whether these plans would succeed in our present industrialism and close knit civilization—all this is a matter for conjecture.

Certain it is, however, that Joseph Smith's followers of to-day, now thoroughly entangled in the competitive system, are still converted to the principles of the United Order, and hope to see it in operation some day—though in what way it is to come about they do not say.

When the Mormon prophet, in his "Views of the Policy and Government" of the nation, invited Texas, Mexico, Canada, and all the world to "come, let us be brethren, one great family," he meant exactly what he said.

Not only was he, in that phrase, re-asserting an age-old longing for a world-union, where swords would be beaten to plowshares and spears to pruning-hooks, but he was expressing his own dream of the future, when "Christ will reign personally on the earth." Before he died he would give his people the bracing stimulus of a Golden Age to come, an Era of peace to which they might look forward, and for which they might work in hopeful anticipation.

For Joseph Smith always viewed mankind as going somewhere. Not to hell, as so many of his religious contemporaries believed, but toward an earthly heaven of universal peace and good will. Things would not be allowed to get so thoroughly out of hand as some people seemed to think. Rulers with wrong-headed ideas might go their way up to a certain point, but after that they went, even though unwillingly, in a generally forward direction. God was still in the whirlwind, as he was still the small Voice.

The sort of thing the Prophet had in mind when he spoke of the forces back of things making for righteousness, is shown in what he said of the discovery of America by Columbus, of the settlement of New England by the Pilgrims, and of the formation of the government of the United States by the Colonial leaders. It is set down in the Book of Mormon and the Doctrine and Covenants—the main text-books of the Latter-day Saints. The ideas there expressed will also serve to show his attitude toward the Constitution and the laws of the United States.

America is a choice land, "choice above all other lands" on the face of the earth. Not only is it "choice" in its physical features, its beauty, and its products, but it is especially so in the spirit, the atmosphere, that broods over it.

This spirit is, first of all, the spirit of freedom. "Whatsoever nation shall possess it," says one of the later prophets among the Nephites, speaking of America, as we now call it, "shall be free from bondage and captivity, and from all other nations under heaven." These words are supposed to have been uttered as a prophecy by Moroni, who presumably lived in the fifth century, A.D.

But the spirit of America is essentially the spirit of the Christian religion. Indeed, political liberty is dependent upon general righteousness among the peoples here. So Moroni goes on to say: "Whatsoever nation shall possess it shall serve God, or they shall be swept off when the fulness of his wrath shall come upon them." This "wrath of God," however, is not to appear till the people are "ripened in iniquity."

Moreover, America was a land of promise, not only to the peoples of the Book of Mormon, the Jaredites and the Lehites, but also to those who have come here in our own times to escape Old World tyranny. As a matter of fact both Columbus and the Pilgrim Fathers were "led" here by the divine hand. And the men who set up our government and nation were "raised up" for that purpose. Nephi the First says this, which is supposed to be a prediction uttered about six hundred years before Christ:

And it came to pass that I looked and beheld many waters; and they divided the Gentiles from the seed of my brethren.

I beheld a man among the Gentiles, who were separated from the seed of my brethren by the many waters. And the Spirit of God came down and wrought upon the man; and he went forth upon the many waters, even unto the seed of my brethren, who were in the Promised Land.

And I beheld the Spirit of God, that it wrought upon other Gentiles; and they went forth out of captivity, upon the many waters. I beheld multitudes of the Gentiles upon the Land of Promise; and the seed of my brethren were scattered before them, and smitten. The Spirit of the Lord was upon the Gentiles, that they prospered and obtained the land for their inheritance. And they did humble themselves before the Lord; and the power of the Lord was upon them.

It has always been understood among the Mormons that the "many waters" means the Atlantic Ocean; the "seed of my brethren," the American natives; the "Gentiles," the European people; the "man" whom Nephi saw in the vision, Columbus; and the "other Gentiles," the Pilgrims and those who came here afterwards.

In the sentence following the quotation just given, from the Book of Mormon, Nephi adds these words, which are generally believed by the Saints to refer to the American Revolution:

And I beheld that their mother Gentiles were gathered together upon the waters, and upon the land also, to battle against them.

And the power of God was with them, and the wrath of God was upon all those that were gathered against them. The Gentiles that had gone out of captivity were delivered by the power of God out of the hands of all other nations. And they did prosper in the land.

Not only were the discovery of the New World by Columbus, the occupation of it by the Europeans, and the winning of political independence by the colonists, accomplished under divine guidance, but the men who formed the government of the United States, in the thought of Joseph Smith, were divinely inspired to do that work.

"It is not right that any man should be in bondage," says the Prophet, speaking in the name of the Lord, as was his wont. "For this purpose I established the Constitution of this land by the hands of wise men, whom I raised up unto this very purpose, and redeemed the land by the shedding of blood." And this "Constitution of the people," the Mormon leader adds, "should be maintained for the rights and protection of all flesh, according to just and holy principles."

At the trial of the Prophet for treason, in Liberty, Missouri, in 1838, the court inquired with eager curiosity into his belief in Daniel's prophecy of a kingdom to be set up in "the last days" on the earth. It may be remembered that part of the vision runs thus: "In the days of these kings shall the God of heaven set up a kingdom, which shall break in pieces all other kingdoms, and shall stand forever. And the kingdom and the greatness of the kingdom shall be given to the Saints of the Most High."

After the passage was read, the judge—one Austin A. King, a Methodist—asked the Prophet: "Do you believe that?"

"I do, your honor," was the answer.

"Write that down," shouted the magistrate to the clerk, "it's a strong point for treason."

Whether or not a belief in that particular passage was a "strong point for treason," might well be a matter of debate, as the lawyer for the defense suggested at the time. But there can be no doubt that Joseph Smith accepted the words of Daniel as a prediction that was then in course of fulfillment and that the work which he, Joseph Smith, was doing in the world looked directly toward the setting up of that "kingdom."

It was not, however, to "break to pieces" all other human governments in such a manner as to provoke the charge of treason. If those "other kingdoms" were to be destroyed at all, it would not be through bloody revolutions or aggressive wars, but rather through the attrition of ideas. The idea of the group would gradually be substituted for the prevailing idea of the crowd. For there were much more subtle and effective ways of producing radical alterations in government than by violence.

That small group of twelve hundred persons in Jackson county, Missouri, formed the nucleus of the world-group. It had been established on the right idea. Those few Mormons, gathered from everywhere, were a group, not merely because they happened to be in the same county. The older citizens, too, lived in the same county as they did. But they were as completely outside the group as if they lived in Russia. Such a group as the Prophet contemplated had far more binding principles than contiguity or political control. The underlying idea of a true group-life was a subordination of all thoughts of self in thoughts of the group. One for all, and all for one was the guiding principle.

As the Prophet's views broadened, they changed. At first "Zion" was that little parcel of land around Independence, with the suggestion that it might be extended as the wealth of the small group of Mormons there increased. But in 1844 he said, "The whole of America is Zion, from north to south." And it was his intention to organize "stakes" wherever churches or branches were established, especially in such "great cities as Boston and New York." "Zion" in Jackson county, however, was always to be "the center stake."

With these views of the religious unity of the Americas, it was only natural for him to say, as he did in that political document, "Come, Texas; come, Mexico; come, Canada; and come, all the world: let us be brethren, let us be one great family, and let there be universal peace."

Indeed, there is reason to believe that the breaking up of the "kingdoms" mentioned by Daniel was contemplated by Joseph Smith, and in a rather unique fashion. That "parallelism" in Isaiah about the "law" going out from Zion and the "word of the Lord" from Jerusalem, was not a parallelism to him at all. The ancient prophet had two places in mind, not one place, and two things back of those two phrases. Jerusalem, of course, was on the eastern hemisphere. Out of Jerusalem should come (has come, as a matter of fact) the religious ideals that have overspread the world. But "Zion" is America, particularly the United States, and from Zion (America) should issue the political doctrine of democracy that would sweep away the kingdoms. How the modern prophet (and the ancient prophet, too, for that matter) would delight in what has happened to the political world, especially since the Great War!

Joseph Smith looked upon the movement he established as part of the larger movement involved in the vision of Daniel. The church was "the stone" of the dream "cut out of the mountain without hands," and it would roll down upon the image and smash its toes to pieces. But this would be done by the power of ideas, not by the power of the sword. If that little group of Mormons in Jackson county could learn to live together in the ideal group-life, so could the entire state of Missouri, and the United States, and Texas, and Mexico, and Canada, and the whole world.

The Mormon prophet and his followers, as is well known, had their troubles in Missouri. Also they had some difficulties in Illinois. But neither of these disturbances was due to defects in the Federal and State constitutions, nor to the laws that had been enacted under them. Rather they were due to defects in the character of the men who had been elected or appointed to carry out the provisions of the constitutions and the laws.

No one knew this better than the Prophet. While he was not a lawyer, either in training or practice, he was a close student of the law. Daniel H. Wells, one of the ablest men that ever came out of Illinois, used to say that of all the men he had known, Joseph Smith had the surest grasp of fundamental law. The truth is, as elsewhere stated, the Prophet had studied law under Doniphan and Atchison, two of the best lawyers in Missouri. And the fact is, too, that the very men who drove the Mormons out of Jackson county, as we have seen, admitted in that "secret constitution" that the laws were "inadequate" for newcomers. And the charge against the Prophet and Lyman Wight, just before the expulsion from Caldwell and Daviess counties, was dismissed by an unfriendly court for lack of evidence.

The "secret constitution," which bound its signers to expel the Mormons from Jackson county, bore the signatures of several county officials—men who had sworn to uphold the law. Lieutenant-governor Boggs stood on the sidelines while the Saints were being maltreated by the mob, and made a flippant remark on the occasion. Governor Dunklin, while admitting that the Mormons had been unjustly deprived of their rights, did nothing in the situation. Later Governor Boggs issued an order to expel or exterminate the obnoxious followers of Joseph Smith from the state—one of the high-handed things in executive history. And, to cap the climax, the state legislature pigeonholed a committee report on the whole affair, although it appropriated the sum of two hundred thousand dollars to pay for the job of driving the Mormons from their homes. These are undisputed facts.

In Illinois the politicians toadied to the Mormon leader before every election to get his favor. Some of the ablest lawyers in the state encouraged the Prophet to believe that the Nauvoo charter gave powers which it really did not—at least, powers that were never intended to be given. Said Governor Ford just after his election, "I know that you have been told by lawyers, for the purpose of gaining your favor, that you have power to any extent. In this they have deceived you for their own purpose."

The cowardice of Governor Dunklin, the savage cruelty of Governor Boggs, the worse than indifference of other officials, the "insolence" of President Van Buren, and the obsequiousness of local politicians—all this angered and disgusted the head of the church to a point where he said some very caustic things about the lack of plain honesty and courage of those who had been placed in power to safeguard human rights and property.

No one had greater contempt than Joseph Smith for the "windy words and vaporous imaginings," as he expressed it, of the "modern politician," whose main qualification was a "peculiar tact" in handling constituents. We have seen how he fared with the men in the national capital, both directly in his own contacts with them and indirectly through the contacts of Orson Hyde. And Cyrus Walker, when he took the Prophet's case in Illinois, had at least one eye on the voters in Nauvoo.

Nor was the Mormon leader at all backward in revealing his contemptuous thoughts. His answer to Henry Clay is typical. Clay, who was a candidate for the presidency, had written about his "lively interest" in the experiences of the Mormons. Clay's letter, the Prophet thought, was "soft to flatter, rather than solid to feed." Moreover, it resembled "a lottery vender's sign, with the goddess of good luck sitting on the ear of fortune, a straddle of the horn of plenty and driving the merry steeds of beatitude without reins or bridle." For the Kentucky senator's propensity for conciliation the Prophet had this biting phrase: "True greatness never wavers; but when the Missouri Compromise was entered into by you, for the benefit of slavery, there was a shrinkage of Western honor."

A later generation of Mormons were somewhat abashed by this strong language of their leader's. But they need not have been. Joseph Smith was not the only man that thought that of the Missouri Compromise. Besides, there was nothing of the diplomat about the Mormon prophet. Forthrightness was a main trait in him. He was no compromiser. No man had his respect, however great in seeming, whose practice did not square with his pretensions. Furthermore, that was a day when everybody spoke out—everybody, that is, except the politician. But then, our own ears, during a political campaign, have been assailed by language quite as strong as Joseph Smith's.

This public hypocrisy came about from a self-consciousness in the politician, instead of from a social consciousness. There lay the seat of the whole trouble. The public man was thinking of himself all the time, rather than of the common good. And the only way to get rid of the difficulty was by harsh measures. The "miserable sycophant" who did not keep his preÃ«lection promise, by which alone he got into office, must be "hurled" from his high place, "to eat grass with the beasts." This was a sort of recall and referendum—and a rough one, at that.

And it must be admitted that Joseph Smith followed his own counsel at this particular. In May, 1844, when his candidacy for the presidency was in full swing east and west, a committee of seven men, representing the "National Reform Association" of New York, wrote him for his views on wages and land monopolies. He answered with his usual frankness. As soon as the problems of slavery, speculation, economy, and the "spiritual wickedness in ermine and lace" that could "swallow up forty or fifty millions of public revenue" was got out of the way under his administration, he would "use all honorable means to bring the wages of mechanics and farmers up, and the salaries of public officials down, increase money and labor by a judicious tariff, and advise the people—who were the only sovereigns of the soil—to petition Congress to pass a uniform law, that the air, the water, and the land might be free to free men."

As a public official he always subordinated his own interests to those of his constituents. Indeed, he had no interest apart from the interest of the whole community. "We are republicans," he said once in a public speech in Nauvoo, "and wish to have the people rule. The laws and ordinances are enacted by the city council on petition of the people, and they can be repealed, if they wish it, and petition accordingly. I am instructed by the city council to tell this people that if there is any law passed by us which you dislike, we will repeal it, for we are your servants."

And he lived up also to his ideals of low salaries for public servants. Like Benjamin Franklin, the mayor of Nauvoo believed that the honor of a public trust should be the main incentive to holding an office. At least, high salaries should not be held out as a bait, for only inferior persons, in the main, would be caught thereby. That is one reason he had for wishing to decrease the salaries of members of Congress and of other Federal office-holders.

He was councilman in Nauvoo from the last of February, 1841, to the middle of May, 1843—a period of a little more than fourteen months. For this service he received less than twenty-five dollars in all. From the last date till his death he was mayor of Nauvoo—something more than a year. Just what he received in that office, we do not know; but it could not have been very much, for, with his income from all sources, he could hardly manage to live.

Joseph Smith's ideal of political service was one with his ideal of religious service. Money was always the last thing to be taken into account, and public welfare the first.

Taxes, economy, slavery, the money question, the penal code, state's rights—these were the governmental problems uppermost in 1844; and, with the exception of slavery, they are still among the most important questions in the public mind.

Although not technically an abolitionist, Joseph Smith was for doing away with slavery. "Break the shackles from the poor black man," he exclaims in his Views of the Powers and Policy of the Government, "and hire him to labor like other human beings; for 'an hour of virtuous liberty on earth is worth a whole eternity of bondage.' " The slave states should, first of all, petition their legislatures "to abolish slavery by 1850, or now," so as to "save the abolitionist from reproach and ruin, infamy and shame"; and, secondly, to petition "Congress to pay every man a reasonable price for his slaves." This money was to come "out of the surplus revenue arising from the sale of public lands and from the deduction of pay from the members of Congress." Josiah Quincy thought this a statesmanlike solution of the question.

Economy and low taxes should be the watchword, nationally and locally. Federal officers received too much for their services, and there were too many of them. Both the number and the salaries should be forthwith reduced. Besides, the Prophet did not like the style in which the government employees lived in Washington. Fewer representatives, lower salaries, plain living—this would require less money from the people. The expenditure by the government of between forty and fifty millions looked to Joseph Smith like extravagance.

And then, too, something should be done about the public finance. Currency, gold, and silver were the life-blood of the nation. Since the Federal government had reserved to itself the prerogative of making money, why should it not also take over the exclusive task of distributing that money? Hence the Mormon leader came out for a Federal banking system, for which he claimed the credit, in the form he suggested. There should be a national bank in each state and territory, "for the accommodation of the people," and a central bank at Washington. All the capital stock in the central bank should be held by the nation, and that in the branches by the states and territories. The officers and directors, with wages "at the rate of two dollars a day for their services," should be elected by the people yearly. No bank was to issue "any more bills than the amount of capital in her vaults, and interest." The "net gain of the central bank was to be applied to the national revenue, and that of the branches to the states' and territories' revenues." In addition, the bills were to be at par throughout the nation, which would "mercifully cure that fatal disorder known in cities as brokerage, and leave the people's money in their own pockets."

As for the penal system, it was all wrong. This is still the belief in some quarters. Not punishment, but reform, should be the underlying principle in dealing with criminals. Prisons should be abolished, also penitentiaries—"except in certain cases." Imprisonment for debt was "a meaner practice than the savage tolerates, with all his ferocity." When state legislators made laws for larceny, burglary, or felony, they should "make the penalty applicable to work upon roads, public works, or any place where the culprit can be taught more wisdom and more virtue, and become more enlightened. Rigor and seclusion will never do as much to reform the propensities of men as reason and friendship. Murder only can claim confinement or death. Let the penitentiaries be turned into seminaries of learning, where intelligence, like the angels of heaven, would banish such fragments of barbarism."

Similarly the Prophet would "abolish the practice in the army and navy of trying men by court-martial for desertion. If a soldier or a marine runs away, "send him his wages, with this instruction: That his country will never trust him again; he has forfeited his honor. Make honor the standard with all men."

On the question of state's rights the Prophet dipped into his Missouri experiences. He came out for greater centralization of power. He said: "Give the president full power to send an army to suppress mobs, and the states authority to repel and impugn that relic of folly which makes it necessary for the governor of a state to make demand of the president for troops, in case of invasion or rebellion. The governor himself may be a mobber; and instead of being punished, as he should be, for murder or treason, he may destroy the very lives, rights and property he should protect."

Finally he would take measures to bring about great equality between the town and the country, so as to reduce social distinctions; "more honesty and familiarities in societies," which would mean "less hypocrisy and flattery" and more frankness and candor in "all men"; and he would get rid of "Democracy, Whiggery, and cliquery" in the nation, which do little else than "foment divisions" among the people. Everybody knew that "the glory of American liberty was on the wane and that calamity and confusion would sooner or later destroy the peace of the people" unless something were done with public credit, slavery, and bribery in high places.

These views are just as interesting to us to-day as to the generation of the Mormon prophet—but for a very different reason. Doubtless most, if not all, of these ideas on government were different from those generally held in his time, and some of them were ahead of his time. But they are interesting to us nowadays chiefly because they throw the spotlight on the Prophet himself.

Here again we see his boldness, his unconventionalism, his forthrightness, and the native vigor of his mind. But most of all we observe his firm grasp of the deep fundamentals and his power of selecting and eliminating details in order to reach an end.

Human welfare is the only excuse for government, for political action. That which looks in any other direction is wrong. Slavery should be abolished because it was unjust to the black man; but, in doing away with it, no injustice must be permitted against the slave-owner. Private banks should not be authorized because they were not run in the public interest, but for private gain. The penal code should be displaced by a system that would return the offender to society in self-respect. And so on. The key to every action was to be found in the common good.

Furthermore, here is the method of persuasion, not of force—another fundamental of Joseph Smith's. Nothing was ever gained by compulsion. All human growth came from within, not from without. What was won through coercion was not really won, but lost in the long run. This was true of slaves, prisoners, soldiers and marines. Was this method too impractical for the nineteenth century? Joseph Smith believed most profoundly in the power of ideas, of reason, of sympathy in human redemption.

Suppose these views had been adopted eighty-eight years ago. Would there have been a civil war over slavery? Could the money power have secured the hold it has on the nation? Would there now be a land monopoly? Would there have been any more headway made in America toward the ideal of the common good than there has been? It is an interesting speculation.

Utah's "best crop" is said to be, not wheat, nor apples, nor sugar-beets, nor any of the precious metals, but—children.

The phrase, which is still current, came to be a dubious compliment on the lips of the outsider with ultra-modern notions about sex. But with the Mormon, whether in Utah or elsewhere, it was looked upon as a badge of honor. This was because it embodies a new domestic order "revealed" by Joseph Smith.

One of the living apostles of the Mormon church takes pride in the fact that he is the father of fifteen children by one wife—who is still very much alive. Another of the apostles, one of the younger members of the quorum, is the father of eleven children by one wife. In one of the country towns in Utah up to four years ago was a couple whose direct descendants numbered four hundred and eleven persons. And the sixth president of the Mormon church was the father of forty-two children—by five wives, however. He was a polygamist.

The truth is that the home is central in the religion of Joseph Smith. This may be surprising to those who have thought of Mormonism as synonymous with sexual indulgence. The Mormon prophet had those "positive and unequivocal" views of the home, which Professor Ellwood demands in "a religion adapted to the needs of modern life." And his ideas on the subject (always excepting polygamy) are the more striking, coming, as they did, when the best ideals of the home were on the point of becoming thoroughly paganized—as they admittedly are at present.

Here again we come upon that deeply fundamental fact in the religious philosophy of Joseph Smith—the sacredness of the human personality. We have seen how he thought of the economic and the political as subordinate in their purpose to the development of the individual. The home, which is basic in Mormonism, also takes a secondary place, as respects the ultimate good of the individual members of the family circle. We shall come once more to this idea when we consider the distinctively religious aspects of life as seen by the Mormon leader.

The purpose of sex, in the religious scheme of Joseph Smith, was reproduction. Doubtless there was an intimate companionship to be found in the marriage relation, but the biological was its principal function. That lay at the heart of all the associations of the sexes. Sex was, therefore, not an end in itself, but a means to an end. This at least was the theory.

Repression was not a part of Mormonism. It has never been. The Prophet did not contemplate celibacy in either man or woman. The idea was abhorrent to him, in spite of what the apostle Paul says on the subject. True happiness was in the home—not only in this world, but in the next world, too. Children were the "heritage of the Lord" in the religion of Joseph Smith as in that of Moses and David.

"To Marcellus Bates," said the Prophet at a funeral, where he "preached for two hours," according to his secretary, "let me administer comfort. You shall have the company of your companion in the world of glory." In the "resurrection morning," which was something to look forward to, the Mormon leader expected "to take his relatives and friends by the hand," and embrace them. Father, son, mother, daughter, brother, sister, wife, children—these all would be as dear in the Hereafter as here.

Not repression, but restraint was the keynote in Mormonism—restraint before marriage, and restraint afterwards.

All sexual intercourse outside the marriage relation was sinful. (Polygamy, although not legal, was within the marriage relation.) Unchastity was second on the list of sins—murder being the first. And this was applied to the man, as well as to the woman. For Mormonism has never recognized the double standard of morality. What was good for the woman was equally good for the man, so far as sex restraint was concerned. "If a man commit adultery," the Prophet said in 1843, "he cannot be saved in the celestial kingdom of God. Even if he is saved in any kingdom, it cannot be the celestial." But restraint does not end with marriage. The primary purpose of sex, which is reproduction, must always be kept in mind. Some of the later Mormon leaders, as a matter of fact, insisted that it is the husband's duty to be continent during the pregnancy of his wife. Moreover, the Mormon church has never countenanced the use of contraceptives in any form.

Early marriage has been consistently advocated by the Mormon leaders from Joseph Smith on. One of the apostles of the second generation used to proclaim from the pulpit his belief that any young man who was not married by the time he was twenty-five was a "menace to the community." That was in a day, however, when less time was spent in school and college and before the economic pressure became as great as it is at present.

As a result of these teachings on sex-restraint, on early marriages, and on the central place of the home in the community, there grew up an ideal of the family.

To this day in Mormondom the family of two or three children is not looked upon with much favor, the presumption being that the parents are "not doing their duty." Families of from eight to twelve and fourteen children in monogamous homes are still to be found in Mormon communities, especially in rural districts, and even among the younger couples. The barren woman is no better thought of among the Mormons than she was among the ancient Israelites—particularly if she is barren by choice. We shall see presently the reason for all this.

Joseph Smith made it incumbent upon parents to see that such an environment be maintained in the home as would best contribute to the development of its members. Children must be taught to work. Idleness was considered as reprehensible in children as in grown ups. Of course, they must be set to work on such tasks as they could properly do without injury to themselves. Later, in Utah, when Brigham Young established the two colleges called by his name, he announced that his ideal of an education was that every boy be taught a trade, no matter what he intended to do for a living—something he could fall back upon in case of necessity. It was the old Jewish idea. Joseph Smith was a farmer, and Young a carpenter, painter, and glazier.

One of the things the Prophet called the "inhabitants of Zion" to task about (Zion in Missouri, we mean) was that they allowed their children to "grow up in wickedness." That is, they were neglectful of their religious education and training, in which event the "sins of the children" would be "visited upon the heads of the parents." In other words, the home as intended by the founder of the faith must be dominated by the spirit of religion.

The home in which the Prophet grew to manhood was such. There were daily Bible readings, prayer, and grace at the table. That was the kind of home he wished his people to have for their children. It was the typical New England home. But, in addition, the Mormon home must be free from "contention," for contention, in the home as elsewhere, was a device of Satan to hinder spiritual growth.

And then, too, the Mormon home should foster the spirit of education. Education was necessary. No man could be saved in ignorance, and salvation came in proportion to the acquisition of knowledge. In fact, the glory of God was intelligence.

In Kirtland and in Nauvoo the Mormons had their own schools. Besides the three R's there were classes in the Bible and the Book of Mormon. For even the schools had a religious atmosphere. Oliver Cowdery was directed to write books for the Kirtland schools. When the Saints were on their westward trek, they held school whenever they stopped for any length of time. And one of the very first things they did when they had located in their new home was to establish a university—the first one in the West. As already stated, Brigham Young founded two colleges—one north and the other south of Salt Lake City. there are some seventy-five seminaries and almost as many high school buildings, where Mormon pupils may attend classes in religion. Pupils in the elementary schools belonging to the Mormon faith receive instruction in "junior seminaries," of which there are probably one thousand.

Women's status in the Mormon church is a social inheritance, chiefly from the ancient Israelites. In the Book of Mormon, a work of more than six hundred pages in the first edition, only two women are mentioned by name, and that quite incidentally. But there, however, as in the Bible, the influence of woman is conspicuous in the background.

Man, in Joseph Smith's religion, was the head of the family, just as Christ was the head of the church. That headship, however, did not imply any sort of over-lordship in the home, or the presence of any dominating spirit in the home, although it may sometimes have come to that. In the time of Brigham Young wives often brought their domestic troubles to the great pioneer, who as often called the offending husband on the carpet. One of the duties of the "block teachers" in those days was to see that husband and wife got on together, if for no other reason than for the sake of the children, who must have a proper example set before them in the home. With the exception of this headship, which, as the women very well knew, was mostly a pleasant fiction, the woman was regarded as at least the equal of the man everywhere.

In the Mormon marriage ceremony there was never an "obedience" clause. And there is not now. Away back in 1835 this was the vow: "You both mutually agree to be each other's companion, husband and wife, observing the legal rights belonging to this condition: that is, keeping yourselves wholly for each other, and from all others, during your lives." The pair having answered "Yes," the officiating elder thereupon pronounced them "husband and wife," and added his blessing and hope that they might "fulfill their covenants from henceforth and forever." Nor did the agreement come as a result of any protest on the part of the modern woman—who was not then in existence. Rather it arose from the feeling that marriage was an equal partnership.

After 1842 what is known among the Mormons as "celestial marriage" was practiced. Celestial marriage is marriage for eternity as well as for time. Under this contract the Mormons do not say "until death do ye part," for they do not look upon death as invalidating the marriage agreement—provided, of course, it has been entered into under proper conditions. Thus those family ties are kept intact in the next world which have been formed under the proper authority here. The highest "degree of glory" in the Mormon Hereafter cannot be attained by either the man or the woman in an unmarried state. We shall see in a little while just why this is.

In March, 1842, the first distinctively woman's organization in the Mormon church was created. It is believed also to have been the second organization of women in the United States. The meeting at which the society was effected is interesting as revealing the Prophet's attitude toward women in general.

Seventeen women were in the meeting. It seems they had asked for an organization. The Prophet, with apostles John Taylor and Willard Richards, was present. After ascertaining the consensus of opinion on the question of organizing, the three men withdrew, to return at a later point in the meeting. It was the women, not the president of the church, who chose the presiding officer; and it was the president of the organization, not the Prophet, who selected the counselors. The one chosen to be president was Emma Smith, Joseph's wife.

When it came to finding a name for the new society, there was a division of opinion as between the men and the women present. Apostle Taylor was for calling it a "benevolent" society, but the president and the secretary did not like the word "benevolent"; they wanted the organization to be known as the "Woman's Relief Society," for reasons which they stated. The Prophet did not like either word. In the end, however, the men yielded their opinion graciously.

The purpose of the "Relief Society," as stated by its organizer, was to "provoke the brethren to good works," to "search for objects of charity and look after their needs," and to assist in "correcting the morals, and strengthening the virtues, of the community."

In two subsequent meetings of the organization the Prophet made some characteristic remarks. Since women, as a rule, were more sensitive and refined than men, they were therefore subject to over-much zeal spiritually. This emotionalism, unless checked, might prove dangerous in bringing about religious rigidity. Also the members should "put a double watch on the tongue." One of their duties was to reform, but they must reform in such prudence as not "to destroy all around them."

There was a distinction between the sinner and his sins. This was of the utmost importance. While the Society could not look on sin "with the least degree of allowance," yet they should treat with great kindness those who had gone wrong. It was better to spare ten guilty persons than to condemn one innocent. You could not compel men and women into the kingdom of God; you could only get them there through tenderness and sympathy. The way to induce poor people to repent was to minister to their wants. One should not fret one's self because of evil-doers. God would see to that. Nothing was so calculated to lead people to forsake their sins as to take them by the hand, and to treat them with tenderness; while the opposite tended to arouse a feeling of hardness and depression of spirits.

The Woman's Relief Society is still in existence. It has a membership of more than sixty thousand, with a monthly magazine devoted to the work of the organization. It is by far the wealthiest of the minor bodies of the Mormon church, having assets approximating a million dollars. In 1931 it spent more than one hundred and sixteen thousand dollars for charitable purposes alone. Its annual dues exceed twenty-three thousand dollars. For many years, on the advice of Brigham Young, the Society had a "wheat fund," which it renewed annually till the Great War, when it was sold to the Federal government at a nominal figure.

On a certain starlit night in 1843 two men and a woman might have been seen walking out of Mulholland Street in Nauvoo toward the river bank. They were Joseph Smith, the prophet, Dimmick Huntington, and Prescindia Huntington, Dimmick's sister, who was thirty-three years old. When they had reached the water's edge, they sat down on a log.

Now, the purpose of this meeting was extraordinary, to say the least. Joseph Smith, a married man according to the law of the land, there and then proposed marriage to Prescindia Huntington, a single woman.

What else he said to her on this occasion, we do not know—except that he asked her to "think it over" and, after she had made up her mind, to let him know her decision. At a second meeting a few nights later, on the same spot and under the same conditions, Prescindia accepted the Mormon leader as her husband, and her brother, who had previously been given the necessary authority by the Prophet, thereupon performed the ceremony that made them husband and wife under "the law of the Lord."

After the death of Joseph Smith, Prescindia Huntington-Smith was married under the same law to Heber C. Kimball, by whom she had a son.

"Mother," asked this son, when he became a full-grown man and his mother was in her last illness, "if you had your life to live over again, would you do as you have done—I mean live in polygamy?"

"I would that," she answered promptly and emphatically, and added: "The principle of plural marriage is true, and, if properly lived, would redeem woman from slavery and put her on a higher plane than she has ever occupied before. There would be no prostitution in the world, and every normal woman would have a husband and children."

That is almost the whole story of Mormon polygamy, as it was practiced in Nauvoo and Utah. There are some interesting details, however.

It was undoubtedly a social reason primarily that gave rise to polygamy among the Mormon people. One cannot read the sermons and writings on the subject by church leaders, from Joseph Smith down, and come to any other conclusion. And these clear-sighted and earnest men attacked the social problem with a courage and boldness that did credit both to their hearts and to their heads.

"If all the inhabitants of the earth at the present time," said Orson Pratt in 1869, "were righteous before God, keeping his commandments, and if, further, the numbers of the sexes were exactly equal, there would be no necessity for any such institution as polygamy. Every righteous man could have his wife, and there would be no surplus of females." That was an enormous concession.

But the professor-apostle went on to say that "the numbers of the sexes of marriageable age" were by no means "exactly equal." And he gave figures in proof. "If you go to the published statistics, you will find, almost without exception, that in every State a greater number of males die in the first year of their existence than females. The same holds good from one year to five years, from five years to ten, from ten to fifteen, and from fifteen to twenty." Thus in Pennsylvania there were more than seventeen thousand females between the ages of twenty and thirty in excess of males of the same age; in Massachusetts, more than thirty-three thousand; and in New York, more than forty-five thousand. These figures were quoted from the United States census reports for 1860. In 1930 the excess of females between the ages of twenty and thirty in the entire country was one hundred thirty-five thousand eight hundred eighty-five.

"What is to be done with this surplus of females?" inquired the noted philosopher. "I will tell you what Pennsylvania, Massachusetts, and New York say. They say, virtually: 'We will pass a law so strict that, if these females undertake to marry a man who has another wife, both they and the men they marry shall be subject to a term of imprisonment in the penitentiary.' Indeed! Then what are you going to do with these thousands of females of a marriageable age? 'We are going to make them either old maids or prostitutes, and we would a little rather have them prostitutes; then we men would have no need to marry.'

"This is the conclusion many of these marriageable males between twenty and thirty years of age have come to. They will not marry because the laws of the land have a tendency to make prostitutes, and they can purchase all the animal gratification they desire without being bound to any woman."

These laws, incidentally, were not Christian laws at all. They were heathen laws. "Since old pagan Rome and Greece—worshippers of idols—passed a law confining a man to one wife, there has been a great surplus of females, who have had no possible chance of getting married." The nation from which present-day Christianity, for the most part, was derived, was a polygamous nation. But when the Reformers of the sixteenth century undertook to change certain aspects of the faith, they overlooked this greater aspect of marriage. And now all the so-called Christian peoples had the notion, in spite of the widespread and deep-rooted blight of prostitution, that they had made a notable advance in culture by adopting the monogamic system of marriage, in place of the polygamic.

That, as we said, was in 1869. Even before this, however, another Pratt, the mathematician's brother Parley, had expounded what may be termed the "limitation" theory, of which the "surplus" argument by Orson was the supplement.

"A wise legislation, or the law of God," said the poet-apostle, in a remarkable little book called A Key to the Science of Theology, first published in 1855, "would punish, with just severity, the crimes of adultery and fornication, and would not suffer an idiot, the confirmed, irreclaimable drunkard, or the man of hereditary disease or vicious habits, to possess or retain a wife; while, at the same time, it would provide for a good and capable man to honorably receive and maintain more wives than one. Indeed, it should be the privilege of every virtuous female who has the requisite capacity and qualifications for matrimony, to demand, either of individuals or government, the privilege of becoming an honored and legal wife and mother, even if it were necessary for her to be married to a man who has several wives."

It remained for the apostle George Q. Cannon, a highly intelligent and widely read and traveled man to set forth a third reason for polygamy. This found its cogency in the leveling of the whole female sex.

"The practice of the world," said this alert statesman, also in 1869, "is to select a few of the sex and elevate them. There is no country in the world, probably, where women are idolized to the extent they are in the United States. But is the entire sex in the United States thus honored and respected? No; it is not. Any person who will travel, and observe while he is traveling, will find that thousands of women are degraded, and treated as something vile, and are terribly debased in consequence of the practice of men towards them.

"But the gospel of Jesus and the revelations which God has given to us concerning patriarchal marriage, have a tendency to elevate the entire sex, and give to all the privilege of being honored matrons and respected wives. There are no refuse among us—no class to be cast out, scorned, and condemned; but every woman who chooses can be an honored wife, and move in society in the enjoyment of every right which woman should enjoy to make her the equal of man, as far as she can be equal."

To the "surplus" theory of Orson Pratt, the "limitation" theory of Parley P. Pratt, and the "equalizing" theory of George Q. Cannon, must be added another, which, although it cannot be ascribed to any particular individual, somehow filtered through into the general mind of the Mormon commonwealth. It is the "character" theory. According to this idea the polygamous family, partly because it is large, is more conducive to the development of the personality than the monogamous family.

It will be remembered that the underlying principle in the religion of Joseph Smith is the preciousness of the soul—not a few souls merely but all souls. That is why the Mormon leaders, including the founder of the faith, were so deeply stirred over the problem of the underworld, and led them to reach out for a solution of it by removing the cause. Individual growth was the ultimate goal of Mormonism—a process rather than an end.

But personal development was impossible in a state of isolation. It was possible only in the group. Character was chiseled out through attrition, through human contacts, through the method of give and take. "I am like a huge, rough stone rolling down from a high mountain," the Prophet said once in Nauvoo, "and the only polishing I get is when some corner gets rubbed off by coming in contact with something else, striking with accelerated force against religious bigotry, priestcraft, lawyer-craft, doctor-craft, lying editors, suborned judges and jurors, and the authority of perjured executives, backed by mobs, blasphemers, licentious and corrupt men and women—all hell knocking off a corner here and a corner there. Thus I will become a smooth and polished shaft in the quiver of the Almighty, who will give me dominion over all and every one of them, when their refuge of lies shall fail, and their hiding place shall be destroyed." It was this knocking off of corners that brought character to perfection.

The primitive group, the family, contributed more to this "smoothing and polishing" process than any other of the social groups. This was especially so in the polygamous family.

Here the children enlarged the scope of their natural associations and made greater and more numerous contacts than in the average monogamous family—which helped them to fit themselves into the mosaic of the larger group, the community. This was true whether the families lived together or in different homes. The wife and mother, in addition to increasing the number of objects of her affection, made at least an attempt to subject her petty irritations, jealousies, what not, to the larger ends of life. And the husband and father, besides having to adjust himself to a new set of conditions, would be called upon to fight a stiff battle for an ideal of justice, fair-dealing, and suspended judgment, in the home; and this ideal, of necessity, would carry over into other and larger social contacts. No one realized better than the polygamous husband, if he tried at all to "live the principle," what Professor Foerster meant when he said: "Only people whose thoughts are entirely removed from the realities of life can fail to realize that the small and intimate circle of the family must afford to the human personality a much richer and more secure development than the best public educational institution."

Polygamy or, as the Mormons prefer to call it, "plural marriage," was first introduced among the Saints in Nauvoo, in 1841—although the Prophet had had the idea in mind ever since 1831.

Bold and courageous as Joseph Smith undoubtedly was, yet he shrank for years from venturing upon a revelation of his secret. He had been reared under the monogamic ideal; his disciples likewise; and they had been taught, and believed, that any deviation from the moral law was not only a wrong in the eyes of the law and of society, but a grave sin in the sight of God. How, then, was he to break the news to his first lieutenants and later to others of the faithful? Lorenzo Snow tells of an all-night conversation with the Prophet on the river bank (a favorite spot, it seems), concerning this very topic, and of the great anxiety of the leader as to its effects on the community. He was under no illusion as to the consequences himself—that it might eventuate in his death. It required, one gathers, more than Joseph Smith's sex-urge, however great that may have been, to fly in the teeth of the age-old convention of monogamy, especially under the circumstances.

Already he had had an inkling of the commotion, even upheaval, such a doctrine as he had in mind would stir up. At intervals, during those ten years, the charge of polygamy was often hurled at the Mormons. Most of them did not know what it was all about. The fact is that Joseph Smith had confided his secret to some intimate friends—one of the Johnsons; and the secret had become known to others, who could not keep it. There is no doubt that polygamy had a good deal to do in bringing about the Prophet's death.

In April, 1841, however, the Mormon leader launched his sensational craft, by taking to himself another wife. The woman was not Prescindia Huntington. There were several before Prescindia Huntington, and several after her—some twenty-seven in all, not counting Emma. One of these was Eliza Snow, sister of Lorenzo Snow, an intelligent woman of poetic ability. Having thus set the example, the Prophet required that the apostles enter the order—which they did after a mental struggle, we are told, and holding out on grounds of fear. By the time of the martyrdom of the Smiths it was an open secret, in Nauvoo and in Hancock county generally, that the Mormons were practicing polygamy.

More or less confusion followed. Once it became known that the principle was practiced, such men as John C. Bennett took advantage of the situation to play fast and loose erotically, without forming the ties that the Prophet had intended. They thus ignored all moral bonds. And when the head of the church publicly rebuked them for their immoralities, they fell upon him in fury.

For, as a matter of fact, the Prophet drew a very sharp distinction between a sexual relation within polygamy, as within monogamy, and a sexual act outside the polygamous bond. Plural marriage was a marriage, just as a single marriage was a marriage. The only difference was that the second had the sanction of the state, while the first had the sanction of the church only. But they were equally binding on the conscience; both proscribed sexual relations outside these ties. In neither case could any moral excursions be tolerated.

This lack of discrimination on the part of both men and women created difficulty among disciples and outsiders. Also it laid the Prophet open to misinterpretation and misunderstanding.

In 1852, after the Mormons had found a home in the West, Brigham Young made a public acknowledgment of the doctrine, and urged its practice. Doubtless among the reasons for this public announcement were: That it explained the marital relations of those who had already embarked on the practice, that it would head off prostitution, and that it would increase the Mormon population. Utah therefore presents an advanced phase of polygamy.

Here are the conditions, in general, under which the doctrine was put into effect:

To begin with, not every married man might take another wife. He had first to secure the permission of the president of the church. For only he, or some one delegated by him, could perform a plural marriage ceremony. Otherwise it was a case of adultery, and thus punishable by excommunication. Presumably the candidate for a second wife had the necessary qualifications—physical, mental, moral, spiritual, and financial. That was the theory. Polygamy was therefore limited to those who were "fit."

And then, whoever wished to marry in polygamy had next to obtain the "consent" of the wife he already had. If, however, that consent was denied, he was free to do as he pleased in the situation. Usually approval was granted—for reasons the wife probably kept to herself. Indeed, quite often the wife not only chose the second helpmeet, but carried on the preliminary negotiations. In some cases two women were taken to the altar at the same time, although of course one of them had to be "first."

As for the manner of living, sometimes there was but one home, sometimes there were as many different homes as there were wives. The separate home system was found to be more satisfactory on the whole. Staying at each home for a day or a week at a time, the husband endeavored to show as little partiality as possible—although doubtless in his heart he may have had a "favorite," as Brigham Young is said to have had.

Always, moreover, there was equality as between the wives and as between the children. No distinction was made between the first wife and the second or the fifth, nor between the children of the legal and those of the plural wife. And, as George Q. Cannon asserted, there was no class of women to be cast out on account of their station. No prostitution existed in Utah till the non-mormon took it there; and few, if any, old maids.

Polygamy died hard among the Mormons. But it is now as dead as the proverbial door nail. When Congress, in answer to a popular demand by men and women all over the United States, passed anti-polygamy laws, the Saints refused to give up their doctrine, till after the highest court had rendered a decision on their constitutionality.

For this apparent disloyalty there were two principal reasons. One was that they did not believe any law could legislate out of existence a religious belief, such as polygamy was in their view. And then the law would require the polygamists to disown women with whom they had lived and by whom they had borne children, and to bastardize the offspring of their plural wives. And this they would not do. They preferred to go to jail—as hundreds of them did. In those days the "co-hab," as he came to be called, who "promised to obey the law" against polygamy was looked upon by his own people as a renegade.

The practice of polygamy among the Mormons, however, was not effected without some opposition from the women. Emma Smith was particularly recalcitrant. William Clayton, secretary to the Prophet in 1843, and the apostle Orson Pratt, give us glimpses of her mind in the matter.

One morning, says Clayton, Joseph and Hyrum came into the office together. They were talking about plural marriage. Hyrum wanted his brother to reduce his ideas on the subject to writing, so that he might convert Mrs. Smith. After that Joseph would have "peace." Any woman, he added, could easily be convinced that the principle was true. "You don't know Emma as well as I do," Joseph commented, but sat down and dictated to his secretary. Hyrum then took the document away with him.

When he returned, Joseph asked him how he had fared. Hyrum answered, "I have never had such a talking to in my life. Emma was very resentful."

The fact is that, later, Mrs. Smith burned the original document, which she had coaxed away from her husband for the purpose. Fortunately Bishop Whitney had made a copy of it.

Emma, Pratt tells us, was "embittered against Joseph, and at times fought against him with all her heart; then again she would break down in her feelings, and humble herself, and would lead forth ladies and place their hands in the hands of her husband, and they were married to him, according to the law of God."

On the whole, however, the Mormon women accepted the principle of plural marriage in the same spirit as the men, especially those who had been taught to look at it in the larger way.

We have already seen what Prescindia Smith-Kimball thought on the subject. Helen Mar Whitney, daughter-in-law of Bishop Whitney, after she had been "a spectator and a participator in this order of matrimony for over thirty years," declared that "the system tends to preserve social purity" and that "it alone can remedy the great social evils of the present day," adding that "when lived up to as the Lord intended, it will exalt the human family." And one of Orson Pratt's eight wives says that all the twenty-five children of her husband were "endeared to her by mutual affection," and their mothers "by mutual and long-continued exercises of toil, patience, and sisterly kindness," notwithstanding they were conscious of "imperfections in this life." Eliza R. Snow-Smith, plural wife of the Prophet, says this: "Virtue is the foundation of the prosperity of any nation; and this principle of plural marriage tends to virtue, purity, and holiness."

As a matter of fact, the Mormon women of Utah were as reluctant as the men to abandon the practice of polygamy—at least those who had been inducted into the principle during the first generation. When anti-polygamy laws were about to be passed, fifteen hundred women met in Salt Lake City "to protest against the misrepresentation of the ladies engaged in anti-polygamy crusades, and to declare their sentiments on the subject." One of these sentiments was thus expressed by Mrs. Snow-Smith:

I am proud to state before this large and honorable assembly that I believe in the principle of plural marriage just as sacredly as I believe in any other institution which God has revealed. I believe it to be necessary for the redemption of the human family from the low state of corruption into which it has fallen.

And I truly believe that a Congress composed of polygamic men who are true to their wives would confer a far higher honor upon a nation, and would perform better service to this country than a Congress composed of monogamic unreliable husbands.
