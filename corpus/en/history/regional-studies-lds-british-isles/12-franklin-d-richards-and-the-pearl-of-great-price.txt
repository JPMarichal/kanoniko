Franklin D. Richards and the Pearl of Great Price

Rodney Turner

Introduction

Publication of the Pearl of Great PriceHCThe Pearl of Great Price is the latest, and the smallest, of the four Standard Works. The Old Testament was finally canonized about A.D. 100, and the New Testament in 405. Fourteen centuries passed before our modern scriptures came into being: the Book of Mormon in 1830, the Doctrine and Covenants in 1835, and the Pearl of Great Price in 1880. Unlike the New Testament-which was not canonized by the Christian church for over two hundred years after its twenty-seven books were written and compiled-the scriptural labors of the Prophet Joseph Smith spanned a fifteen-year period (1827-42), and were canonized within a fifty-year period (1830-80).

Publication of the Pearl of Great PriceHC177The Pearl of Great Price is unique in that it did not begin as a standard work or even as a publication authorized by the presiding authorities of the Church-as, for example, the Latter-day Saints Millennial Star-but instead it was the inspired action of one man, Elder Franklin D. Richards, president of the British Mission. In all likelihood, this volume of scripture, as we know it, would not exist were it not for him. The hand of the Lord is now clearly visible in that Apostle's decision to produce what, at the time, must have seemed to some to be but another, however superior, publication of the British Mission.

Publication of the Pearl of Great PriceHC178Indeed, it is to be wondered if even Elder Richards grasped the significance of that event. He appears not to have recorded it in his journal, nor did he cite the Pearl of Great Price in enumerating the things accomplished during the seventeen months of his presidency (January 1851-May 1852): greatly expanding the circulation of the Millennial Star, revising the hymnal, operating the Perpetual Emigration Fund, and publishing a new edition of the Book of Mormon. [^1]

Franklin Dewey Richards

Franklin D. Richards was born 2 April 1821 at Richmond, Massachusetts to Wealthy Dewey and Phinehas Richards, a cousin of Brigham Young. His brother was Samuel W. Richards (who also served as president of the British Mission), and his son was George F. Richards. Franklin was baptized at the age of 17 on 3 June 1838 and confirmed the following day. He obtained his endowments in the Nauvoo Temple. He later also obtained the fullness of the priesthood and was sealed to eleven wives, by whom he had twenty-eight children. At the age of 27, on 12 February 1849, he was ordained an Apostle and set apart as a member of the quorum of the Twelve by Heber C. Kimball.

178Before serving as president of the British Mission four times and editor of the Millennial Star between the years 1846 and 1868, [^2] Elder Richards served five missions in the United States.

Accompanied by his brother Samuel, Elder Richards first arrived in Liverpool on 14 October 1846 and was immediately called to preside over the Scottish Conference. Three months later (January 1847) he was appointed president of the British Mission by the departing Orson Hyde. However, he held that position only two weeks, because the call was based on a false report of the death of Orson Spencer, who had been chosen to succeed Hyde by Church authorities in Salt Lake City. Upon Spencer's arrival, Richards became his counselor in the presidency. There were over twelve thousand Saints in Great Britain at that time. [^3]

179At the October 1849 conference of the Church, he was called to return to England. He arrived 29 march 1850 and served as acting president of the British Mission during Elder Orson Pratt's absence in the United States. Following Pratt's return, Richards became Pratt's counselor until the close of 1850.

179Elder Richards' first extensive term as president of the mission began 1 January 1851 and ended with his release 8 May 1852, when he was succeeded by his brother Samuel. Elder Richards accomplished a prodigious amount of work during his administration, including the printing of a variety of tracts by the tens of thousands.

179He became mission president for the third time on 8 June 1854 and served with typical vigor until his release 13 July 1856. By 1866 some felt that the British Mission had passed its zenith and was in decline. The arrival of Elder Richards in June 1867 for his fourth and final foreign tour of duty led to its revitalization. Greeting him on his return 1 October 1868, President Brigham Young said: "Welcome home! I am glad to see you. I congratulate you on your revival of the British Mission." [^4]

179Elder Richards concluded his life of service by assisting, and then serving, as Church Historian from 7 April 1889 until his death at the age of seventy-eight on 9 December 1899.

Publication of the Pearl of Great Price

Publication of the Pearl of Great PriceHCThe Millennial Star-which had been established 15 April 1840 at a General Conference of the Church in Preston, Lancashire, England by Brigham Young and the Council of the Twelve-began publication in Liverpool in May 1840. Parley P. Pratt was appointed its first editor. As of 20 April 1841, the president of the mission also served as editor of the Star and supervisor of mission publications. This broad authority may have been a factor in Elder Richards' decision and ability to publish the Pearl of Great Price.

Publication of the Pearl of Great PriceHC181It is clear from this letter that the publication of the Pearl of Great Price was, as before stated, an independent decision on the part of Elder Richards; he was not authorized to do so by Church authorities. Although in his Preface to the Pearl of Great Price he wrote that he had "been induced by the repeated solicitation of several friends" to publish the "very important articles contained therein," it is also clear that he was motivated by a much more compelling purpose: "for the use of the Elders and Saints to arm and better qualify them for their service in our great war." Although Church leadership was centered in Utah, of the approximately forty-two thousand Saints living in Utah and Great Britain in 1850, almost thirty-one thousand (73 percent) lived in Great Britain. The British Saints had a need Elder Richards intended to satisfy.

Publication of the Pearl of Great PriceHC181The letter further shows that he had been contemplating the project for some time-probably beginning in 1850-and had given much thought to its form and content. Levi Richards undoubtedly responded to the request for suggestions, and may have influenced the final character of the publication, for it was prepared in the first six months of 1851.

Publication of the Pearl of Great PriceHC181Comparison of his initial plan as outlined in the letter with the final form of the project shows that Elder Richards attempted to give "the whole a general feature of chronological order." Thus he began with the prophecy of Enoch ( Moses 6:43 -7:69) followed by the vision of Moses ( Moses 1 ) and the Mosaic account of the creation, fall, and Adam's redemption, etc. However, a truly consistent chronological order-based either on the dates of the ancient writers, the overall historical sequence, or the labors of Joseph Smith-was not maintained. A chronological order was in fact followed only to the extent that the ancient materials of Moses and Abraham preceded the modern ones of Joseph Smith-including his translation of Matt. 24.

Publication of the Pearl of Great PriceHCApparently Richards thought that the Michael Chandler letter could introduce the Book of Abraham, but he wisely omitted that letter from the final compilation. In addition to D&C 77 and 87, he also added extracts from D&C 20, 27, and 107. Most important, he decided to include the vital First Vision portion of the history of Joseph Smith, together with the Articles of Faith as extracted from the "Wentworth letter."

Publication of the Pearl of Great PriceHC182The materials selected by Elder Richards-especially the prophecy of Enoch, Matt. 24, and the history of Joseph Smith-reflected the general conviction of the Saints with regard to the imminence of Christ's return and millennial reign. The members had a sense of urgency about preparing for these momentous events. The very name of the publication of the British Mission, the Millennial Star, reinforced that sense of urgency. This is evident not only from much of the content of the early Star, but even from the title pages of early volumes.

Publication of the Pearl of Great PriceHC182When issued, the Pearl of Great Price consisted of fifty-six pages rather than the initial sixty-four pages. Two points made in his letter to Levi Richards are reiterated in both the Star announcement and in the book's preface: 1) the Pearl of Great Price is not primarily "designed to pioneer our doctrines to the world," and 2) its contents will enable the Saints to expound and defend the restored gospel.

Publication of the Pearl of Great PriceHC182The Pearl of Great Price was a pamphlet, not a book, only 5 1/4 by 8 1/4 inches in size with a salmon colored paper cover. It sold for one shilling. The first American edition of 1878 also had a paper cover and sold for fifty cents.

Publication of the Pearl of Great PriceHC182The number of copies composing the first and only British edition is unknown, but Peter Crawley's research indicates that it was not less than fifty-five hundred and very likely as many as ten thousand. A fairly large number of copies in varying conditions are known to exist.

Publication of the Pearl of Great PriceHCA single Welsh edition was published by John Silvanus Davis-who had taken over publishing responsibilities from Dan Jones-in 1852. Davis also wrote and published the Beehive Songster.

Contents of First Edition

The Pearl of Great Price is a microversion of the Bible in that it contains elements of both the Old and the New Testament: the words of both the Father and the Son, together with those of such early patriarchs as Adam, Enoch, Noah, and Moses (who quotes the former, as well as the prophetess Eve) and a brief, but priceless, extract from Abraham's personal history, and finally the prophecies of Jesus concerning the fate of the Jews in the first century A.D., and the prophetic signs of his own latter-day return.

184In addition, there are the latter-day contributions of the Prophet Joseph Smith. This admixture of materials renders the Pearl of Great Price-especially in its original edition-the most diverse and least homogeneous of the four Standard Works. It was, and to a lesser extent, still is, something of a potpourri of doctrine, history, and exposition classified by Franklin D. Richards as "revelations, translations, and narrations of Joseph Smith."

Consequently, the materials the compiler selected were rich and varied, consisting of the writings of Moses and Abraham, the prophecy of Jesus (JS-M), extracts from the history of Joseph Smith covering the period 1805-1829 (JS-H), two complete revelations ( D&C 77 and 87-the latter had no prior publication) and extracts from three other revelations ( D&C 20, 27, and 107). [^10]

The Book of Moses

The eight chapters comprising "Selections from the Book of Moses" were revealed to Joseph Smith in connection with his "translation" of the Bible between June 1830 and February 1831. Between August 1832 and April 1833, W. W. Phelps, editor of the first Church newspaper, the Evening and Morning Star, published in volume one, and for the first time anywhere, the following selected extracts from the Moses material:

185 Moses 7:1-69 (entire chapter)No. 3, August 1832 Moses 6:43-68 No. 10, March 1833 Moses 5:1-16 No. 11, April 1833 Moses 8:13-30 No. 11, April 1833

185Chapter one of Moses was first published in the Times and Seasons (vol. 4, no. 5), 16 January 1843, in connection with the "History of Joseph Smith."

185Analysis shows that only 129 verses (36 percent) of the present text of Moses were published by Phelps in the Evening and Morning Star, and 263 verses (74 percent) by Elder Richards in his Pearl of Great Price. The 93 verses of Moses (4:20-21, 26-32; 5:16b, 17, 18, 23b, 24-31, 41-6:42 and 8:1-12) [^11] missing from the 1851 edition were added by Orson Pratt to the first American edition of 1878.

Franklin D. Richards also used a private copy of at least some of the book of Genesis, as translated by Joseph Smith, for a portion of the Moses material. This material was not available either in the Evening and Morning Star or the Times and Seasons. It apparently included Moses 2:1-4:19 22-25, and 5:1-16a, 19-23a, and 32-40-material never in print before 1851.

Book of Abraham

The five chapters which make up the Book of Abraham were translated by Joseph Smith from the contents of one of two main rolls of papyri obtained about 1822-23 by Antonio Lebolo. [^12] These were acquired along with eleven mummies from a pit tomb (number 33) in the valley of the nobles on the west side of the Nile, opposite ancient Thebes (modern Luxor). The intriguing story of the transportation of these Egyptian artifacts to America, and of the subsequent persons and events associated with them, is still incomplete, but thanks to the earlier research of James R. Clark and James R. Harris, and the current research of H. Donl Peterson, that story is being investigated.

186Between 1835 and 1842 the Prophet Joseph Smith labored on the papyrus material as he had labored on the reformed Egyptian material on the plates of the Book of Mormon. Yet we have but a small fraction of the writings of Abraham, and none of the writings of Joseph of Egypt. The Prophet intended to provide the Saints with additional translation material, but circumstances in the closing two years of his life precluded his doing this.

186Elder Richards used the five extant chapters of Abraham, together with their three facsimiles, as published in the Times and Seasons March 1, 15 and May 16 1842. [^13]

Matt. 24

At the close of his ministry, Jesus turned for the first time to the subject of things to come. His prophecies concerning the fate of the Jews and the signs of his latter-day return, as found in Joseph Smith's 1831 revision of Matt. 24, had been published as a broadside, either in Kirtland about 1835 or in Nauvoo in 1842 or 1843. The former is thought to be more likely. Of JST Matt. 24, Peter L. Crawley has written:

187Two manuscripts containing this text are in the possession of the RLDS Church: the original manuscript (NT 1), and a copy made by John Whitmer in the summer of 1831 with later corrections by Joseph Smith (NT 2).

187[[The Pearl of Great Price] mainly follows NT 1 but embodies three significant modifications written into NT 2. [^14]

187While Elder Richards may or may not have had a copy of this broadside, he either had a copy of the Whitmer material (NT 2) or an independent copy which reflected the Prophet's own emendations of NT 1.

187The inclusion of the Prophet's revision of Matt. 24 in the Pearl of Great Price is, as suggested, probably a reflection of the keen interest in prophecy, especially pertaining to the Lord's coming, that characterized the Saints at that time.

History of Joseph Smith

The narrative of the early years of Joseph Smith (JS-H)-which begins with his birth in 1805 and ends with the coming of John the Baptist in May 1829-was begun in 1838. [^15]

Articles of Faith

The Times and Seasons was the original printed source of the "Articles of Faith." These thirteen statements of belief appeared at the end of a brief history of the Church prepared by Joseph Smith at the request of John Wentworth, owner and editor of a newspaper, the Chicago Democrat. Apparently Wentworth never published the Prophet's letter, but it was reproduced elsewhere. It was published first in the Times and Seasons (vol. 3, no. 9, pp. 706-10) on 1 March 1842 by Joseph Smith. [^16]

188The Prophet's succinct emphasis on distinguishing tenets of the Restored Church obviously impressed Elder Richards, who was very mindful of the influx of new, uninformed members and of the need to provide them with a common understanding of those tenets. The Articles of Faith have served that need ever since. Interestingly, it was Elder Richards who was privileged in General Conference on 6 October 1890 to propose that the Articles of Faith, as such, be accepted by the Church as a rule of faith and conduct for the Latter-day Saints. Thus accepted, they are now scripture. [^17]

Publication of the Pearl of Great PriceHCTruth

Publication of the Pearl of Great PriceHCElder Richards ended the Pearl of Great Price with his testimony in the form of a poem entitled "Truth," written by John Jaques (pronounced Jacks), an English convert of 1845. It was later published in the Millennial Star (12:240) of August 1850 and thereafter appeared in the eleventh edition of the Hymnal of 1856. Now known as "Oh Say, What is Truth?" it is-with all due respect to William Shakespeare-probably the most inspired and significant work ever written in Stratford-on-Avon. [^18]

Publication of the Pearl of Great PriceHCConclusion

Publication of the Pearl of Great PriceHCA memorial was presented to the departing Apostle Franklin D. Richards by the presidents of the several conferences of the Church in Great Britain on 6 April 1852. In it they wrote: "When we ponder over the pages of the `Pearl of Great Price,' we are, and ever shall be, inspired with admiration and love, intense and deep, towards you, whose illuminated mind has concentrated that peerless pearl of august intelligence." [^19] Those who know and appreciate the priceless contents of that sacred volume of scripture echo similar sentiments today.
