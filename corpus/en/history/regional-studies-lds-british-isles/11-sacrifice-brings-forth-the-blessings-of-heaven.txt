Various authors, Regional Studies in LDS History: British Isles ( 1990)

Sacrifice Brings Forth the Blessings of Heaven

E. Dale LeBaron

Introduction

"A religion that does not require the sacrifice of all things never has power sufficient to produce the faith necessary unto life and salvation." [^1]

151So taught the Prophet Joseph Smith. Sacrifice has been a salient teaching and practice of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints since the Church's inception. This has been particularly evident in missionary work, explained President Ezra Taft Benson:

151In fulfillment of the all-important, Godgiven mandate, this great work has gone forward through the years unabated. It is a dramatic chapter in the history of a "peculiar people." But in all the annals of Christendom there is no greater evidence of courage, willingness to sacrifice, and unbounded devotion to duty. Men, women, and children all have joined in this heroic effort with no hope of material reward. These ambassadors of the Lord Jesus Christ, as they firmly believe themselves to be, have trudged through mud and snow, swum rivers, and gone without the common necessities of food, shelter, and clothing in response to a call. [^2]

The fourth verse of the hymn "Praise to the Man" begins with the single truth "Sacrifice brings forth the blessings of heaven." Clearly there is a direct relationship between some blessings in our lives, and the sacrifices we make when called upon. "I [am] persuaded of one great truth. Whenever the Lord has a great blessing for one of his children, he puts that son or daughter in the way to make a great sacrifice" states Elder Harold B. Lee. [^3]

152The introduction of the gospel to the British Isles could never have been accomplished without great sacrifices; innumerable and significant blessings resulted from these labors. In the first two decades of the Church, it seems there were three major thrusts of missionary work which involved members of the Quorum of the Twelve as well as other missionaries. Each thrust focused on Great Britain. Each came at a time of great trial and hardship within the Church. Each required great sacrifice of its participants. Together they laid the foundation for a worldwide missionary movement which has become the life blood of the Church.

152The first of these major missionary thrusts was in 1837 when the gospel was first taken to the British Isles, the first mission field outside North America. The second came in 1839-41 when the Quorum of Twelve Apostles were directed to go to England; nine apostles were in England at the same time. The third significant thrust was a more prolonged effort, from 1846-53, a continual missionary service from members of the Twelve and other brethren, which eventually spread from Great Britain to many other nations. Each thrust coincides with a period of great crisis and hardship for the Church generally, and for the Prophet specifically.

The First Mission to England

When conditions seemed most desperate, the Lord required more sacrifice from his servants. The sacrifices were personally heavy, and even the strongest were tested; a great sifting took place. But the resultant blessings permeated the Church and continue to bless us today.

When Elder Heber C. Kimball and the other brethren were sent to open the work in Great Britain, an economic depression and the collapse of the Kirtland Safety Society had affected Church membership. The fact that many of the Saints in the Kirtland area blamed their financial losses on the Prophet created disunity, dissension and apostasy among previously faithful and loyal members and even some apostles.

153Though the Prophet was burdened, more was required. Heber, one of Joseph's most trusted brothers, was sent to England with Apostle Orson Hyde, Willard Richards (who would be ordained to the Apostleship while on this mission), and four other brethren from Canada. Why did these strong brethren leave at this critical time? The Prophet Joseph wrote, "In this state of thingsÂ�God revealed to me that something new must be done for the salvation of His Church." [^4]

153Heber C. Kimball's influence in the British mission began long before his mission call came. About one year before his call, he set apart Parley P. Pratt for a mission to Canada. In the blessing, Heber prophetically promised:

153Thou shalt go to Upper Canada, even to the city of Toronto, the capital, and there thou shall find a people prepared for the fullness of the gospel, and they shall receive thee, and thou shalt organize the Church among them, and it shall spread then into the regions round about, and many shall be brought to the knowledge of the truth and shall be filled with joy; and from the things growing out of this mission, shall the fullness of the gospel spread into England, and cause a great work to be done in that land (Italics added). [^5]

153Elder Kimball was the instrument in proclaiming the promised blessing; later he was an instrument in fulfilling it. Among the many converts of Parley P. Pratt were John Taylor (third President of the Church), and the four brethren who accompanied Heber to England in 1837 (James Fielding, Isaac Russell, John Snider, and John Goodson). Also baptized were the first missionaries to take the Gospel to Scotland-Samuel Mulliner and Alexander Wright. Like other Canadian converts, these brethren who had family roots in Britain desired to see the gospel taken to that land.

154Elder Heber C. Kimball received notice of his call during a Sunday meeting in the Kirtland temple, 4 June 1837, when the Prophet Joseph whispered to him, "Brother Heber, the Spirit of the Lord has whispered to me: 'Let my servant Heber go to England and proclaim my Gospel, and open the door of salvation to that nation.'" [^6] Heber had been told months before, by the Spirit of prophecy, that he would serve a mission to Europe, yet he found the call from the Prophet overpowering and overwhelming. [^7] Elder Kimball recorded:

154I felt my weakness and unworthiness, and was nearly ready to sink under the task which devolved upon me, and I could not help exclaiming: "O Lord I am a man of `stammering tongue,' and altogether unfit for such a work. [^8]

154In spite of these fears and concerns, Elder Kimball characteristically recorded:

154The moment I understood the will of my Heavenly Father, I felt a determination to go at all hazards, believing that He would support me by His almighty power, and endow me with every qualification that I needed; and although my family was dear to me, and I should have to leave them almost destitute, I felt that the cause of truth, the Gospel of Christ, outweighed every other considerationÂ�I daily went into the east room in the attic story of the temple and poured out my soul unto the Lord, asking His protection and power to fulfill honorably the mission appointed me by His servants. [^9]

154On 20 July 1837, when the small boat which carried Heber and his missionary party to English soil was six or seven feet from the pier, he leaped on shore. This approach was symbolic of his nine months of mission leadership.

For the last six months of their mission, Elder Kimball and his companions did not recall retiring to bed before midnight. From excessive work, Elder Hyde was ill for six weeks. They repeatedly spoke in small and very crowded houses, and to large assemblies in the open air. Consequently their lungs tired and their bodies fatigued. Elder Hyde wrote to Willard Richards: "We have scarcely time to sleepÂ�. We are preaching and baptizing daily. We are so thronged whenever we preach, that some faint and have to be carried outdoors." [^10] [^11]

155Because of this first pioneering missionary thrust, the Church was established in the British Isles with more than two thousand members in twenty-six branches. Elder Kimball had personally brought into the Church about one thousand five hundred souls. [^12]

Earliest Missionary EffortsHC155To Elder Kimball one of the most priceless blessings was to witness the lives of people touched and changed through the power of the Gospel of Jesus Christ. One of hundreds of such experiences was Heber's involvement with the Moon family. For some time the family rejected Elder Kimball's message, but then through earnest prayer they received a witness of the truth and were all baptized. Elder Kimball related, "Before I left England there were about thirty of that family and connections baptized, five of whomÂ�were ordained to be fellow laborers with us in the vineyard, and I left them rejoicing in the truths they had embraced." [^13]

Earliest Missionary EffortsHC156A great blessing which came to Elder Kimball from his sacrifices on this mission was a rich spiritual and emotional experience which he had before his departure from Britain. As Heber walked down the streets of Chatburn, many followed him. The doors of the houses were filled with well wishers. There were many tears. A crowd followed him for miles. "My heart was like unto theirs, and I thought my head was a fountain of tears, for I wept for several miles after I bid them adieu. I had to leave the road three times to go to streams of water to bathe my eyes." [^14]

Earliest Missionary EffortsHC156At this time Elder Kimball was constrained to remove his hat and bless the entire region. He felt it was holy ground. Years later Elder Kimball reflected on the significance of these feelings:

Earliest Missionary EffortsHC156I went through the streets of that town feeling as I never before felt in my life. My hair would rise on my head as I walked through the streets, and I did not then know what was the matter with me. I pulled off my hat, and felt that I wanted to pull off my shoes, and I did not know what to think of it. When I returned, I mentioned the circumstance to brother Joseph, who said, "Did you not understand it? That is a place where some of the old Prophets travelled and dedicated that land, and their blessing fell upon you." [^15]

156Yet another Prophet, Elder Kimball's grandson, President Spencer W. Kimball, told the British Saints: "I should like to think that the whole of this great land is blessed and still carrying a blessing from our Heavenly Father from great and holy men who have walked upon its shores." [^16]

Before to the departure of Elder Kimball and Elder Hyde for America, a general conference was held with six to seven hundred Saints. It lasted eight hours, without intermission. [^17]

157Verily I say unto my servant Heber, thou art my son, in whom I am well pleased; for thou art careful to hearken to my words, and not transgress my law, nor rebel against my servant Joseph Smith, for thou hast a respect to the words of mine anointed, even from the least to the greatest of them; therefore thy name is written in heaven, no more to be blotted out for ever, because of these thingsÂ�I will go with you, and be on your right hand and on your left and my angels shall go before you and raise you up when you are cast down and afflicted; remember that I am always with you, even to the end. [^18]

The Second Missionary Thrust to the British Isles

On 8 July 1838 the Lord gave the signal for a second major missionary thrust. The Twelve were instructed to take their leave from the Saints, departing from the temple site in Far West, "to go over the great waters, and there promulgate my gospel, the fullness thereof, and bear record of my name" ( D&C 118:4 ). By their departure date, the membership of the Church was in the most destitute condition they had yet experienced. Drivings, mobbings and persecutions in Missouri had left the Saints scattered and homeless. The Prophet Joseph and others had just been released from a lengthy imprisonment in Missouri, and they and the Church were in dire need of the leadership and strength which the Twelve could have provided. But more sacrifice was required.

Elder Wilford Woodruff observed that Satan was determined to prevent the Twelve from accomplishing this mission to England, for when the Apostles prepared to start their missions, they and their families were smitten with chills, fever, and sickness. It required tremendous faith and perseverance to start. [^19]

158Elder Woodruff and John Taylor were the first of the Twelve to leave for their missions. At the time of their departure, Elder Woodruff had been ill with chills and fever for thirteen days. His wife, Phoebe, was also suffering from the same illness. Wilford's family was living in one small room, about fourteen feet square, crowded together with the family of Orson Pratt. On 8 August Elder Woodruff arose early from his sickbed, laid his hands on his wife's head and blessed her, and then departed, leaving her almost without food and life's necessities. Yet his wife supported him. Elder Woodruff recorded in his journal: "Phoebe, farewell! Be of good cheer; remember me in your prayers. I leave these pages for your perusal when I am gone. I shall see your face again in the flesh. I go to obey the commands of Jesus Christ." [^20]

158In his weakened and sick condition, Elder Woodruff was taken across the Mississippi River by President Brigham Young. Once on the other side he lay down to rest, exhausted. The Prophet Joseph came along and looked at him, saying, "Well, Brother Woodruff, you have started upon your mission." Wilford replied that he felt more like a subject for the dissecting room than a mission. Joseph responded, "Get up, and go along, all will be right with you." With that encouragement and admonition, the first two members of the Twelve began their missionary journey.

Elder Kimball and Brigham Young followed. On 14 September, President Young left his home to start for England, so sick he needed assistance just to get to the Mississippi River, less than a mile away. After crossing, he was taken by Israel Barlow to Elder Kimball's house where he stayed, sick, for four days. President Young left his wife sick with a three-week old baby and five other sick children. They were all so sick they were unable to wait on each other. Their only clothing was what they wore, for the mob had taken everything else.

159On 18 September, the companions set out. Some brethren loaded their trunks into a wagon. Elder Kimball embraced his wife as she was lying in bed with two sick children; she herself was shaking with chills. The family's provisions would not last ten days, and there was no one able to go to the well for a pail of water. But with difficulty the elders climbed into the wagon and started down the hill. Heber felt he was leaving his family in the arms of death, and he would not be able to endure it. He asked the teamster to stop, and he said to Elder Young, "This is pretty tough, isn't it; let's rise up and give them a cheer." They arose, and swinging their hats three times over their heads they shouted, "Hurrah, hurrah for Israel." Heber's wife heard the noise and came to the door, calling back, smiling, "Goodbye. God bless you." Heber and Brigham returned the compliment and told the driver to go ahead. Heber felt joy and gratitude, having left his wife standing on her feet, instead of lying in her bed. [^21]

159The Prophet Joseph not only encouraged them to make the necessary sacrifices to leave at this time, but also gave them a prophetic blessing: "If you will go, I promise you, that your family shall live, and you shall live, and you shall know that the mind of God is in calling you to go and preach the Gospel of life and salvation to a perishing world." [^22]

159Elder George A. Smith was the last Apostle to leave for England. Describing his pathetic condition, the Prophet Joseph wrote:

[They] upset their wagon on the bank of the river, before they got out of sight of Commerce. Elders Smith and Turley were so weak they could not get up, and Brother Hedlock had to lift them in again. Soon after, some gentlemen met them and asked who had been robbing the burying-ground-so miserable was their appearance through sickness. [^23]

160Sickness continued to plague some of the brethren as they journeyed. About three weeks after leaving Nauvoo, Elder Woodruff recorded in his journal that the adversary had a powerful grasp on the life of Elder Taylor, who had failed several times. It seemed as though he would die, and they were obliged to stop. Because of his stop, Elder Taylor arrived in New York about two months after Elder Woodruff. Elder George A. Smith suffered so severely and became so emaciated from the disease that he was mistaken for an old man while he and President Young were staying at an inn. He was barely twenty-two years of age at the time. President Brigham Young also suffered greatly. By the time he reached England he was so emaciated and sickly that Willard Richards did not recognize him. They were first cousins. [^24]

160When the Apostles arrived, meetings were held, and assignments made, and the work moved forward with great power. Although there had been some apostasy since Elder Kimball's departure, the work had progressed well under the faithful leadership of Presidents Fielding, Richards and Clayton. President Young wrote to the Prophet Joseph to say that the work was progressing; eight to nine hundred had been baptized since Elder Kimball had left. [^25]

160Within the 376 days the apostles spent in Britain, they built up and strengthened the established foundation, and expanded the missionary efforts to the rest of the British Isles. Churches were established in almost every major town and city in Great Britain, with seven to eight thousand converts. Five thousand copies of the Book of Mormon were printed, as well as thousands of hymn books, tracts, and copies of the Millennial Star. One thousand souls emigrated to America. [^26]

161Elder John Taylor introduced the gospel to Ireland and the Isle of Man. Seventy were baptized in all, in spite of the combined powers of earth and hell. [^27] Among those families converted were the Cowleys, Cannons and Quayles. [^28]

161The gospel message was taken into their native Scotland by Elders Alexander Wright and Samuel Mulliner, who had been converted in Canada. They arrived in Glasgow on 20 December 1839 and left the following day for Edinburgh, where they were welcomed by Elder Mulliner's parents. It was midwinter, and Elder Wright suffered physically from nights in the open, poor food and poor covering. After walking for over one hundred miles he was treated by a druggist who diagnosed his sickness as smallpox. With this news he rested three days and then continued to his father's home in Banff. Despite his weakened condition, he proclaimed the gospel vigorously during his journey.

161Among the early converts from Scotland were Charles W. Nibley, later Presiding Bishop and counsellor in the First Presidency; and William Budge, a stake president, Mission President, Logan Temple President and missionary for over nine years, who taught and baptized more than two hundred persons, including Dr. Karl G. Maeser, founder of BYU. [^29]

161Elder Wilford Woodruff found great success in his missionary labors. He was directed by "the voice of God" to leave the Staffordshire Potteries area of England and go south to Herefordshire. There he found a group of over six hundred men and women who had broken from the Wesleyan Methodist faith; they called themselves the United Brethren. They were searching for light and truth, and had done all they could to find it, praying to the Lord continually to open the way before them that they might gain salvation. [^30]

During the first thirty days after Wilford's arrival he baptized thirty-five preachers and one hundred sixty members of the United Brethren. Wilford was given the use of one chapel and fifty-four houses, allowing him to preach according to the law. This opened the door, and over eighteen hundred souls were baptized. Only one of the United Brethren failed to embrace the Gospel. [^31]

162Referring to God's power in guiding him to the United Brethren, Elder Woodruff humbly testified:

162The power of God rested upon us and upon the missionÂ�. The sick were healed, devils were cast out, and the lame made to walkÂ�. The whole history of this Herefordshire mission shows the importance of listening to the still small voice of the spirit of God, and the revelations of the Holy Ghost. [^32]

162When Wilford left Herefordshire, he was satisfied with the results of his labors, and grateful to God for continued blessings. He marvelled that the Lord had led him through eight hundred miles of densely populated country to the very door which opened to his successes. [^33]

Mission of the TwelveHC162As the Twelve exercised their faith in pursuing their mission as the Savior directed his original Twelve, "without purse or scrip," they witnessed great miracles. This principle of faith and sacrifice brought power to the teachings of the Twelve. It also set a vivid standard for the hundreds of missionaries who later served in Britain and worldwide. As late as 1883, President John Taylor proclaimed that preaching the Gospel throughout the earth without purse or scrip was "a duty placed upon us by the Almighty." [^34]

Mission of the TwelveHC162Elders Kimball and Young noted that in their travels to New York they spent over $87.00 from a purse which contained only $13.50. They testified that "the money could only have been put in [Brigham's] trunk by some heavenly messenger, who thus administered to our necessities daily as he knew we needed." [^35]

Mission of the TwelveHC162Elder John Taylor related a similar experience:

Mission of the TwelveHCWhile laboring on the Isle of Man he had secured the printing of some tracts, which he wrote in reply to the falsehoods circulated by ministers and others regarding the character and doctrines taught by the Prophet Joseph Smith. When the tracts were ready the printer would not deliver them until every penny was paid which was due him. Elder Taylor did not have sufficient to meet the demand, and being very anxious to obtain the tracts went immediately into a private room, and, kneeling down, told the Lord in plain simplicity exactly how much he needed to pay for the matter he had published in defense of his cause. In a few minutes after his prayer was offered a young man came to the door and, upon being invited to enter, handed Elder Taylor an envelope and walked out. The young man was unknown to him. The envelope contained some money and a little note which read: "The laborer is worthy of his hire," and no signature was placed thereon. In a few minutes later a poor woman engaged as a fish vender came to the house and offered a little money to assist him in his ministerial labors. He told her there was plenty of money in the world and he did not wish to take her money. She insisted that the Lord would bless her the more and she would be happier if he would accept it, whereupon he received the offering, and to his surprise the poor woman's mite, added to what the young man had given him, made exactly the amount sufficient to pay the printer the balance due him. [^36]

163Just as the Lord had acknowledged the service and sacrifice of following Elder Kimball's mission, he gave such a communication to Brigham Young, this time in the form of a commandment through the prophet Joseph Smith. Eight days after President Young's return to Nauvoo, the following was revealed to the Prophet:

163Dear and well-beloved brother, Brigham Young, verily thus saith the Lord unto you: My servant Brigham, it is no more required at your hand to leave your family as in times past, for your offering is acceptable to me. ( D&C 126:1-3 )

Perhaps this is the only record we have of the Lord instructing someone to refrain from doing so much missionary work. It should be noted, however, that within a few weeks the Prophet placed added responsibilities upon President Young and the Twelve to "Â�stand in their place next to the First Presidency, and attend to the settling of emigrants and the business of the Church at the stakes," as well as the missionary work for which the Twelve had been responsible. [^37]

The Third Missionary Thrust to the British Isles

The third thrust commenced about two years after the shock and grief of Joseph's and Hyrum's martyrdom. The Saints had again been driven from their homes and from the city they had built. They were scattered on open prairies with the ominous intent to move west. Under the new leadership of President Brigham Young the Lord required more sacrifice. For seven years, despite the desperate conditions of the Saints, President Young assigned many of the leading brethren to distant missionary labors. Many of the Twelve and other brethren were called to labor in Great Britain from 1846-53; this culminated in the opening of missions in various parts of the world.

164The Saints had been driven from their comfortable homes in beautiful Nauvoo. They were either scattered on barren plains or struggling to establish themselves in the valleys of the western mountains. Yet again, they were called to sacrifice by sending many of the leading brethren into missionary service.

164Besides continuing the work in the British Isles, President Brigham Young moved with great vigor and vision to send the gospel into other areas of the world. In the fall conference of 1849, missionaries were called to open the work in the Society Islands, Switzerland, Italy, Scandinavia, Iceland and in islands of the Pacific. One year later, South America, Hawaii, Australia, New Zealand and Tasmania were also opened. Two years later missionaries were sent to South Africa. Unsuccessful attempts to preach the gospel were made in China, India, Gibraltar, Spain, the West Indies, and British Guiana. [^38]

165These missionaries were a most peculiar sight as they traveled against the flow of traffic at the time. One writer observed,

165In 1849, when the feverish haste for gold drew men in a steady stream across the nation to California, the strangest sight in all that dreary march was the Mormon missionary threading his way against that tide. It was so strange an event that few historians of the West have passed it by without commentÂ�. The startling difference [between the missionary and seeker for gold] lay in the fact that, while the illusive lustre of the yellow metal was drawing all other men toward it, the missionary, who often had been at the very scene of the discovery and felt the thrill of the golden dust in his hands, was turning his back against it all, and was traveling out into the world to give, without thought of self and without a wage, two or three years of his time for the salvation of his fellow men. [^39]

165Many sacrificed much to take the Gospel message to the world. Orson Spencer is exemplary. He came from a rather prestigious background, being a graduate of two colleges and a Baptist minister of a flourishing congregation in Massachusetts, of which the govenor of the state was a member. A newspaper account of his conversion contained the following observation: "Scholarly, enthusiastic, eloquent, young Spencer was fast rising into influence in the Baptist Church. His defection to the despised Latter-day Saints created a sensation throughout New England." [^40]

165Orson and his wife Catherine were introduced to the Gospel by his brother, Daniel, who had previously joined the Church. Daniel shared his testimony and a copy of the Book of Mormon with them. After prayerful and careful study they both shed tears as they acknowledged the truthfulness of the Gospel. [^41] Now sacrifice was required:

I counted the cost, to my self and family, of embracing such viewsÂ�. The expense I viewed through unavoidable tears, both in public and private, by night and by day. I said, however, the Lord, He is God, I can, I will embrace the truthÂ�. What could I do? Truth had taken possession of my mind-plain, simple Bible truth. It might be asked if I could not expel it from my door; yes, I could do it; but how would that harmonize with a sincere profession to preach and practice the truth, by way of example to others? It was a crisis I never shall, I never can forget. I remember it as an exodus from parents, kindred, denomination, and temporal support. [^42]

166Orson and Catherine, baptized in the spring of 1831, immediately gathered with the Saints in Nauvoo. They built a home, and Orson served as a city alderman and taught school. Elder B. H. Roberts later observed that from the time of Orson's baptism into the Church, he "became one of the most faithful, as he was one of the most refined, talented and highly educated disciples of the New Dispensation." [^43] Along with the rest of the Saints, the Spencer family fell victim to the drivings and mobbings in Nauvoo, which forced them from protection of their home to face severe hardships on the Iowa plains. Catherine, youngest daughter of an affluent family, suffered greatly during this time. She fell victim to the hardships of the persecution, for her slender and delicate frame could not endure the cold weather and lack of sleep. Her health gradually deteriorated, and just before her death she said to her husband, "A heavenly messenger has appeared to me tonight, and told me that I had done and suffered enough, and that he had now come to convey me to a mansion of gold." Throughout her deterioration she had instructed her children on how she wanted them to live when she was gone. Upon her death she asked that a message be delivered to her father's family: "Charge them to obey the gospel." [^44]

While caring for six young motherless children, Orson was called by the Quorum of Twelve to preside over the British mission. This appointment came at a most critical time for the British mission: a financial scandal had occurred involving the mismanagement and fraudulent handling of funds, collected from members of the Church in Britain for emigration to America. The Mission President, Reuben Hedlock, was excommunicated for his dealings. The Church in Britain needed good leaders to restore confidence and trust, as well as to build and strengthen the membership there. Hence Orson Spencer was sent.

167 On 28 October 1846, about seven months after the death of his wife, Orson left his children in the care of friends at Winter Quarters and started for England. In Orson's absence, President Young, Elder Wilford Woodruff and others personally cared for and nurtured the Spencer children. [^45]

167Through the sacrifice and labors of Orson Spencer, the British Isles experienced their greatest growth in membership. For two years, mostly under Orson Spencer's leadership, 8,467 were reported baptized. [^46] Perhaps Orson's greatest contribution was his effectiveness in restoring confidence and unity among the Saints. It was believed that the spirit of the Saints had never been better, and that Christian love and service flourished. [^47]

167When Elder Orson Pratt replaced Orson Spencer as presiding officer, he wrote of the blessings brought by President Spencer's sacrifices,

167The Saints in this land have been highly favored and extensively benefited by the indefatigable and praise-worthy labors of our much esteemed and dearly beloved brother, Elder Orson Spencer, whose wise and judicious course in his presidential administration over the saints in this land will ever live in remembrance of all the faithful. His integrity and sterling virtue have erected for him an enduring monument that can never perish. [^48]

167Following his release in 1849, Orson Spencer returned to America and his children in Salt Lake Valley. He later served missions in Prussia and the United States. While serving among the Lamanites, Orson Spencer became ill with malaria and died, on 15 October 1855. Through Aurelia Spencer Rogers, the third child of Catherine and Orson Spencer, the Primary program of the Church was founded. She was also the mother of twelve children, and was very active in the women's suffrage movement in Utah. [^49]

British Converts: Their Sacrifices and Blessings

From the introduction of the first missionaries, new British converts were encouraged to share the gospel with family and friends. Many were called to active missionary service, either part-time or full-time. These recently converted missionaries were expected to serve in a manner similar to the Twelve-without purse or scrip.

168Because of a severe economic depression at the time, there was extreme poverty throughout the British Isles, the pathetic conditions being made worse by poor weather. Many in England were starving. The streets were crowded with men, women and children begging for alms, and many with little clothing or shelter. [^50]

168Because of these conditions, a mission without purse or scrip became an even greater challenge than it might have been in America. Missionaries had to be largely supported by the British Saints, who often had little more than the poor missionaries. Yet journals record that as missionaries and members exercised their faith, blessings were poured out upon them.

Baptized in 1850, shortly after his eighteenth birthday, Charles W. Penrose was the only member of his family to accept the Gospel. Within seven months he was ordained an elder and called on a mission. When he turned his back on a secure government position if he remained at home, Charles was ridiculed by friends and associates. Charles accepted the call to serve without purse or scrip. He and his companion set out on foot for their first field of labor, Maldon, in Essex. They were assigned to build up branches of the Church where none existed. After days of walking on sore and bleeding feet, and spending nights sleeping outdoors, Elder Penrose and his companion arrived at Malden. After one day in the new area, Elder Penrose's companion returned home, discouraged. Alone, and having not eaten in over a day, he begged for food and water. He was rejected, humiliated and discouraged. Finally, receiving some water, he lifted it to his lips, then broke down and cried. [^51]

169His first mission lasted a decade. Each year he walked between three and four thousand miles, baptizing more converts than any other missionary. He also contributed articles to the Millennial Star, and wrote poetry, much of which was put to music. Elder Penrose wrote the words to the popular LDS hymn "O Ye Mountains High." Though he had never seen Utah, he often imagined Utah's mountains as he contemplated his desire to gather with the Saints. The hymn was written on a dusty road in Essex, as Elder Penrose walked with blistered feet, to a cottage meeting, where he sang it for the first time. He once remarked, "It was inspirational and seemed to please President Brigham Young." [^52]

169Elder Penrose, recognized as an effective leader, presided over the London, Cheltenham, Herefordshire and Birmingham conferences. While presiding over the Birmingham Conference, he had a trying experience which inspired him to write the words for another popular Latter-day Saint hymn, "School Thy Feelings, O My Brother." After giving years of selfless service, Elder Penrose was transferred from presiding over the Birmingham Conference. He took with him some belongings and personal furniture, which he had brought with him some years earlier. A missionary, believing the properties belonged to the Church, started a false rumor that questioned Elder Penrose's integrity. Penrose later reflected:

169I had labored then over ten years in the ministry, most of the time as traveling elder, literally without purse or scrip. I started that way and continued, suffering a great many hardships and difficulties and trials that I need not refer to now, and this touched me right to the heart. I did not know how to bear it. Weltering under these feelings I sat down and wrote that little poem, right from my soul, and intended it for myself. [^53]

170Finally in 1861, Elder Penrose emigrated to America with his family and six hundred and twenty other emigrating Saints who traveled to Zion under his supervision. Five years later, and again in 1885, he returned to serve additional missions in the British Isles. During both these missions Elder Penrose traveled and preached in various parts of Europe. Throughout his life, he gave to his church and to his adopted state and country. He served as editor of the Deseret News, Assistant Church Historian, President of the European Mission, Apostle, and counselor to two Presidents of the Church. [^54]

170As British converts attempted to serve missions in the British Isles, they found intense persecution. In 1840, while laboring in England, Elder Wilford Woodruff witnessed the following:

170The church ministers in this region were stirred up very much at this time, because of the success of the work of God in the midst of the people, and every exertion was made by them to stay its progressÂ�. They were alarmed, and were holding conventions and meetings to contrive plans and adopt means to overthrow the latter-day work of God, which they understood not, and believed not, yet feared its power. And well indeed they might; for in some instances they did not have more than ten or fifteen at their churches on the Sabbath, while around them on every hand they had seen forty branches of two organized conferences of the Church of Latter-day Saints spring up in about six months, with over a thousand members and between one and two hundred officers ordained to scatter the seed of the gospel everywhere in this prolific field. [^55]

170Edward Gilbert Harding, of Trowbridge, Wiltshire, England, was a traveling missionary in Britain for eight and one-half years, without purse or scrip. His journal relates experiences typical of the persecutions of the times. While serving in the Lynn District, he recorded that he was repeatedly stoned by his countrymen. When he stood to speak, mobs gathered and tried to disrupt the street meetings. When meetings were held inside, mobs would play loud band music just outside during most of the meeting.

171One day, Elder Harding and his companions attempted to avoid the mob. They crossed through a field, but boys lay in the field to meet them, and a mob came down into the field to meet them, throwing stones and weeds. When they got to the meeting house, they found one of the brethren with his head cut open, blood running into his eyes. But the meeting succeeded, and two were baptized the next day.

171Many threats were made against their lives. On another occasion, Elder Harding recorded,

171Many times after we closed our meeting, a mob of 300 or 400 would come after me and swear they would jam the Holy Ghost out of me. They tried all they could to get me under their feet, but there was a power greater than the power of man around me, so that with all their trying they did not hurt me at all. Many stones were thrown, but not one touched my flesh. For this also, I have to praise the LordÂ�. Truly sometimes it appeared as though the gates of hell were opened and all the demons let free. [^56]

171Many missionaries and converts had their employment threatened because of their affiliation with the Church. This was particularly trying, because jobs were so scarce. Elder John T. Evans and four other missionaries, serving missions in north Wales, worked in the iron works to support themselves and their families. When their employers learned about their religious activities, these men were given the choice of renouncing the "heresy" or losing their jobs. They chose the latter. Following this action, about two hundred of their converts were put to the test. They were given one month to renounce their religion or lose their jobs. Finding another job would be almost impossible, especially when terminated for being a Mormon. About half of them chose to renounce their religion. The others lost their jobs, and this caused considerable suffering to them and their families. These faithful Saints were given temporal support by other members of the Church during this difficult time. [^57]

172William Fowler, a missionary for four years after his conversion in Sheffield, also lost his job in a factory when he joined the Church. His testimony stood firm as he expressed in the favorite Latter-day Saint hymn, "We Thank Thee, O God, For a Prophet." [^58]

172The sacrifices of thousands of British converts in gathering with the Saints in America should not be forgotten. During the last six decades of the nineteenth century, over eighty-five thousand members of the Church emigrated from Europe to America, most of these from Great Britain. Many gave their lives and lost loved ones while traveling to Nauvoo or Utah, because of the crowded and unsanitary conditions on the ships and the ordeals they encountered while crossing the plains.

172Crossing the Atlantic usually meant suffering and sickness. In reporting one such crossing, William Clayton wrote: "Such sickness, vomiting, groaning and bad smells I never witnessed before and added to this the closeness of the berths almost suffocated us for want of air." [^59] The combination of poor diet, sea sickness, and extremely crowded and unsanitary conditions brought suffering and oftentimes death at sea. An outbreak of cholera killed forty-three British Saints on the ship Berlin in 1849, and measles took the life of twenty-one children and two adults on the Clara Wheeler in 1854. [^60]

172There are also countless stories of sacrifice and suffering in crossing the plains to Utah. Of these, few Saints suffered more than the ill-fated Willie and Martin handcart companies, which together included more than one thousand British converts.

It must be noted that there have been sacrifices and service rendered by faithful Saints who did not emigrate but remained in Britain. Over the years they have built and strengthened the Church. Robert and Maria Coleby, for example, joined the Church in 1850 and lived out their days in the Lowestoft Branch in Suffolk, England. Today there are still descendants of Robert and Maria Coleby who are active in the Lowestoft Ward. Over the past 138 years the family has furnished seven branch presidents, who have given approximately sixty-five total years of service in that position. Other family members have given much time and effort in other callings, as well as in providing a haven for missionaries and visiting Church authorities. [^61]

173I believe that George and Hannah May made a most significant sacrifice. After joining the Church in Berkshire, England in 1847, they and their seven children left their homeland in 1852 to gather with the Saints in Utah. After considerable hardship, the family arrived at Council Bluffs. While there, a cholera epidemic ravaged the Saints, taking George's life, then their youngest daughter's, then their oldest daughter's, then their youngest son's, and finally Hannah's. Suffering the grief of this great loss, the oldest son, James, and the three other orphaned children moved on alone over more than a thousand miles of wilderness to join the Saints in the Salt Lake Valley. Through this great sacrifice and faithfulness came a rich heritage and fine posterity which now bless my life. I am blessed, as are my children and grandchildren, through my marriage to a great-granddaughter of James May.

Conclusion

Indeed, sacrifice brings forth the blessings of heaven. The resultant blessings which have come to the Church from the British Isles missions are incalculable. One very significant blessing, which continues to be a source of strength to the ever-growing Kingdom of God, was the vision and foundation of missionary work gained during these early years. President Gordon B. Hinckley has testified:

The call of Heber C. Kimball and his associates to go across the sea to Britain was a declaration by the Prophet Joseph of a vision of the great destiny of this restored workÂ�. From that time forth there has never been a dimming of that vision. Through the years that followed, regardless of drivings, persecution, poverty, oppression and every other force the adversary could exercise against them, the work has grown and expandedÂ�as the doors of the nations are opened, the messengers of truth will go forward in fulfillment of that great millennial vision which was opened in the dark days of Ohio and Missouri with the call of seven men to go to the British Isles. Their response to that call was a magnificent declaration of faith. [^62]

174This great chapter of history portrays the principle of sacrifice with the abundance of resulting blessings. It is the history of the Gospel taken to the British Isles, and the faithful response of the thousands who embraced it there. We should ever remember their sacrifices. They should inspire us. President Spencer W. Kimball has challenged us all:

174We must begin to think about our obligation rather than our convenience. The time, I think, has come when sacrifice must be an important element again in the Church. Remember the story of how Brigham Young and Heber C. Kimball went on their missions. Remember they were both ill. They were in poverty. Remember how Brigham Young fell down and could not get up, and Grandfather Heber C. Kimball went over and tried to lift him up but could not because he was so weak. So he called across the street to another brother and said, "Come on over here and help me get Brother Brigham up!" The next day both of them were on their missions. But we ofttimes must have everything ideal and perfect! [^63]
