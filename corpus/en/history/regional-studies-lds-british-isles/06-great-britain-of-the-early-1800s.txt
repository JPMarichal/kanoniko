Various authors, Regional Studies in LDS History: British Isles ( 1990)

Great Britain of the Early 1800s

H. Dean Garrett

When Mormon missionaries arrived in Great Britain in 1837, they were met by a diverse, troubled, land. Major changes were taking place that affected the lives of all the citizens of Great Britain. This period of time has been labeled as a

61turbulent, confusing era variously labelled the Age of Reform, the Age of the Chartists, or the Hungry Forties. Massive events like the New Poor Law, Chartism, the repeal of the Corn Laws, and the Great Irish Famine are played out amidst pedestrian political manoeuverings and violent fluctuations of the economy. [^1]

61The economic and social conditions were an open invitation to Mormon missionaries to sow the gospel seed successfully in the British Isles. In a letter to his wife, Orson Hyde described the people who responded to his message as "those whoÂ�are mostly manufacturers and some other mechanics. They know how to do but little else than to spin and weave cotton, and make cambrick, mull and lace, and what they would do in Kirtland or the city `Far West,' I cannot say. They are extremely poor, most of them not having a change of clothes decent to be baptized in, but they have open hearts and strong faith." [^2]

Shifting From Rural to Urban Population

Social and economic change in British society produced a shifting population. In spite of large-scale emigration, Britain's population increased from 24.1 million in 1831 to 26.7 million in 1841 and 27.3 million in 1851. During this period there was a general shift of the population from rural to urban areas. Migration "took the form of short distant movementÂ�into the industrial towns of Lancashire and Yorkshire." Later, in the 1840s, "large numbers of immigrant Irish swelled the numbers of native English and Scots who were migrating to urban areas." [^3]

62Major population centers of Northern Britain "continued to grow rapidly between 1831 and 1851: Manchester from 182,000 to 303,000; Leeds 123,000 to 172,000; Birmingham 144,000 to 233,000; and Glasgow 102,000 to 345,000. Bradford, the fastest growing town in this period of industrial revolution, had 13,000 inhabitants in 1801, 26,000 in 1821, and 104,000 in 1851." [^4] [^5] [^6]

Impact of the Industrial Revolution

The industrial revolution altered the focus of British Society from rural to urban, from agricultural and domestic to factory manufacture. By the time the first Mormon missionaries arrived in England in the summer of 1837, the stress and challenges of major change were being felt by all segments of the society, especially in such areas as transportation, because of the widespread use of the railroad. Employment and educational opportunities responded to increased industrialization, transforming the whole basis of British life. As production modification occurred, the capital gained was put into investments. For the average person, however, living conditions either remained the same or grew worse.

63The cities themselves became overpowering and confining. Describing a visit to Leeds in 1841, one writer wrote: "I drew near the townÂ�and the tall chimneys of the factories becameÂ�visible through the dense clouds of smoke." [^7] [^8]

63Thus there emerged a new class of people, referred to as the laboring poor or the working class, which included all workers (except a minority made up of the gentry class), a few professional and commercial men, and industrialists.

The labor force differed considerably from other classes in Britain, for it was totally separated from them. A tremendous gulf was fixed between the manual laborer and the skilled worker. It appears that no matter how skilled a man was, the type of work he did determined his status. Thus when one reads authors and novelists like Dickens, one finds that even the lowest paid, unskilled clerical help viewed himself above that of the highly skilled artisan. Even where the laborers lived was an act of separation, because most often they lived together in a closed world of their own, removed from the cultured middle-class experiences.

64Yet this vast body of laboring people reflected great diversity of their own, dependent on the type of skills and regularity of their employment, their trade and industry, and their training, education, and location. Consequently, when the missionaries entered Great Britain, they met a great diversity among the existing social structures. Those who generally responded to the gospel message were the laboring poor. [^9]

Economic Booms and Depressions

One of the greatest challenges that faced the working man in Victorian England when the missionaries arrived was the lack of job security. The years 1825 to 1835 was a boom period for the economy, as bounteous crops were grown and factories were producing. However, in 1836, the good harvests were gone, and trade slowed. "By 1837, the country was plunged into a prolonged depression, lasting until 1842. These six years were the grimmest period in the history of the nineteenth century. Industry came to a standstill, unemployment reached hitherto unknown proportions, and with high food prices and inadequate relief, the manufacturing population faced hunger and destitution. At no time did the whole system seem nearer to complete breakdown." [^10] [^11]

Even in good times, for many of the working poor, employment was neither constant nor steady. Many writers have indicated that "only about a third of the labouring people in the country were fully employed, another third were partially employed, and the remaining third wholly unemployed at any given time." [^12] [^13]

65several manufactoring establishments were shut up, and several thousand workmen as well as women and children were thrown out of employment, whose sufferings during that time were severe, and I was credibly informed. and verily believe, that numbers perished from starvation. Such sufferings I never witnessed in my life before and the scenes which I daily beheld while in that country was ready to chill the blood in my veins: the streets were crowded with men, women, and children, who solicited alms from the passengers as they walked along. Numbers of those poor wretches were without shoes or stockings and scarcely any covering to screen them from the inclemency of the weather. [^14]

Wilford Woodruff reported the same feelings: "The streets were crouded with the poor both male & female going to & from the factories with their wodden or Clogg Shoes on which makes a great ratling over the pavement. The poor are in as great Bondage as the children of Israel in Egypt." [^15] [^16]

66P. A. M. Taylor, in an article in the Millennial Star, noted that "In Britain, workers lived in 'poverty andÂ�refined yet cruel slavery,Â�free to starve, and the willing toiler free to beg, and often to beg in vain for the privilege to toil'; while their lot was made all the more insecure by the trade cycle." [^17]

66The conditions the missionaries met appear to have motivated them to go and proclaim the gospel to these people and to assist them if at all possible. Joseph Fielding wrote in his diary that "a Brother took Elder Turly and myself through a Factory. I was much affected to see the Slavery that is there endured, the Dust, the bad Smell of oil, etc., the deafening Noise, and the confinement. With a constant aim of the Masters to sink their Wages or fire [fine?] them for any and every trifling defect, one would think (it) enough to make even a wise Man mad. We felt a great desire to see them delivered out of such Bondage & captivity. Many are out of employment, and I suppose without food." [^18]

66As a result of this and similar conditions that existed during this time, there was much labor unrest, which resulted in rioting and in the formation of trade unions. The action of the trade unions caused more unrest as well as legal actions. Laws were passed to try to assist the workers, and yet protect the owners of the mills and the companies. Thomas Day recorded in his journal that "dissatisfaction arose among the employees on account of reductions of wages, a sixteen-week strike ensued accompanied by riots in which I stood a silent observer but took no part. At the end of this time the men returned to work, reduced to dire poverty and completely conquered." [^19]

The government of Great Britain had always been concerned about the condition of the poor and had tried to assist them. As early as the 1300s, Parliament passed legislation referred to as The Poor Law, designed to assist the poor, and it remained on the books, with additions and changes, through 1600. The main ingredients of the law remained until The Poor Law Amendment Act of 1834 was passed. [^20]

67During these times those who were unable to work were given a competent sum of money. Provisions were made for children to be apprenticed out and trained. Boys were apprenticed until they were twenty-four, and girls until the age of twenty-one. The greatest struggle that faced English society at that time was what to do about the able-bodied who were not able, or were unwilling, to work. It was in this setting that the workhouses developed. To many writers the term "workhouse" was a very poor title. Many observers referred to them rather as the "poor houses." As with other governmental programs, many provisions and restrictions were placed on the poor houses in order that help could be given to those who came to these seeking it, and the government could try to direct what happened to them. The people assigned to the poor house included those who were unwilling to work, those who had lost their job and could not, therefore, seek employment other than through the workhouse system, and also those who were prisoners of debt. Because of the great abuse that had taken place over the centuries in the administration of the poor laws, and particularly because the commission report indicated the readiness of many to live on the dole, the Poor Law Act of 1834 was passed. It added the workhouse test, which was designed to make it harder for unemployed people to qualify for help and to receive outside assistance without work. However, the workhouse tests did not solve the problem of unemployment and poverty:

The numbers of under-unemployed artisans were too great for all to be housed in what The Times dubbed "the new Bastilles," which were bitterly disliked both by handworkers whose skills power-driven machinery was making obsolete and by factory workers who formed a pool of the unemployed during trade depressions. Workhouses were administered as Charles Dickens tells us in Oliver Twist, on the principle "that all poor people should have the alternative of being starved by a gradual process in the house, or by a quick one out of it." The separation of married couples, and the housing of their children separately, were practices bitterly resented. In fact, when Dickens was writing Oliver Twist, in 1837 and 1838, he was exposing poorlaw inhumanities, some of which survived into the twentieth century. [^21]

68Apparently workhouses sprung up throughout England and Wales. The conditions varied from place to place, but even in the best of them, the conditions were dreadful. Under the Poor Law Act of 1834, relief was given under strict conditions. First, anyone who received assistance had to be living in the workhouse. Second, workhouses were governed by the philosophy that conditions and wages in the workhouse were to be more miserable than what the lowest paid worker outside the workhouse received. Thus anyone who entered the workhouse knew that conditions inside were worse than the conditions outside. Carlyle responded that "if paupers are made miserable, paupers will needs decline in multitude. It is a secret known to all rat catchers." [^22]

68The poor and the working poor, especially during times of recession, when jobs were unbearable, viewed the workhouse system with fear, as they watched the great buildings built and heard the rumors of the conditions inside. Many had a constant daily fear that tomorrow they could be in the workhouse. Harrison described what happened to a family who entered a workhouse:

68When a pauper family presented itself for relief at the gates of the workhouse it was immediately broken up, men, women and children being housed in separate parts of the building and forbidden to reunite as long as they remained. Able-bodied men were set to work at stone breaking, grinding corn or picking oakum. Food was plain and monotonous: mainly bread and gruel, with a small allowance of meat and cheese. Until 1842 all meals were eaten in silence, and smoking was forbidden. A special workhouse dress was worn, and the master of the workhouse was enjoined "to enforce industry, order, punctuality and cleanliness" at all times. Visitors were allowed infrequently and only in the presence of a master or matron. [^23]

69These conditions created an atmosphere of fear, and rumors spread concerning them. Many cases of abuse were talked about outside the wall. For example,

69the two and a half year old child in Warwick workhouse was punished for dirtying himself by having his own excrement forced into his mouth; the master of the Hoo Union workhouse who pruriently flogged teenage girls; the prisoner in Knutsford gaol who had been sent there for fetching his child into his own bed when he heard it crying during the night in another part of the workhouse in which he then was. Most notorious of all was the scandal in the Andover Workhouse, where the paupers were so hungry that they fought among themselves for bits of gristle and marrow among the old bones they were set to crush. [^24]

Family Life and Housing Problems

The development of the factory system strained the families among the laboring poor. Instead of the family working together at home, or the father working as the breadwinner, the whole family worked outside the home and most often in different factories. Thus more and more mothers worked outside the home, and many, if not all, the children worked as well.

69The 1841 census showed that out of 118,000 coal miners in Great Britain, 2,350 of them were women, mostly in the West Riding, Lancashire and Scottish pits. In the cotton districts in 1852, about 30 percent of all married women were employed, and of these almost two-thirds were textile operators. Of course the percentage of married women who at one time or another had gone to work was much higher. [^25]

69The employment of children was looked upon as an economic necessity. The mine and mill owners considered prohibitive the cost of not employing children. Before 1830 there was no law that controlled the employment of children. Thus small children as young as 5-8 years of age were employed in coal mines as trappers whose jobs were "to open and close the doors as the tubs went by so that air circulation would be maintained." [^26]

70The first Parliament elected after 1832 discussed a bill that restricted the employment of children under nine and limited the number of hours that those under the age 18 could work to ten hours per day. A Royal Commission, on investigating working conditions, recommended that the bill not be passed because it would bankrupt the mill owners. Parliamentarians sarcastically commented: "Our great stay and bulwark is to be found in 30,000 little girls." [^27] An example of this was found in a visit to a young girl factory worker in Manchester in 1841. A typical day for her began when

70the workman has knocked on the window at 4:30 in the morning; the girl's mother: "rouses the unwilling girl to another day of toil. At length you hear her on the floor; the clock is striking five. Then, for the first time, the girl becomes conscious of the necessity for haste; and having slipped on her clothes, and (if she thinks there is time) washed herself, she takes a drink of cold coffee, which had been left standing in the fireplace, a mouthful of bread (if she can eat it), and having packed up her breakfast in her handkerchief, hastens to the factory. The bell rings as she leaves the threshold of her home. Five minutes more, and she is in the factory, stripped and ready for work. The clock strikes half-past five; the engine starts, and her day's work commences.

70"At half-past sevenÂ�the engine slacks its pace (seldom stopping) for a short time till the hands have cleaned the machinery and swallowed a little food. It then goes on again, and continues at a full speed till twelve o'clock, when it stops for dinner. Previously to leaving the factory, and in her dinner-hour, she has her machines to clean. The distance of the factory is about a five minutes walk from her home. I noticed every day that she came in at half-past twelve, or within a minute or two, once she was over the half hour; the first thing she did was to wash herself, then get her dinner (which she was seldom able to eat), and pack up her drinking for the afternoon. This done, it was time to be on her way to work again, where she remaines without one minute's relaxation till 7 o'clock; she then comes home, and throws herself into a chair exhausted. This [is] repeated six days in the week, (save that on Saturdays she may get back a little earlier, say, an hour or two)Â�. This young woman looks very pale and delicate, and has every appearance of an approaching decline. I was asked to guess her age; I said, perhaps fifteenÂ�Her motherÂ�told me she was going on nineteenÂ�. She is a fair speciman of a great proportion of factory girls in Manchester." [^28]

71The Factory Act of 1833, finally passed, did prohibit the use of children under 9 except in silk mills, and the working hours of persons under 18 were limited to 12 hours a day and 69 hours a week. In 1834, a bill was passed that did not allow anyone under the age of 13 to be employed for more than 9 hours a day and 48 hours a week. Night work was made illegal for all under 18 years of age, and children under 13 were to attend school for at least 2 hours a day. [^29]

This Act resulted from a public debate over the impact of the employment of women and children on family life. As the missionaries entered England, they found a major change taking place in the nuclear family. Great concern was being expressed that the domestic system and stable family relationships were in danger. This concern focused on the fact that the factory system broke up the working class family, first by physically separating the family. Family members worked 12 to 14 hours a day. They were together only to eat and sleep, and on Sundays and holidays. Second, a working mother did not "have time to do the housework, sewing and cooking except in the evenings, when she was tired, and on Sundays (when she should have been at church)." [^30] [^31]

72The factory reformers, extremely concerned about the working family, consequently, made the family one of their major challenges. The concern was expressed by Lord Ashley in 1844 when he, after realizing that a wife left home at 6 o'clock every morning and worked with an hour's dinner break between 12:30 to 1:30 until 6 at night, asked the question "where sir, under these conditions are the possibilities of domestic life?" [^32]

72Because a highly paid artisan earned about 40 shillings a week, it became very difficult for the laboring man to provide decent housing for his family. In addition, the social structure of Great Britain was developing in such a way that all laboring people were housed in certain areas of the city. Thus even if the means for better housing were available, the ability to acquire improved housing might be limited. Consequently, the condition of life of the working man and his family was determined by the types of housing they lived in. Harrison described the average home "Most urban and some rural workers lived in cottages of three or four rooms and a kitchen. Such homes were two-storeyed, and (in the towns) were built in terraces or around courts. Unlike the labouring poor on the Continent, very few British workers lived in tenements: only in the old town of Edinburgh were there to be found tenement buildings up to ten-storeys high. British towns expanded outwards rather than upwards. Long rows of terrace houses in red brick, or sometimes of stone in the northern towns, sprawled outwards from the older city centres." [^33]

Consequently, a phenomenon which began to develop in the 19th century became a separator in British society as the laboring poor were segregated into definite areas of housing in working class districts, where in the past they had mingled with the rich and the merchants. In Leeds "the statistical committee of the town council in 1839 estimated that of the total population of 82,120 in the township, 61,212 belonged to the working classes, and the majority of these lived in certain well-defined areas of the town." [^34]

73In 1850, Edward Hall, a Unitarian Domestic missionary in Holbeck, described daily life in such accommodations:

73They are built back to back, with no possibility of good ventilation, and contain a cellar for coals and food, the coal department being frequently tenanted with fowls, pigeons, or rabbits, and in some cases with two or all three of these-a room from 9 to 14 feet by from 10 to 12 or 14 feet to do all the cooking, washing, and the necessary work of a family, and another of the same size for all to sleep in. Think for a moment what must be the inconvenience, the danger both in a moral and physical sense, when parents and children, young men and women, married and single, are crowded together in this way, with three beds in a room, and barely a couple of yards in the middle for the whole family to undress and dress in. [^35]

73Throughout industrial England a similar description is repeatedly given of the squalor and the struggles that the working class experienced as they tried to maintain decent housing. [^36] Describing his visit to one of these dwellings, Robert Baker wrote:

73I have been in one of these damp cellars, without the slightest drainage, every drop of wet and every morsel of dirt and filth having to be carried up into the street; two corded frames for beds, overlaid with sacks for five persons; scarcely anything in the room else to sit on but a stool or a few bricks; the floor in many places absolutely wet; a pig in the corner also; and in a street where filth of all kinds had accumulated for years. In another house, where no rent had been paid for years by reason of apparent inability to do it, I found a father and mother and their two boys, both under the age of sixteen years, the parents sleeping on similar corded frames, and the two boys upon straw, on the floor upstairs; never changing their clothes week's end to week's end, working in the dusty department of a flax mill, and existing upon coffee and bread. [^37]

This appeared not to be an exceptional event, for records show that in the 1830s in Manchester a total of 40 to 50 thousand people were living in cellars, while in Liverpool more than 45,000 lived in cellars. [^38] [^39] [^40]

74As a result of housing, sanitation conditions in many urbanized areas of England were also very poor. Factory exhausts made the air quality poor. The water supply was impure. Sanitary conditions-sewer, garbage, etc.-were nonexistent. J. Walker reported that

74it became the practice to discharge sewage through city drains into the Thames, the river, the main source of London's water supply, became dangerously impure. In many towns, fly-invested refuge from kitchens and privees littered muddy highways, courtyards and alleys, removed sometimes by pauper labor or for sale as manure by the "night soil" men. In Greenock in 1840, reputed to be dirtier than other Scottish towns, a hundred cubic feet of manure was piled up in the centre of the town, while the open sewers of Royal Windsor were at least as bad as anything to be found in the newly industrialised North, and possibly worse. [^41]

75Subsequently, as a result of the sanitary conditions, great epidemics of disease were common in the British Isles. There were cholera outbreaks in London in 1832, 1849, 1854, 1866, and 1871. The outbreak of cholera in 1849 in that city alone caused the death of 13,161 people. [^42] [^43] [^44]

75Food available to the working class was not very nourishing, nor was it readily available. The cost was several times beyond the means of families, especially during long periods of unemployment. There were times when even if money was available, food, because of lack of production and weather problems, was not. In the history of England, the 1840s has become known as "the Hungry Forties." [^45]

76In a letter to Joseph Smith, Heber C. Kimball wrote:

76With regard to the state of the country, we may say it is bad indeed: trade appears to be growing worse, in fact, many branches of it is almost at a stand, and not expected much to improve for some months. Thousands are out of employ, and we may safely say that there are thousands famishing for want of bread: we often see in the streets whole families begging for bread; and in many instances, some respectable looking characters may be seen singing through the streets to obtain a little bread; it is truly heart rending to see so many small children nearly naked, going from house to house begging. This scene of things is passing before our eyes daily, and we look upon it with sorrow and regret. [^46]

76Since missionaries were traveling without purse or scrip, the economic conditions and lack of food affected them directly. Willard Richards expressed the sentiment that "I have reason to thank the Lord for potatoes and salt the past week; And this one [week] a loaf of bread and a slice of meat at Sister Smith's." [^47]

76Without funds for sufficient food, proper clothing and adequate housing, many people consumed large quantities of alcohol. "Temperance workers assessed the total amount spent on drink at over 67 million in 1830 and nearly 81 million in 1850. This averaged nearly 3 per person per year, which in a laboring family of five or six amounted to more than they paid in rent." [^48] Liberalization of laws controlling the sale of beer and other alcoholic beverages, added to the problem. Some workers, such as the coal heavers, had to spend part of their wages on liquor as part of their employment conditions. This became a way of life for the working people. "For a labouring man to eschew drink in these circumstances was more than giving up a single habit: it was a repudiation of important aspects of working class community life. Conversely, adherence to drinking habits could be an assertion of independence, a resistance to the pressures for conformity to the values of the new industrial civilisation." [^49]

The heavy use of alcohol caused great concern among the social planners, and the temperance movement became active in Victorian England. As a result of their belief in the Word of Wisdom, the missionaries were allowed to use the temperance halls as places for meetings. Thus halls were available in almost all of the cities in which the brethren preached. [^50]

The Problem of Illiteracy

One of the major challenges the missionaries faced as they preached the gospel was the lack of education of the working class. Because of the many hours spent working, many adults had not learned how to read or write. At a hearing in the House of Commons in 1832, a witness testified, "I don't think there is above one in a hundred in the factories that can write." [^51] [^52]

77The push for adult education built slowly and focused on the Sunday School Program. Both adults and children were encouraged to attend and learn reading and writing. The industrial revolution produced an overwhelming need for an educated work force. In 1841 in Huddlesfield, an evening school program, sponsored by an association of working men, was organized to meet the needs of the working force. Volunteer teachers taught classes in "reading, writing, arithmetic, geography, art, and French." [^53]

77The Factory Act of 1833 expressed the concern regarding the education of the citizens. This Act mandated that textile employers "could only employ children between the ages of 9 and 13 who produced school attendance certificates." [^54] Thus factory schools became a way for employers to fulfill this requirement of the law. By 1851, 600,000 of the 5 million children between the ages of 3 and 15 were at work. "Over 2 million were in school, and the remainder were neither at work nor school." [^55]

Members of the Mormon Church benefitted from this movement. One Church member reported, "At the age of nine I commenced to work in Messrs. Marshalls Flax Mill, where my father worked and where my grandfather worked, and for years before his death was a clerk and cashier. I was a half-timer until I reached the age of thirteen, was at school in the forenoons and in the factory in the afternoons." [^56]

78If the people taught by the missionaries were a representative cross section of the population of Great Britain, then only 2/3 of the males and 1/2 of the women could write their names. [^57] In many cases, knowledge of the gospel came to them through vocal instruction and the Holy Ghost. Heber C. Kimball observed, "We have to live quite short, but the brethren are very kind to us, they are willing to divide with us the last they have, they are quite ignorant, many of them cannot read a word and it needs great care to teach them the gospel so that they can understand." [^58]

Emigration

The conditions in the British Isles in the first half of the nineteenth century encouraged many of the working class to look for security elsewhere. Emigration to other countries was the result of a desire for something better. "In 1815 less than two thousand persons left the British Isles: in 1830 the figure was over 55,000; by the late 1840's and early '50s more than a quarter of a million emigrants were leaving in single years." [^59] Most of the emigration was to the United States, Canada, and Australia, but some chose to go to other countries within the British Empire. [^60]

78When the call went out for the Saints to flee to Zion, it was not necessarily a new idea that was hard for the English Saints to accept. Even though it was difficult to leave family and friends behind, it did not appear to be difficult to leave England, Scotland or Wales behind. For most who watched the Saints leave, there was no expression of concern or sense of loss. Many viewed emigration as a solution to the problem of overcrowding and unemployment.

Conclusion

The beginning of missionary work in the British Isles took place at a time of extreme difficulty and change. The development of the factory system, the migration into industrial cities, the poor living conditions, and the challenges of employment had tremendous effects on the families in the British Isles. These changes and challenges were a catalyst to bring many to the gospel message. From that difficult environment came a people able to accept the gospel in such a way that many would do whatever God commanded.

79Social conditions in England during the nineteenth century produced a people able to leave England, cross the plains, and live in the unyielding deserts of Utah. It is hard to imagine the gentry of England emigrating in sufficient numbers to save and sustain the Church. It is hard to imagine them crossing the plains in covered wagons, and handcarts, or living in desert dugouts without complaint.

79It was the working poor of England that responded to the gospel call. As Joseph Fielding observed, "The people here are many of them poor, and not many rich will come in at the straight gate, the Lord will not accommodate their pride. We have now in Preston upwards of 50 baptismsÂ�. They are nearly all poor. If others come in it appears the poor will be first." [^61]
