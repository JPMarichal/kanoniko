Various authors, Regional Studies in LDS History: British Isles ( 1990)

Beginnings of the Millennial Star: Journal of the Mission to Great Britain

Alan K. Parrish

The Church periodical the Millennial Star originated in the apostolic mission to the British Isles in April 1840. It was published as a monthly, biweekly or weekly publication for 130 years as what most of us would call the British Church Magazine. It is the longest continuous publication in the history of the Church, terminating in 1970, along with The Improvement Era, The Instructor, and The Relief Society Magazine.

133I had thought to present a concise review of the entire 130 year run of the Millennial Star. As time passed, this seemed increasingly difficult. One day I ran across a history of the journal which reported that by 1920 the Star already exceeded 72,000 printed pages. Anyone familiar with it will attest that the printing is very small. Add 50 more years to the date of its termination (1970), and you will appreciate why I have narrowed my topic.

My next choice was to report on the first 25 years, for I felt these were the most critical to the progress of the Church. As I researched these beginnings, I found that the foundation laid in the first few years was closely followed throughout its duration. It was evident that the early Brethren were farsighted and inspired in their plans. Through repeated evaluations runs the feeling that the publication was successful, truly a "smashing" success. Many are the contributions of the Star.

134The list of editors of the Star in its first 100 years of publication is almost a "who's who of Mormonism"; it truly reflects the importance the brethren attached to this journalistic mission. These brethren gained valuable experience for future periodicals. Each added his personal touch to the foundation objectives and rules of style the journal followed. From the beginning the Star was usually edited by one of the Twelve. Almost all of them actively contributed to its publication.

134Another important insight is the spirit of the Brethren, who felt the restoration in every sinew and were eager to prepare the world for the day of the Lord's second coming. The restored kingdom was a living reality to them; nothing was of greater importance. They declared their message to everyone, from Queen Victoria to the beggar on the street, with a flowing sense of urgency. The world became the setting for the workings of God in bringing to pass the great Millennial day. Hardly a tremor, famine or other natural disaster in the earth passed without mention in the Star. Wars and contentions across the world were monitored. Activities in the Holy Land and the meanderings of the Jewish nation were recorded. The spreading of the Gospel, the progress of the restored kingdom, and the new revelations of God were broadcast to the entire British Nation.

Journalistic Mission

The 1840 Apostolic mission consisted of a second wave of missionaries to the British Isles. They were already convinced of the value of journalism as a part of the missionary responsibility of the Church. This is evident in the activities of Parley P. Pratt from the time he set out for his mission to England on August 29, 1839. Enroute, he and Orson stopped in Detroit for a fortnight to visit their aged father and mother. Parley reported, "We tarried with them two weeks; during which I preached in the city Hall at Detroit, and superintended some printing and publishing matters." [^1]

135Parley then traveled to New York City, where he resided with his family for six months. While there he "preached most of the time in the city and also superintended the printing and publishing of several of our books." [^2] During this time he performed occasional missions, including one in Washington, D.C., where he "printed a circular to each member of congress, and to the President of the United States and his cabinet, setting forth our principles in plainness, and bearing testimony of the truth." [^3]

135Shortly after the arrival of the Quorum in England, three days of conference and council meetings convened in Preston, 14-16 April 1840. One of the important business items, recorded in the minutes of the General Conference on the 15th, is as follows: "Moved and seconded that a monthly periodical shall be published under the direction and superintendence of the Twelve, for the benefit and information of the Church, as soon as a sufficient number of subscribers shall be obtained; carried." [^4]

135The minutes of the council meeting of the Twelve on the following day begin with this entry:

135The council met pursuant to adjournment, April 16th, 1840. The number of the Quorum the same as on the 14th.

135Moved by Elder Young, seconded by Elder Taylor, that Elder Parley P. Pratt be chosen as editor of the monthly periodical for the Church.

135Moved by Elder Willard Richards, seconded by Elder George A. Smith, that the name of the paper or periodical be the Latter-day Saints Millennial Star. [^5]

Vision and Mission of the Millennial Star

As the conference closed, "the Elders were assigned to their respective districts with instructions to solicit subscriptions for the forthcoming periodical." [^6] Elder Pratt recorded that he

136repaired for Manchester and commenced preparing to fulfil my new appointment. The first number of the Star was issued in May. The following hymn was written by myself expressly for the introduction of this periodical, and originally appeared on its cover.

136The morning breaks the shadows flee; Lo! Zion's standard is unfurled! The dawning of a brighter day Majestic rises on the world. [^7]

136While the hymn was Parley's most expressive description of the mission of the Star, he was more explicit in other statements. The first number of the Star, issued on Wednesday, 27 May 1840, contained a Prospectus and an Editors Address to his Patrons, both of which detailed the mission and objectives of the Star. In the Prospectus Elder Pratt expanded upon his poetic hymn:

136The long night of darkness is now far spent-the truth revived in its primitive simplicity and purity, like the day-star of the horizon, lights up the dawn of that effulgent morn when the knowledge of God will cover the earth as the waters cover the sea. It has pleased the AlmightyÂ�to send his servants in these last days, with a special message to all the nations of the earth, in order to prepare all who will hearken for the Second Advent of the Messiah, which is now near at hand.

136By this means, the Church has caused many tens of thousands to rejoice above measure, while they are enabled to walk in the light of truth.

136And feeling very desirous that others should be made partakers of the same blessings, by being made acquainted with the same truths, they have thought proper to order the publication of a Periodical devoted entirely to the great work of the spread of truth. [^8]

137A thesis on the history of the periodicals of the Church confirms this missionary role of the Star; "Mormonism" had no central home between Nauvoo and settlement of Great Salt Lake Valley in July, 1847:

137The Church published various publications during this interim, however, but none within the place later to be called Salt Lake City. They were published to act as printed carriers of the gospel in the several areas of the world chosen as early mission fields. [^9]

137The imbued sense of mission and the rapid growth of the Church in its first decade fed the courage of these brethren and sent their expectations soaring. Elder Pratt once wrote,

137We trust this paper will prove a welcome visitor to the palaces of the noble, the mansions of the rich, the towers of the brave, and the cottages of the poor: that the sublimity of its truths, the splendour of its light, and the easy simplicity of its style and language, may, at once, interest and edify the learned, and instruct and enlighten those in the humbler walks of life. [^10]

137The Prospectus contained an indication of the mission of the Star and the specific subject matter it would be devoted to.

1371."The spread of the fullness of the gospel."

1372."The restoration of ancient principles of Christianity."

1373."The gathering of Israel."

1374."The rolling forth of the Kingdom of God among the nations."

1375."The signs of the times."

1376."The fulfillment of prophecy."

1377."recording the judgments of God as they befall the nationsÂ�, whatever is shown indicative of the coming of the Son of Man, and the ushering in of his universal reign on the earth."

1389."As an Ancient Record has lately been discovered in America, we shall give some extracts from time to time as will be most interesting to the lovers of truth."

13810."Extracts from some remarkable visions and revelations which have been given to the saints in this age." [^11]

138Elder Pratt expressed his heartfelt joy in issuing the first number of the Star,

138that luminary, which, rightly conducted, may be a means in the hand of God, of breaking the slumber and silence of midnight darkness, which, like a gloomy cloud, has long hung over the moral horizon-of dispelling the mists of error and superstition which have darkened the understanding and benumbed and blunted every great and noble faculty of the soul-and of kindling a spark of light in the hearts of thousands, which will at length blaze forth, and light up the dawn of that bright day which was seen afar off by holy men of old-the Sabbath of Creation. [^12]

138To achieve his lofty goals, Elder Pratt laid down some guiding principles for the Star:

138We shall pursue a straightforward, bold, and fearless course, without turning a hair's-breath to the right or left from the principles of truth, to court a smile or shun a frown. We shall not be careful to inquire what will be popular or unpopular-what will please or displease, but, what is truth; and when we discern that a principle is true, and will benefit mankind, we shall publish it, even if it were to come in contact with the opinions of all Christendom.

138In our style, we shall endeavour to be plain and simple, as our principles are designed for the benefit of all classes of society. In short, we hope, by the aid and assistance of the Spirit of God, to comfort the mourner-to bind up the broken-hearted-to preach the gospel to the poor-to bring glad tidings to the meek; and "that those who have erred in spirit may come to understanding, and those who have murmured may learn doctrine." [^13]

Befitting the spirit of the brethren, the opening article in the first issue was entitled "The Millennium." It reviewed the teachings of the biblical prophets on the periods ushered in by the restoration of Israel, the rebuilding of Jerusalem, and the Second Coming of the Lord Jesus Christ. Included are teachings on the destruction of the wicked, the establishment of the Lord's universal kingdom, and union of the righteous with their immortal and mortal worlds. Peace, joy, love, and glory will fill the earth. "Such then, is the great Millennium of which our little `Star' would fain announce the dawn." [^14]

139Elder Pratt's work on the Star had to be suspended in July when he received a letter from his family telling him of serious illness and the threat to life from scarlet fever. On advice of the rest of the Quorum he embarked for New York. In October he returned with his family and resumed his duties. [^15] During his absence, Elders Brigham Young and Willard Richards assumed the editing duties of the Star. Under the date July 12, 1840, Brigham Young recorded the following:

139Soon after our Conference brother P. P. Pratt started for America, to bring his family to England, and I took charge of the Millennial Star, and edited the same, assisted by brother W. Richards. I was much confined to the office for several months, proofreading the Hymn Book, conducting and issuing the Millennial Star, Hymn Book and Book of Mormon, giving counsel to the Elders throughout the European Mission, preaching, baptizing and confirming. [^16]

139At the end of the first year the brethren attached a preface to the first bound volumes (April 17, 1841). In it they claimed success in penetrating the thick darkness and the mists of error by the help of God. They also wrote of their desire for the Star in history, a message intended for our day and especially an event such as this commemoration-

139to hand down to posterity a journal, which shall stand when wickedness is overthrown, and shine forth as a monument of truth, amid the wreck of error, and the crush of thrones, that ages to come may read with astonishment and admiration the history and progress of that mighty revolution which has now commenced, and which will then have been consummated, to the joy and satisfaction of the whole earth.

The nations of them which are saved will then look back through these pages and contemplate the acts, the scenes, and the events of other and different times. And what will they behold? They will see in 1830 "a cloud like a man's hand", or in other words, a church of six members rising from obscurity, and coming forth from the wilderness. The curtain falls, and opens upon 1838Â�1840Â�the 20th century. And what is then beheld? A great multitude which no man can number-peace smiles around them, and truth is shining in their midst. They are the church, which 70 years before consisted of six members. Such then is the interest with which posterity will peruse these pages. [^17]

140Undertaking the task of producing the Journal in England in May of 1840 must be viewed with astonishment and admiration. So also is the effect on its history of a disciple of the restoration. From the Prospectus through successive decades, the sense of destiny is evident in the early brethren. One is overwhelmed by the bravado with which they proceeded. They were on the errand of the Lord. They had a kingdom to build, a world to prepare, and sheep to be gathered in. Having set their hand to the plow, they set their sight always forward.

The Parley P. Pratt Years

Elder Pratt, editor and publisher during the first two and one half years of the journal's life, established a solid foundation for the Star; it continued under the same format, style, and mission for at least ninety years. [^18] In the second year, the headquarters of the Star were moved from 47 Oxford Street, Manchester, to 36 Chapel Street in Liverpool.

140In keeping with the objectives identified in the Prospectus, Elder Pratt included at least eight articles that monitored developments among the Jews throughout the world, including events in Jerusalem. He recorded the activities of Orson Hyde's monumental mission to Jerusalem. Several of Elder Hyde's letters, including his experiences in dedicating that hallowed land and his dedicatory prayer, were published in full.

Many of the revelations composing Latter-day Saint scriptures were reproduced for the readers of the Star. From the Doctrine and Covenants Elder Pratt reproduced all or parts of eight of the longest and most loved sections (20, 107, 84, 45, 88, 76, 124, & 133). D&C 101 from the 1835 edition (on marriage) and the first four Lectures on Faith were also included. From the Book of Mormon , Elder Pratt reproduced chapters 8-18 of Third Nephi, the 8th chapter of Moroni, and the testimony of the Three Witnesses.

141He also published "The Prophecy of Enoch," which later became chapter seven of the Book of Moses; and the full text of what is now the Book of Abraham, including facsimile #1. Oliver Cowdery's account of the visit of Moroni and his instructions about the gold plates were included in four parts. There were also two segments from the "History of Joseph Smith."

141Elder Pratt reported many successful encounters of the brethren as they publicly defended the faith across the world. He included responses to numerous published attacks against the church. Detailed reports of conferences held in the branches of the Church were included. Catastrophic and unusual events from all parts of the globe that seemed to be related to the prophecies of the last days were regularly reported. Excerpts from letters of missionaries in the British Isles and from wives or friends in Nauvoo or other parts of the states were always included.

141Upon leaving his post after two and a half years as editor of the Star, Elder Pratt published an affectionate farewell address (October 1842). In it he recounted his efforts as editor and founder of the Star, declaring that he had labored diligently to be true to the charge given him, and had earnestly desired that the Star be a useful and interesting periodical, true to the principles of the restored Gospel:

141How far I have succeeded in these endeavours, I leave for my readers to determine, and for the fruits to bear witness. I felt great satisfaction in a review of my editorial course: I feel my conscience clear, and a secret whispering within, that I have done my duty faithfully before God. [^19]

142Excerpts from some of the precious items printed in the Pratt years will add color to this brief overview. A letter from a prominently known English convert sometime after his gathering to Nauvoo gave the British Saints comforting assurance and encouragement about the gathering.

142I know there are many in the church that are waiting for a word from me, and I am desirous of giving my testimony to these things as far as I know.-As respects Joseph Smith, I have been acquainted with him for some time, and as far as I can see he is a good man, and I find him to be a prophet of the Lord, and the more I speak with him and the more I hear him, the more I am convinced of the truth of these things. I have heard him speak of the mysteries of the kingdom, and these things cause my heart to rejoice. As respects the land it is extremely good, and there is every prospect of being happy, having the things of this world and every opportunity of receiving the things of another.

142My fellow-Englishmen and brethren, you may rely upon what I say, for it would be nothing to my profit for me to deceive you, then believe me when I say this land is good-the things that have been taught you are true, and Joseph Smith is a prophet of the most High. [^20]

142In a letter addressed to the "Traveling High Council and Elders" of the Church in Great Britain, Joseph Smith shared some personal feelings related to the work in Britain and the events the Star was devoted to:

142Be assured beloved brethern, that I am no disinterested observer of the things which are transpiring on the face of the whole earth; and amidst the general movements which are in progress, none is of more importance than the glorious work in which you are now engaged.

142The spread of the gospel throughout England is certainly pleasing; the contemplation of which cannot but afford feelings of no ordinary kind in the bosom of those who have borne the heat and burthen of the day.

142I feel desirous that every providential opening of the kind should be filled, and that you should prior to leaving England, send the gospel into as many parts as you possibly can.

Beloved brethren, you must be aware in some measure, of my feelings, when I contemplate the great work which is now rolling on, and the relationship which I sustain to it, while it is extending to distant lands and Islands, and thousands are embracing it-I realize in some measure my responsibility and the need I have of support from above, and wisdom from on high, that I may be able to teach this people, which have now become a great people, the principles of righteousness, and lead them agreeably to the will of heaven. [^21]

143Because of the revelations and inspired instruction it contained, and the fact that the Doctrine and Covenants had not been available to the British Saints, the Star almost took on the importance of scripture. [^22] Impatient at the lack of enthusiasm for the published works among the saints, Elder Pratt warned of the probable discontinuance of the Star:

143The fact is, the Lord is not well pleased that his word published among the inhabitants of England should be so little sought after, and so long neglected; and this will apply to thousands of Saints, who neither purchase the Star nor the Book of Mormon, and yet are able to purchase needless ornaments, and to adorn themselves with the vain and foolish fashions of the world. [^23]

After the Pratt Years

The first transition following the Pratt years was critical to the Star's success; it presents a good picture of a major aspect of the historical character of the journal. In those years is seen the exchange and cooperation between veteran church leaders, usually apostles, and devoted assistants, usually converts and laymen, who stretched their muscles to fill assignments almost beyond their ability. While the Star was the official mission publication and therefore under the wing of the mission presidents, they differed in their active work at editing and writing for it. Their activity determined the involvement of supportive assistants in the Star office.

In the editorial career of Elder Thomas Ward is seen this interesting dynamic of the Millennial Star and the British or European Mission. Elder Ward followed Elders Heber C. Kimball, Joseph Fielding, Brigham Young and Parley P. Pratt. He was succeeded by Elders Reuben Hedlock, Wilford Woodruff, Orson Hyde, and Franklin D. Richards. Thomas Ward was a British convert of the first months of the mission. He was Mission President from October 1842 to November 1843. He diligently sought to follow Elder Pratt's example and practices and was the sole editor or the "practical editor" [^24]

144Thomas Ward had assisted Elder Pratt for several months and became editor of the Star upon the departure of Elder Pratt. His editorial in his first issue (November 1842) manifested his devotion to the same principles that motivated the Twelve and Elder Pratt:

144The interests of the church of Jesus Christ are widely different from those of any of the sects of the day; our success is not limited to a certain locality, or to the prosperity of a certain school of doctrine in connexion with others of different tenets, but to the advancement of the principles of truth, by which the Saints of the Most High must be gathered together, and on which they must be built up into a nation and people, to receive the laws of heaven for their guidance; and by obedience to which a remnant from amongst all people shall be prepared to meet the Son of Man at his coming. Having, then, an object in view which affects all nations, and in which all mankind are interested for good or evil, our readers will see the necessity of a periodical through whose columns the progress of the kingdom of God can be made known, as also the laws and revelations given from time to time for our guidance by the authorities to the church, can be communicated. [^25]

144Elder Ward published the same variety of material he was accustomed to seeing in the Pratt years-the same attention to world events, events in the growth of the Church among the British, conferences, correspondence, and revelations or epistles from the headquarters of the Church. In addition, there were many installments in the "History of Joseph Smith."

As the Church expanded in the British Isles, so did demands on the operations of the mission and the office and staff of the Star. Many of these were literary contributions, including important histories (History of Joseph Smith & Manuscript History of Brigham Young), and doctrinal works by prominent church leaders (Parley and Orson Pratt, Orson Spencer, Franklin D. Richards, George Q. Cannon, B. H. Roberts, John A. Widtsoe, etc.). There were also the Journal of Discourses and several mission periodicals in other European countries and Australia.

145Other projects, related to the programs and teachings of the Church, created distractions from and complications to normal operations. Emigration was encouraged but was difficult to accomplish. The mission and the Star were active in making arrangements for the Saints. The Joint Stock Company, formed to aid in the emigration movement, developed into a serious controversy that cast a dark shadow on the careers of Reuben Hedlock and Thomas Ward. This enterprise occupied many pages of the Star in 1845-46. One history of the Star, some eighty years later, summarized the incident as follows:

145The ostensible purpose appeared at first to have the approval of the Twelve, and no doubt much good was hoped to result from it. It, however, developed into a money-getting enterprise, enlisting the thought and energies of elders, whose calling as missionaries to preach the gospel was subordinated to the affairs of the company, by which means the spirit of their missions was largely withdrawn, and lapses and errors crept in to produce ill-will and cause the downfall of men, who had enjoyed the love and confidence of the authorities and members of the Church in a high degree. Among these were Elders Hedlock and Ward. The latter, however, showing a spirit of humility and goodwill, was soon commended by Elder Hyde, who wound up the company, and later on we believe Elder Hedlock also recovered his standing in the Church. [^26]

145This report would be remiss if it failed to recognize the diligent work of many laymen. Thomas Ward, William L. Davies, James Foggo and dozens like them assisted the brethren and kept the Star alive through its many leadership changes.

Interesting Anecdotes of the Early Years

The British Saints had been asked to assist in the collection of funds for construction of the new Nauvoo Temple. Various schemes were proposed and followed. One of interest was published in the minutes of the Clitheroe conference in 1843:

146Elder Heap from Nauvoo, and elder Barlow then addressed the Saints, and pressed upon them the necessity of straining every nerve to forward the building of the temple of God at Nauvoo. William Walker, priest, spoke very humourously upon laying the tea pot aside, and calling it the temple pot, and also the tobacco box, and designate it the temple box and by these little matters great things might be achieved. [^27]

146The January issue of 1844 reports the trial of two missionaries on the charge of manslaughter. The incidents caused an editorial of caution and instruction in the ordinance of baptism.

It is with feelings of no ordinary kind, that we address you on the present occasion, relative to the administration of the ordinance of baptism. Whereas, two fatal accidents have lately occurred, in connexion with this ordinance, one at Crewe, in Cheshire, and the other near Sheffield, whereby two individuals have been drowned. We, therefore, strenuously urge upon the attention of the elders and priests of the church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, that they use every precaution in attending to the all-important ordinance of baptism for the remission of sins, and be not over anxious, so as to endanger themselves or the candidate, in any way whatever. Remember, that as the servants of the Lord, we are under great responsibility how we administer, and should exercise our judgments in all matters pertaining to our duty and calling, and not do anything that will bring the cause of God into disrepute, or endanger the lives of our fellow-men: the Lord does not require such things at our hands. The apostle Paul, in speaking of the ordinances and gifts, says, "Let all things be done decently, and in order;" therefore, the candidate should be properly clothed, on such occasions, for the sake of decency and convenience in administering the ordinance. We sincerely hope that the Saints will take warning by the late lamentable accidents, and let a word to the wise be sufficient, neglecting nothing with regard to the fitness of time, place, or appearance. [^28]

147Often we envision the early missionaries confronting the people of the large cities of Britain in public areas where public preaching was permitted. I feared that scene very much when I left for my mission to the British Isles, yet, like thousands before me, and so I resigned myself to such a display should the occasion ever arise. It didn't, yet I still took comfort from this brief assessment of that system. Elder Thomas Margetts, in a letter to the editor appearing in the July 1844 issue, related these sentiments:

147I will here remark, that when I was first called to the priesthood, I thought my mission was to expose the errors of the religious systems of the day, and that I must use my best endeavours to make manifest the deformities of the Babylon that obtains in the name of christianity at the present time; and I have often stood and borne testimony against her for an hour together, and said but very little about the glorious gospel of our Lord Jesus Christ.

147But let me speak of the feelings of my bosom after I had thus preached, and by those feelings I have an evidence that I was wrong altogether; and I trust that these few remarks may prove a benefit to others that are young in the priesthood; for as I have felt the fire, I am enabled to warn others, lest they also be burnt as well as myself After I had done speaking, instead of feeling that glow of joy and consolation in my heart which I should have done, my mind was dark and gloomy, and I felt as if I had done wrong, though I knew not at that time wherein. I used to rebuke this influence, thinking it to be of Satan, until I discovered the cause of it, which was that I had not been doing that for which I had been sent, which was to preach the gospel, instead of railing against the sects of the day.

147From that time I took a different course altogether, and I found to my greatest satisfaction that God was with me; that he prospered my labours, and gave success to the preaching of his word. [^29]

Behind the success of the Millennial Star is the momentum infused into it by the apostolic mission of 1840 and the workings of the Holy Spirit in directing the mission to the British Isles as part of the mission of the whole dispensation. As an indication of the aspects of momentum and continuity in the Star, I close with the following observation from an early history of the star:

148Within its pages appear tracts and treatises, poems and hymns, sermons and lectures; correspondence from all the missions, and reports of conferences, in which the names of well nigh all the elders of Israel, who have attained distinction in the service of the Lord in this dispensation, are found bearing witness to the restoration of the gospel and the progress of the Church in preparation for the Millennium, the reign of peace-the great Sabbath of Creation. Paraphrasing the words of the first editor: We are aware of the greatness of the undertaking in conducting such a review; for it is indeed like entering upon "a boundless field-a shoreless ocean

148-a fathomless deep" in which all that he anticipated has been gloriously realized in the abundance and variety of the contents of this periodical, in the inspiration and high quality of the many writers, whose best literary work adorns its pages. [^30]
