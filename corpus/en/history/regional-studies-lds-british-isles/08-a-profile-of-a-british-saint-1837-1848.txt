Various authors, Regional Studies in LDS History: British Isles ( 1990)

A Profile of a British Saint 1837-1848

Susan Easton Black

Most historical writing on the Church in the British Isles emphasizes the American missionary, rather than the British convert. It focuses on the missionary's sacrifice, his abject poverty, his sacred manifestations, and his successful baptismal record. Such writing portrays "American gospel heros" in Britain, but fails to communicate the magnitude of the contribution made by the individual English convert.

103For example, Heber C. Kimball has become an "American gospel hero" in Britain. As historians reiterate his conversion of Englishmen, they consistently mention his first convert, George Watt. [^1] Yet they fail to name the other eight baptized on 30 July 1837 or the baptisms recorded on other days. It appears their American focus has left British saints nearly devoid of "British gospel heros."

103When an Englishman is mentioned who has "heroic" tributes, an American is mentioned whose deeds appear to supersede those of the Englishman. An example of this occurring is James Fielding, [^2] the man who first opened his church in Preston, England for the preaching of the gospel. 104With the general lack of historic recognition of the British saints, the personal history of Church members in Britain is left to American-born descendants to tell. They repeat from generation to generation the unwritten stories of their ancestors. Through this process they have often enlarged upon facts and created moralistic fables and traditional legends of their ancestry. Through their efforts the legendary "British gospel hero" has often emerged as less than a credible figure.

104Perhaps it is not farfetched to conclude the following account, of the baptism of George Watt, in another generation or two could be viewed as authentic, faith promoting, and accurate. "Few of us recall the details of July 30th of 1837 at the River Ribble, but I know well the details because my ancestor was George Watt. At 9 o'clock on Sunday morning near the River, two of the nine baptismal candidates lined up for a foot race to the water's edge. It may have been a 24 mile marathon to their destination, but no less than a 6K. When the starter gun signaled the beginning of the race, the well-laid out track which charted the runners' destination, was surrounded by a cheering crowd of 9,000 breathlessly awaiting the victor. Speculation and even wagers were being called from the crowd as everyone hoped his favorite would win. George Watt was the younger man and more sure of foot. He easily out ran the other and won first place. As the cheering of the crowd ceased, they reverently watched as the first man in Britain was baptized."

"Dramatic, but impossible" cautions another living descendant. "No one will believe this story unless its details are verified." However, we do tend to believe and accept the less dramatic family stories of Sarah Ann Durnford [^6] [^7] [^8]

105Examination of available records confirms only a portion of the descendant's story of Sarah Ann Durnford. Sarah Ann and her brothers, Arthur and Edward, did join the Church in England and did emigrate to America. Their names are listed as passengers aboard the "Amazon." [^9] However, she was a not member of the Daniel McArthur Handcart Company. [^10] On the journey west, she did wear out one pair of her shoes, but arrived in the Salt Lake Valley wearing the second pair. [^11]

105The descendant's legendary account of William Towler's conversion begins with missionaries singing on the street corners of Wales. William, impressed with their music, eventually accompanied them with his coronet. His musical talent was readily noticed by Brigham Young, who agreed to pay his way to America, if he would continue his talent. William accepted his offer and enthusiastically boarded a train at Cardiff for Liverpool and headed for the adventures that awaited him in the Americas. William's girlfriend Charlotte Stephens, informed of his emigration plans, came to the train. Unfortunately, she was stronger than William and yanked him from the train. William never came to America but did marry Charlotte. [^12]

Some of the more noteworthy parts of this story, when checked by documented sources, cannot be confirmed. The records do confirm William Towler's membership in the Church. They also indicate William as a member of the Welsh band. [^13] [^14]

106Year after year, as the stories of Sarah Ann and William are told and retold by their posterity, they become more clever, more dramatic, and more glorified. Although these stories lack reference to prayer, the Book of Mormon, testimony, or faith, they do demonstrate determination, sacrifice, and enthusiasm. These qualities are noteworthy, yet they don't explain why Sarah or William accepted the gospel in the British Isles.

106Scorning or mocking the descendants' tendency to "huff and puff" as they elaborate on such stories, is not the answer. Such criticism can just as readily be turned on the written accounts of the professional historian. A trend in historiography is increasingly leading to demographic studies, which attempt to acknowledge the followers, the common folk, and the typical. Yet despite the recent demand for more information on the ordinary members, few Church historians have attempted to highlight a common saint in the British Isles. Where is the historian who has written of the ordinary British saint? Church historians have viewed a portrayal of the most common followers as of slight interest when compared to the "American gospel hero" or even the legendary "British gospel hero."

106Instead of attempting to produce a documented biographical account of the early English converts, the historians have clustered them into statistical formats. This grouping has often resulted in impressive but undocumented numbers, which lack a carefully researched empirical basis. These statistical generalities appear to be just as contrived as the descendants' exaggerated reminiscing about the lives of their ancestors.

107Examples of such exaggerated statistical stipulations without empirical foundation are as follows: "After the initial baptism of George Watt, Heber C. Kimball continued to baptize. By April of 1838 he `had converted 1,500 Britishers.'" [^15] [^16] [^17] [^18] [^19]

107This rhetorical and statistical form of reporting about common membership of the Church lacks foundation. Is it a coincidence that all numbers are rounded to the nearest zero, and in some cases rounded up to the nearest thousand? The baptismal success ratio was impressive to Willard Richards, who wrote to the editor of the Millennial Star on 15 May 1840 from Ledbury, Herefordshire, England, "There are many doors open which we cannot fill; calls for preaching on almost every hand which we cannot answer. Oh! that the Saints would pray to the Lord of the harvest to send forth labourers!" [^20]

107But the majesty of the accomplishment is not enhanced by exaggerated and vague reporting. To question a statistic that has been reproduced in historical text may be viewed by some as heretical. Nevertheless, the Church historians in the British Isles should not be satisfied with anything less.

Documented records of British Church history have always been available to historians. For example, in Wilford Woodruff's meticulous diary he summarizes with impressive accuracy his experiences in southern England during the year of 1840: "The baptisms of the year were 336 persons under my own hands, and I assisted at the baptism of 86 others. I baptized 57 preachers,Â�also two clerks of the Church of England." [^21]

108As journals, branch records, ship lists, ordinance data, parish records, etc., are searched, factual data provides objective and credible information. These facts uplift and inspire, without expansive editorial narrative. They emphasize typical achievements rather than exceptional ones. They move the focus from the relatively few American missionaries in Britain to the people who accepted the gospel. Through documentary research a less dramatic, but more realistic, picture emerges. It is a convert legacy, whose strength appears to pale in comparison with the "hero," but leaves a lasting impression of England's gospel heritage.

The Documented Solution

The documented view of the British convert is just a beginning. In actuality, any hope of a history containing detailed information on all early British converts is merely an idealistic historian's dream and a zealous descendant's disappointment. Yet there is now emerging a glimpse not seen previously by historians or descendants. A documented beginning of the history of the membership in the early British Church is now possible because of a newly published fifty-volume reference set entitled Membership of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints: 1830-1848. [^22]

109This documented research enables historians and descendants to gain a clearer and historically verified picture of an individual British saint and his family. For example, if the historian wanted to trace what happened to the William Owens' family after they emigrated to America, details are available. He would learn that for seven weeks the family sailed across the Atlantic Ocean until they arrived in New Orleans. From New Orleans they traveled up the Mississippi River and the Missouri River to St. Joseph, Missouri. Enroute they contracted cholera, a violent disease, which caused the death of six of the family members. On 7 May 1849 Jane Owens died and was buried on the banks of the Mississippi River. William, son of William Owens, died on 8 May 1849. Alice died on the same day as her brother, 8 May 1849. Eleanor Owens, the mother of the children, died on 9 May 1849. William Owens, the father, died on 12 May 1849, and Richard Owens died on 18 May 1849. The deaths of these family members, occurred in a tragically rapid succession: Jane died on Monday, William and Alice on Tuesday, the mother on Wednesday, and the father on Friday, all of the same week; and Richard died on Friday of the following week. [^23]

The research volumes can also be used to help historians develop more accurate statistics. As an example, 3,781 members of the Church, from 1830 to 1848, were randomly selected from these volumes for statistical analysis. Of these 3,781 selected saints, 1,063 were born in the British Isles. This figure indicates that by 1848 28 percent of the membership of the Church had their origins in the British Isles.

As the proselyting efforts continued in England from 1837 to 1848, the key years of conversion were from 1840 to 1842. During these years the Quorum of the Twelve served their mission in Britain. However, the peak years for baptizing in Britain occurred after the death of Joseph, in 1847-1848.

Once baptized, the saints attended conferences held in the British Isles. The conferences most frequently attended were held in Manchester, England. The other conferences which attracted large numbers were Liverpool, Sheffield, and Birmingham. Conference attendance verifies that the greatest missionary efforts were occurring in the northwest section of England and not in Ireland, Wales, or Scotland.

Of the representative English membership of 1,063, only 202 individuals emigrated to America. Thus, this emigration movement included only 19 percent of the English converts. The main reason why the others did not emigrate was that 245 were cut off from the Church. Of those cut off, 61 have listed the reasons. The most predominant reason was "neglect," second "bad conduct," third "apostasy," and fourth "adultery or fornication." Less typical reasons for being cut off included being "out of harmony," "swearing," and "not cleaning her house." Another major reason why 117 of the British saints didn't emigrate was death. Unfortunately, 499 persons are unaccounted for following their baptism. This falling away or misplacing of records of 47 percent of the original British converts is a historical tragedy. As a result, the real story of nearly half of the British converts from 1837-1848 may never be known.

If the present random selection is indicative of the total picture, there were approximately 31,500 known Saints baptized from 1830 to 1848 throughout the world. Missionaries who labored in the British Isles converted 8,858, making the British membership of the Church 28 percent of the entire membership. Of this British membership, 8 percent emigrated before 1849.

With a clearer perspective of the statistics available on British converts, it is easier to focus on a representative Saint randomly selected from the 1,063 British born saints who joined the Church from 1837-1848. The most typical member was a woman by the name of Mary who was thirty years old when baptized. Her marital status following her baptism was reported only sporadically on membership lists. Because she did not leave a journal, her biographical information is sketchy. However, it is known that she most likely was a resident of Lancashire, England. She was most commonly employed as a cotton spinner, dress maker, or housekeeper. Her unskilled vocation was reflected in her impoverished economic status. She joined the Church while she was employed. Joining was not an isolated decision on her part; members of her extended family, not necessarily the immediate family, also joined at the same time. Her activity at local branch functions and conferences was minimal. She did not receive leadership callings and appears to have been a quiet follower. She did not emigrate to America. Unfortunately, it does not appear that her posterity continued in the Church.

Conclusion

Balanced historical writing on the Church in the British Isles needs to emphasize the British convert. In the past, writings by professional historians have focused on the contribution of the American missionary and the statistical "guesstimates" of conversions and emigrations. This has been coupled with descendants repeating legendary stories of their ancestors. The history that needs to be written is of the almost forgotten British Saint. This history may not appear as detailed or dramatic as the stories of the "American gospel hero" or even the legendary "British gospel hero," but it must be told.

It is disappointing that the typical saint does not rise to the sometimes illustrious, but often unbelievable, majesty of the legendary convert. It is equally disappointing that the documented statistics indicate a conversion story mixed with separation from the Church. Yet these early converts in Britain were the first to accept the Gospel. As such, they made a decision and many made a sacrifice which gave the Church a vital British heritage. This heritage indicates that "Mary" was the first to accept the gospel, but unfortunately she has been the last to be remembered.
