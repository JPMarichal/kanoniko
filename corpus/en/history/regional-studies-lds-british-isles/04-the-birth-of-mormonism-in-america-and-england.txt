Various authors, Regional Studies in LDS History: British Isles ( 1990)

The Birth of Mormonism in America and England

Milton V. Backman, Jr.

For approximately four years, Wilford Woodruff, a farm boy living in upstate New York, was involved in a quest for religious truth. "I had no desire to join any of the sectarian Churches," he wrote. Because of a "falling away," the pure "Church of Christ was in the wilderness." "I was looking," he continued, "for the Church to arise and to again be established upon the foundation of the ancient Apostles and Prophets." On December 29, 1833, he met a Mormon missionary, Zera Pulsipher, and listened intently to the restoration message delivered by this elder. Immediately, he testified, "I felt the spirit of GodÂ�. bear witness that he was the servant of God." After he had received a copy of the Book of Mormon, that same spirit bore witness to him of the truthfulness of that sacred record. Two days after meeting Elder Pulsipher, Wilford Woodruff joined the Restored Church. [^1]

Seven years later, on March 5, 1840, Elder Wilford Woodruff proclaimed this same message of restoration to United Brethren congregations in Herefordshire, England. These former Primitive Methodists, numbering about four hundred, were divided into small branches scattered in an area covering about fifteen or twenty miles. Under the leadership of Thomas Kington, they "were praying for [increased] light and truth." [^2] According to Elder Woodruff, Kington received his testimony with candor and made it a subject of fervent prayer. "The Lord manifest the truth of it unto him," Elder Woodruff testified, after which Kington and all of his household were baptized. Wilford Woodruff "continued to preach and baptize daily," and within thirty-six days he had baptized more than 150 members of the United Brethren society, including forty preachers. Summarizing this experience, Elder Woodruff noted in his diary, "This has opened the largest field for labour & increase of numbers of any door that has been opened in the same length of time since the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter Day Saints had been established." [^3] He later recalled his unusual success as a missionary in England by saying that all but one member of the United Brethren church united with the Latter-day Saint. [^4] Then to emphasize that he and others were governed by revelations from God, Elder Woodruff observed that altogether he (probably meaning he and his missionary companions) baptized some 1800 individuals in that field of labor. [^5]

2 The religious experiences of Wilford Woodruff, especially his conversion and missionary success, were similar to those of many other Latter-day Saints. In 1850 a high percentage of members of the Restored Church united with this movement in North America or Great Britain. During the first seven years of the history of Mormonism, nearly all converts were citizens of the United States or Canada, and many of the Canadian Saints were recent immigrants from England. After the restored gospel spread overseas in 1837, the Church increased more rapidly in England than in any other land. This pattern of growth continued until the middle of the eighteenth century. In 1851, when Mormonism reached a membership peak in England for that century, there were almost 33,000 members there, representing 63 percent of the total Church membership. After more than 5,000 converts from England had migrated to America, there were still more members in that land than in the Great Basin of North America. [^6]

3 Although from 1837 to 1851 England was the land of the greatest missionary success, the main gathering place for Latter-day Saints was America. Nephi had predicted that the gospel would be restored in the new nation ( 1 Ne. 13:12-18 ; 3:6, 12-15), and this land was possibly the only place in the world at that time where the Saints could have assembled in large numbers to build temples and maintain doctrinal and organizational uniformity.

3 There are not only meaningful parallels in patterns of growth of the Restored Church in England and the United States, but there are striking similarities in forces which prepared people in these two nations for the restoration of the everlasting gospel.

Growth of Toleration and Birth of Religious Liberty

One of the most important precursors to the restoration in the young republic and England was the growth of religious freedom. Although the process of establishing genuine religious liberty in these two lands was slow and was marred by innumerable episodes of intolerance, toleration had become a legal reality in both countries before 1830. The spirit of toleration and religious liberty was stronger in North America and Britain during the third decade of the nineteenth century than in any other portion of the Christian world.

Early English immigrants transplanted to the Thirteen Colonies both intolerance and seeds of liberty. At the time of the English colonization of America and for decades thereafter, most Europeans associated religious uniformity with the preservation of political tranquility and social cohesion. In order to maintain harmony in society, leaders generally believed that the state should suppress heresy and prevent the rise of dissent. There was also a strong belief that since religion was desirable and needed the support of the state, inhabitants should be required to pay taxes to maintain an established church.

4 The belief that the state should support one legal religion was also introduced by early immigrants to the New World. While religious uniformity was firmly established in all North American English colonies founded before 1633, dissenters from England found a refuge in the American wilderness. [^7] In the third decade of the seventeenth century, toleration and religious liberty appeared in isolated settlements. Persecuted English Catholics initiated this movement by establishing in Maryland a haven where all Trinitarian Christians could worship in peace. And shortly after the founding of Maryland, Roger Williams fled from the intolerance of Massachusetts and established the principle of religious liberty in Rhode Island. [^8]

One of the major forces weakening religious solidarity in America and opening the gates for religious diversity was the American wilderness. While there was an abundance of people and scarcity of land in Europe, the opposite was true in America. As settlers scattered into the frontier, the arm of the law weakened, and efforts to maintain uniformity and an effective religious establishment crumbled. Policies and programs of leaders in England also promoted the growth of religious pluralism in America. Mercantilists, for example, supported colonization because they anticipated that colonies would provide England with essential raw materials and would serve as a market for their manufactured goods. Some English leaders also believed that pressures caused by a rapidly growing population could be partially alleviated by sending the "surplus population" to the New World. Others regarded America as a convenient dumping ground for undesirable inhabitants, such as persecuted Quakers. Therefore, rather than restrict emigration to orthodox members of an established church, as was the policy of the French, Spanish, Dutch and Scandinavians, English leaders sought to strengthen their provinces with nonconformists of various Protestant persuasions.

5Following the English Civil War in the midseventeenth century, English leaders launched a major program of colonial expansion. Between 1660 and 1685, England conquered New Netherlands, thereby occupying the Middle Colonies, and expanded south of Virginia into what became the Carolinas. In addition to formulating an effective plan of expansion, leaders decided to strengthen their settlements by encouraging non-English Protestants to migrate to North America. Moreover, to discourage emigration from the Middle Colonies of Dutch and Scandinavian settlers who had been conquered in 1664, the English guaranteed toleration to Lutherans and members of Reformed churches. During the latter half of the seventeenth century, leaders in England decided that strengthening the empire was more important than maintaining the religious uniformity that prevailed in the mother country.

Among the most important influences advancing toleration in early America were policies, programs and laws adopted in England. Policies employed for governing colonies were eventually incorporated into the laws of England. One of the milestones along the path toward religious liberty in England and her colonies was the Toleration Act, passed in 1689. Following the Glorious Revolution, Parliament passed a law providing Trinitarian Protestants the right of worship, providing their ministers and chapels were licensed and their services held behind unlocked doors. [^9] [^10] [^11]

6Although the Toleration Act of 1689 opened the door for increased pluralism in England and her colonies, it did not immediately lead to a growth of dissent in the mother country. In 1700 there were about 330,000 dissenters in England, representing about 6 percent of the population. Forty years later the number had declined by about one-half, and the percentage of the population belonging to non-Anglican churches dropped to less than 3 percent. During this period, there was relatively no growth among Baptists and Independents (Congregationalists) and there was a decline of possibly fifty percent among Roman Catholics and Friends (Quakers). Meanwhile, most Presbyterians (the largest non-Anglican group in 1700) abandoned their faith in the eighteenth century. Some of these former Calvinists embraced Arminianism, then Arianism, and later became Unitarians. [^12] Historians have also estimated that of the 150,000 associated with communities of dissenters in 1740, possibly no more than 50,000 were fully committed members. [^13]

6Partly because of decreasing religious dissent and the subsequent lack of pressure on the government to advance toleration, the Toleration Act of 1689 did not lead immediately to the passage of other laws advancing liberty. Meanwhile, this act left dissenters in an inferior legal position by failing to repeal the Corporation Act of 1661 and the Test Act of 1675. According to the former law, all who refused to receive the sacrament according to Anglican rites were prevented from holding municipal office; and by the terms of the latter law, nonconformists were barred from civil and military office in the service of the Crown. [^14]

The rights of nonconformists were further restricted by Parliamentary legislation enacted during the first half of the eighteenth century. The Corporation and Test acts were strengthened in 1711 by legislation forbidding "occasional conformity," a practice that enabled a few dissenters to temporarily fulfill regulations for gaining municipal or Crown office. The Schism Act of 1714 prevented non-Anglicans from "keeping a school," and the Marriage Act of 1753 required nonconformists to participate in Anglican marriage ceremonies. After the failure of dissenting groups to obtain a repeal of the Corporation and Test acts in the 1730s, there was no serious attempt to change these laws for fifty years. [^15]

7Since the Toleration Act limited the right of public worship to Trinitarian Protestants, Latter-day Saints, like Roman Catholics, could not have legally worshipped in public in England nor in the majority of her colonies during most of the eighteenth century. Before the American Revolution, Joseph Smith could not have written that he organized the Church according to the laws of New York. Conditions were not favorable for a restoration until after the passage of additional acts of toleration.

Growth of Pluralism

Between 1770 and 1830, vital seeds of liberty were planted in England and America. Legislation guaranteeing people of various religious persuasions the opportunity to worship in peace showed a significant growth of pluralism in both countries.

7One of the powerful forces that developed from increased denominational pluralism was the religious revivals that swept across England and America about 1739. An attempt by John Wesley to arrest apathy and to reform the Church of England inaugurated the Methodist revolution. While Wesley launched a powerful program of reform, revivalists in America, seeking to advance spirituality, participated in America's first widespread revival, known as the Great Awakening. The increased religious zeal which accompanied both movements fostered controversy, and internal strife precipitated schisms and the formation of new religious communities. [^16]

8Although the growth of pluralism was relatively slow in England and America between 1740 and 1770, during the last three decades of the eighteenth century discontent grew in both lands. By 1800 there was greater denominational diversity in England and the United States than had previously existed in the history of modern Christianity. At the beginning of the nineteenth century, English historians identified more than five hundred dissenting groups that had not existed thirty-five years earlier. [^17] Meanwhile, the most complex religious mosaic in the Christian world was that of the United States. Unlike European and Latin America cultures where religious establishments prevailed, no one faith in any of the young states dominated the religious scene. [^18]

8The fastest growing religious community in both countries during the first four decades of the nineteenth century was the Methodist society. This reform movement gradually separated from the Church of England and was officially organized in the new nation in 1784. By 1801 there were 91,000 members in England and almost 65,000 in the United States. Although this faith spawned many offshoots, the mother church (Wesleyanism in England and the Methodist Episcopal Church in the United States) remained by far the largest branch in both lands. After doubling or almost doubling its membership every ten years, this denomination (including all branches) grew to 288,000 members in England and to more than 500,000 in the United States by 1831. During the 1830s, conversions continued at a remarkable rate, with membership increasing in England to 435,000 (1841) and to almost 900,000 in the United States. [^19]

Even though the growth pattern of Methodism was similar in England and America, the relationship of this church to other faiths was different in the two nations. Since there was an established Church in England, the Anglican faith remained the dominant religion there, and a smaller percent of the people in that land united with the Methodists than in the United States. In 1801 about 1.6 percent of the adult population was Methodist. This figure increased to about 4.5 percent in 1840, the latter estimate being about 2 percent of the population. [^20] [^21]

9Although the Baptist community grew rapidly in England and America after the middle of the eighteenth century, the growth of this denomination was much greater in the new nation than in England. In England, membership in the two largest branches of this family of churches, Particular (Calvinist) Baptists and the General (Free Will) Baptist New Connexion (organized in 1770), increased from 10,000 in 1750 to 27,500 in 1800 and to 100,000 in 1838. [^22] [^23]

9Although the Congregational church in England followed a pattern of growth similar to that of the Baptists and actually increased more rapidly than that body, Congregationalism in America grew slowly during the first half of the nineteenth century. Members of this faith failed to resolve effectively the problem of an expanding population; and since they did not develop effective missionary programs, Congregationalism failed to expand significantly outside New England. [^24]

Rise of New Religious Persuasions

After 1770, there was not only a major increase in England and the United States of faiths whose historical roots reached back beyond the mideighteenth century, but there was also an increase in membership of religious persuasions that were constituted in these lands during the latter half of that century. After gaining strength in the British Isles, Unitarians and Universalists secured strongholds in New England before 1790. [^25] Meanwhile, disciples of Emanuel Swedenborg (a Swedish reformer) formed a society in England in 1783; shortly thereafter, teachings of the New Jerusalem church were introduced in America. [^26]

10The fragmentation of Protestantism in England and America accelerated at the end of the eighteenth and in the early nineteenth centuries. In England, the Methodism generated more religious offshoots than any other faith. Controversies primarily centering on the authoritarian nature of Methodist polity, missionary techniques, and proper interpretations of the Bible led to the formation of new faiths, such as the Methodist New Connexion (founded in 1707), the Bible Christians (organized in 1815), and the Primitive Methodist Church. This latter group was organized in 1811 after Wesleyan Methodists refused to countenance camp meetings, a program introduced in England by an American Methodist, Lorenzo Dow. [^27] The desire of leaders in America for increased lay participation and democracy were also major forces that led to the formation of Methodist offshoots in this country, such as the Christian Church movement led by James O'Kelly (initiated in 1784), the Reformed Methodist Church (organized in 1814), and the Methodist Protestant Church (constituted in 1830). [^28]

Three other groups seeking a purer form of Christianity were formed in England about 1830. As mentioned, not fully satisfied with reformed efforts of Primitive Methodists, Thomas Kington molded a new faith in Herefordshire called the United Brethren. In 1827 an evangelical group who believed that anyone could preach and administer the sacraments was constituted in Dublin. Three years later a former Anglican clergyman, J. N. Darby, transplanted this millennial denomination to Plymouth, England, and this communion adopted the name Plymouth Brethren. [^29] [^30]

11The cultural exchange between England and the United States included the introduction to England of religious communities initially organized in the young republic. Mormonism was not the only faith that expanded beyond the Atlantic. Other restoration movements originating in this land, such as Christians and Disciples, carried their forms of primitive Christianity overseas. Various American versions of evangelical Christianity were also planted in England by free-lance revivalist preachers, such as Lorenzo Dow, Asahel Nettleton, James Carighey, and Charles G. Finney. [^31]

Dissent Advanced Toleration

An expansion of political pressures advancing toleration accompanied the growth of dissent in England and America. Dissenters in England initially gained political strength in the rapidly growing cities. There, nonconformists, representing the old mercantile and the new manufacturing stock, attained wealth; and after becoming a force among the social elite, they gained political potency. [^32] [^33]

12Tendencies that had been gathering momentum for decades were exposed, and a new religious era in the history of the world was inaugurated during the Revolutionary generation. Immediately after gaining control of new state governments, Americans launched an internal revolution. At the time of the creation of free and independent states, the Church of England was a tax-supported faith in all the South and in New York City, and the Congregational church held that same privileged position in all New England, outside of Rhode Island. During the early stages of the War for Independence, all Anglican establishments were crushed; and following the military phase of this war, the Congregational tax-supported churches in New England were weakened. The spirit of religious liberty was so strong and pluralism so pervasive that there was no serious consideration in the new nation of creating an established religion. While the founding fathers were forming new patterns of government, leaders of the young republic enacted laws guaranteeing to all inhabitants, Christians and non-Christians, Trinitarians and non-Trinitarians, the right to organize and worship without interference of the state. [^34]

Although the Anglican church remained an established tax-supported and politically favored religion in England during the first half of the nineteenth century, significant changes just before the birth of Mormonism made it possible for the Restored Church to be legally constituted in that land. In 1791 the Toleration Act was extended to Roman Catholics, granting them freedom to worship in public. [^35] [^36] [^37]

A Reorientation of Christian Thinking

The growth of dissent in England and the United States before the restoration not only advanced toleration and the birth of religious liberty, but precipitated increased doctrinal diversity. As the number of diverse religious communities multiplied, the reorientation of Christian thinking intensified. The restoration was preceded by a Second Reformation, a reformation of Protestantism. During the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, people in England and America debated fervently the correct Biblical teaching concerning the means of salvation, the nature of the Godhead, life beyond the grave, proper subjects and mode of baptism, the Second Coming and millennial reign and characteristics of church government. Since most colonists were Calvinists and members of the Established Church in England embraced free will, in some respects, the Methodist advance precipitated a greater theological revolution in America than in England. Denying predestination, Methodists expounded the belief that man played a vital role in the salvation experience. Wesley also taught that since man could fall, he should constantly strive for perfection. The doctrine of free will was not only proclaimed effectively by Methodists, but by members of all other major faiths that emerged in America during the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries. The new religious groups challenged effectively the predestinarian views espoused by Congregationalists, Presbyterians, most Baptists and Lutherans, and members of the Dutch and German Reformed faiths. Since Presbyterianism declined significantly in eighteenth century England and the Independents (Congregationalists) and Calvinist Baptist faiths were not as strong there as in America, the debate over man's role in the salvation experience was not as intense in that land as in the new nation. Meanwhile, Methodist evangelists constantly reminded people in England and America of their responsibility to seek salvation.

14The Second Reformation in America was primarily an attack on Calvinism and Trinitarianism. On a positive note, it was an advancement of Arminianism (free will) and Arianism. During the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, Unitarians who supported the views of William E. Channing; Christians who embraced the beliefs of Barton Stone; Eastern Christians who followed in the path of Abner Jones, Elias Smith, and David Millard agreed that the Father and Son were two separate and distinct Beings. Rejecting the popular creedal definition that the Godhead consisted of three persons of one essence, early nineteenth century Arian Christians insisted that the oneness of the Godhead was not essence or substance. Instead, at the time of the restoration, many Americans proclaimed that Jesus, though divine, was separate and distinct from the one Supreme Being.

The controversy over the nature of the Godhead was not as intense in England as in America in 1820. Views espoused by American restorationists like Barton Stone and David Millard, and beliefs heralded by reformers like Channing, were not as popular overseas as in the new nation. American Unitarianism, which emerged primarily from Congregationalism, proceeded more slowly in the direction of liberalism than did the English branch of this movement. [^38] [^39]

15Partly because of the more rapid growth of the Baptists in America than in England, the method and subjects of baptism were also, in some respects, a more controversial issue in the new nation than it was overseas. However, most Baptists in America and England were Calvinists, and this belief, along with their lack of a central organization to direct missionary work, interfered with the growth of this movement. Nevertheless, in both lands baptism was a divisive issue that led to theological confrontations.

Strong beliefs in the imminence of the Second Coming and an approaching millennium were other doctrines debated vigorously in America and England at the time of the restoration. These beliefs were not limited to Irvingites or Plymouth Brethren but were embraced by members of the Established Church and most dissenting communities. While there was a noticeable increase in the popularity of these doctrines in England between 1825 and 1830, a major crescendo occurred in the United States between 1833 and 1844. During those years, William Miller prepared others for the Lord's Coming. After an initial disappointment occurred in the spring of 1844, a reevaluation led to a new prediction. When the Lord did not appear on October 22, 1844, a "Great Disappointment" fragmented the movement. [^40]

16The growth of dissent fostered increased debate in the two countries, and the "contest of opinions" created confusion in the minds of many. As individuals listened to the "war of words" and studied the Bible, their quest to unite with the church that best represented New Testament Christianity intensified.

A Quest for Religious Truth

Another major force preparing people in England and America for the restoration of the everlasting gospel was a growing interest in religion and a fervent search for truth. As noted earlier, a significant increase in religious zeal commenced in both countries in 1739. The rise of Methodism and the Great Awakening were forerunners of countless revivals that continued during the remainder of the eighteenth century. This quickening did not weaken in the early nineteenth century but instead gained momentum and reached a new level of enthusiasm during the 1820s, 1830s, and 1840s. While the flames of revivalism burned brightly, large numbers asked the question, "Which church should I join?" This question was followed by decisions, and many people united with different religious communities. [^41]

At the time of the restoration there were significant increases in church membership in the United States and England. In 1800 there were probably more people in the new nation who were not affiliated with a religious community than in any other Christian land. As people migrated westward, many lost contact with organized religion. Primarily because the population was scattered and most Americans lived on isolated farms, most inhabitants did not live near a meeting house. The westward expansion accelerated after the American Revolution, and in 1800 only about 7 percent of the population was churched. As Methodist circuit riders, Baptist preachers, Presbyterian ministers and missionaries of other faiths brought organized religion to the unchurched, the tide was turned and church membership increased from 11 percent in 1820 to 23 percent in 1860. [^42]

17Meanwhile, Anglicanism failed to provide many English people with spiritual leadership and adequate facilities for worship. Although the Established Church was supported by an ecclesiastical tax, this resource was not sufficient to provide all inhabitants with organized religion. In 1811, for example, half of the Anglican benefices did not have a resident incumbent. Whereas in America a religious vacuum was created by a massive wilderness, in England the Establishment was especially weak in borders of parishes and in rapidly emerging towns. The Church of England never had a significant influence on the working class in early Victorian industrial society, and that group was rapidly becoming the largest segment of the total population. [^43]

17The Established Church, like many other religious communities of that age, especially failed to provide adequate leadership and space in church buildings for the poor, there being only a small section of the houses of worship set aside for those who did not purchase pews. The sermons and general atmosphere in the churches were also more appealing to the wealthy or middle class than to the poorest citizens. Partly because of the apparent weakness of the Establishment, dissenters grew in areas where the Church of England was weakest and among the poorer class who had been neglected by this movement. [^44]

Another deficiency of the Establishment was that it was never universally accepted. Always there were dissenters seeking reform and others who were not attracted to any of the popular modes of worship that emerged. In one respect, the Toleration Act of 1689 had conceded very little. It sanctioned a dissent that had existed since the beginning of the Reformation. A more immediate effect of the act in England was the encouragement of voluntarianism in matters of religious commitment. The legislation, which gave Protestants the right to dissent, was interpreted by some to mean that they had the right to abstain from public worship and practice religion according to their personal commitment. Consequently, the act of toleration increased the visibility of public apathy and led to a decline in church attendance. By 1740 more people in England neglected all forms of public worship than affiliated with dissenting groups. [^45] [^46]

18Many who did not unite with others in public worship or who were not affiliated with any church in England or the United States at the time of the restoration should not be considered irreligious. Rather, many were not satisfied with doctrines and practices of the existing churches. Partly because of the creation of a spiritual vacuum in these two lands, some had lost their identity with religious patterns embraced by their ancestors and were, therefore, prepared to explore new religious horizons. After examining the claims of different religious groups, many were seeking a purer form of New Testament Christianity.

Other forces encouraged people in England and America to seek religious truth. In addition to being confused during the religious upheaval of the early nineteenth century, many had been humbled by a variety of external forces. Many experienced stress resulting from social dislocations. This disruption, accompanied in part a movement of Americans westward and the migration of the English from villages to rising towns. During the initial stages of the industrial revolution in England, some abandoned the relative freedom and diversity of village life for the economic opportunities available in the rising towns. Such opportunities, however, created new challenges. Unhealthy working conditions in factories were not alleviated when workers returned to their small and crowded dwellings. Meanwhile, after leaving the villages, farmers, field hands and craftsmen became machine operators, and therefore their power to create and regulate their lives dissipated under the monotony of laboring as machine tenders. Moreover, the squalor in the towns precipitated plagues, and the strength of many was weakened amid disease and death. Unemployment in towns and villages also presented a growing population with an increased threat of malnutrition and starvation. Many had been humbled by a variety of external pressures.

Summary

In the early nineteenth century, conditions were favorable in the new nation and in England for a restoration of the everlasting gospel. Toleration had become a reality. In both lands Latter-day Saints could legally preach and organize religious communities. The religious pluralism that had spurred the growth of toleration stimulated people to seek answers to religious issues. In both lands various Protestant communities launched aggressive programs to bring organized religion to the discontented and the unchurched. In unprecedented numbers, inhabitants of these countries, involved in a quest for religious truth, united with different religious communities.

20During a period of social upheaval, many recognized that religion brought into their lives stability, security, peace, harmony and authority. Some were humbled by poverty. [^47] Some by social disruptions. Some by plagues and untimely deaths of loved ones. And some had been humbled by a combination of these and other forces. Nevertheless, during a powerful spiritual quickening in England and America, many individuals searched reverently for religious truth.

20Before contacting Latter-day Saint missionaries, most early converts to Mormonism (who recorded their beliefs) were seekers. Many members of churches and others not affiliated with any religious persuasion were searching for the plainness of doctrines and the spiritual power of pristine Christianity. [^48] In the early nineteenth century, the fields of a young republic and of an early Victorian England had been cultivated and were ready to harvest.
