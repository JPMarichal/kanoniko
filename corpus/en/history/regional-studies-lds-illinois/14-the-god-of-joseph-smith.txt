The God of Joseph Smith

Joseph Fielding McConkie

That a universal apostasy followed the ministry of Christ is fundamental to the faith of the Latter-day Saints. The Savior warned of false Christs and false prophets who would follow his ministry. Virtually every book in the New Testament warns in one way or another of this impending night of darkness. Nevertheless, the prophetic word also foretold a time in which all the truths of salvation, with all the keys, powers, and authorities known to the ancients, would be restored in a marvelous dispensation of the fulness of all things. That dispensation commenced in the spring of 1820 when the youthful Joseph Smith went into a grove of trees near his parents' home in Upstate New York to ask God which of all the churches he should join. It is singularly remarkable that the sincere prayer of a fourteen-year-old boy would threaten the kingdom of the Prince of Darkness as it did that day. The events that it precipitated justify that threat and amply attest that there is immeasurable power in the simple injunction, "Ask God."

Asking God

We do not pretend to be able to answer the question as to why others in the midst of all the religious tumult that the world has known during the past seventeen centuries did not importune the heavens in such a simple and direct way as did Joseph Smith. We do know, however, that none of the canonical lists mention inspiration as a criterion for determining which books should be included in what we have come to know as the New Testament. [^1] We know that the Nicene Council, at which the doctrine of the Holy Trinity was officially formulated, was devoid of any pretense to inspiration. The historian Socrates, who was present, described it as "a battle fought in the dark, with the combatants unable to distinguish friend from foe." [^2] We know that Catholic, Protestant, Jew, and Muslim declare the heavens sealed to additional revelation. For them the voice of God is but a distant echo from ages past. True enough, they p.206] profess that God's voice speaks afresh through the ancient text, but none could countenance a man on earth today speaking with authority equal to the ancients. None could believe that words have been added to the canon. I would think it common for the adherents of these various religions to be enjoined to believe the particular brand of gospel being preached to them. I would think it unheard of for them to be challenged to pray and ask God about the truthfulness of what they were being told to believe. Warnings against such prayers are common to anti-Mormon literature.

206In contrast, Joseph Smith held that true religion required all to ask of God. "Ye are commanded in all things to ask of God, who giveth liberally" states a revelation given to the Church in March 1831. This that they might be protected from evil spirits, doctrines of devils, and the commandments of men ( D&C 46:7 ). The Book of Mormon exemplifies the pattern. Moroni writes and tells his father about the practice of infant baptism among some of their people. Mormon responds: "Immediately after I had learned these things of you I inquired of the Lord concerning the matter. And the word of the Lord came to me by the power of the Holy Ghost" ( Moro. 8:7 ). Moroni follows this same pattern in the concluding chapter of the book. All who read it with an honest heart are enjoined to inquire of the heavens as to the truthfulness of the book ( Moro. 10:3-5 ). It is fundamental to the doctrine of the Church that all are to drink at the fountainhead. This pattern is not common to the rest of the religious world.

Spiritual Equality

The prophetic efforts of Joseph Smith did not center in sharing his spiritual experiences but rather in the effort to qualify us to have our own spiritual experiences. The emphasis of his ministry was not on what he had seen but on what we could see. "God hath not revealed anything to Joseph," he said, "but what He will make known unto the Twelve, and even the least Saint may know all things as fast as he is able to bear them." [^3] There is a remarkable openness about all that Joseph did. Many pretenders to the prophetic office, for instance, have claimed to entertain angels or to having spoken with God, but who other than Joseph Smith introduced his angels to others? Joseph introduced Moroni to Oliver Cowdery, David Whitmer, and Martin Harris. He was never alone p.207] ( when priesthood or keys were restored. Witnesses were always present. He received many revelations in the presence of others. He and Sidney Rigdon received the revelation on the degrees of glory together. Together they saw legions of angels and the Father and the Son ( &C 76:21-23 . Oliver Cowdery was with Joseph Smith when John the Baptist came to restore the Aaronic Priesthood, and testified that they also "were wrapped in the vision of the Almighty!" [^4] Oliver was with the Prophet when Peter, James, and John came to restore the Melchizedek Priesthood, and again when Christ came to accept the dedication of the Kirtland Temple. It was on that occasion that he and Joseph received keys at the hands of Moses, Elias, and Elijah.

207Joseph Smith not only parted the veil, he extended the invitation for all of us to pass through it. The God of Joseph Smith promised all who would serve Him in righteousness and truth that He would reveal to them "all mysteries, yea, all the hidden mysteries of [his] kingdom from days of old, and for ages to come" ( D&C 76:7 ). He promised to make known to them his will concerning all things pertaining to his kingdom. "Yea, even the wonders of eternity shall they know, and things to come will I show them, even the things of many generations" ( D&C 76:8 ).

To Critics of the First Vision

Critics of the Church have made a lot of fuss about the fact that we have so few contemporary accounts of the First Vision. But that rather makes the point. Joseph was talking more about what we could do than what he had done. We have a dozen revelations in the Doctrine and Covenants that invite us to see God. Joseph invited us to check him by having our own Sacred Grove experience. The validity of an experiment is if it can be repeated. A good seed not only bears good fruits but it always bears the same fruits-regardless of who plants it. Thus Joseph Smith taught that the seed of faith will always bring its train of attendants, including revelations, visions, dreams, etc., and that all who plant and nurture the seed are entitled to the harvest. [^5]

The true test of a prophet and seer does not rest in his conveying his revelations, but in his qualifying you to receive your own. Moses did not take the children of Israel to Sinai to tell them that he spoke with God as one man speaketh with another, but rather to p.208] sanctify them and bring them all into the Divine Presence. With the restoration of the Melchizedek Priesthood came the restoration of the "key of the knowledge of God" ( D&C 84:19 ). "The power and authority of the higher, or Melchizedek Priesthood, is to hold the keys of all the spiritual blessings of the church-to have the privilege of receiving the mysteries of the kingdom of heaven, to have the heavens opened unto them, to commune with the general assembly and church of the Firstborn, and to enjoy the communion and presence of God the Father, and Jesus the mediator of the new covenant" ( D&C 107:18-19 ).

208Let's lay the matter plain: that priesthood restored to Joseph Smith and Oliver Cowdery by Peter, James, and John, that same priesthood given to every worthy male adult in the Church, has as its purpose to prepare all who comply with its saving ordinances to stand in the presence of the Father and the Son. This was the very doctrine that Moses taught the children of God in the wilderness when he "sought diligently to sanctify his people that they might behold the face of God; but they hardened their hearts and could not endure his presence; therefore, the Lord in his wrath, for his anger was kindled against them, swore that they should not enter into his rest while in the wilderness, which rest is the fulness of his glory" ( D&C 84:23-24 ).

208From that point the story is well known, but perhaps the implications are not. The Lord took Moses and the higher priesthood out of their midst. He gave them, in its stead, the Aaronic or lesser priesthood which contains the keys of the ministering of angels. Paul referred to this experience as the "provocationÂ�in the wilderness" ( Heb. 3:8 ) as did Lehi's son Jacob ( Jacob 1:7 ). That is, the children of Israel provoked the Lord by refusing to prepare themselves to see him. Thus the privilege was lost and in its stead they were given the law of Moses. The invitation and its implications are the same in our dispensation. We have the higher priesthood and it has the same purpose-preparing us to stand in the presence of God while in the flesh. Should we refuse that privilege then we have every right to anticipate that it will be taken from us and a lesser order of things given to us in its stead.

A revelation given in March 1831 concludes with the promise that all who will build on the "stone of Israel," that is the rock of revelation, will enjoy a future day in which they shall "hear" and "see" and "know" the Savior ( D&C 50:44-45 ). In Doctrine and p.209] Covenants 67, given in November of the same year, the Lord promised those who had been ordained to the ministry that if they would strip themselves of jealousies and fears and humble themselves before Him that the veil would be rent and that they "shall see me and know that I am-not with the carnal neither natural mind, but with the spiritual" (v 10). The vision of the degrees of glory concludes with the declaration that such things can be seen and known only by the power of the Holy Spirit, "which God bestows on those who love him, and purify themselves before him; to whom he grants this privilege of seeing and knowing for themselves; that through the power and manifestation of the Spirit, while in the flesh, they may be able to bear his presence in the world of glory" ( D&C 76:116-18 ). In December of the following year the command was given to "sanctify yourselves that your minds become single to God, and the days will come that you shall see him; for he will unveil his face unto you, and it shall be in his own time, and in his own way, and according to his own will" ( D&C 88:68 ). In May of 1833 came the promise that "Every soul who forsaketh his sins and cometh unto me, and calleth on my name, and obeyeth my voice, and keepeth my commandments, shall see my face and know that I am" ( D&C 93:1 ). In the summer of 1833 came the commandment to build a temple in Kirtland with the promise that, if they would keep it undefiled, God's glory would rest upon it. "I will come into it, and all the pure in heart that shall come into it shall see God" ( D&C 97:17 ). In the dedicatory prayer for the temple the Prophet noted that they had built a house to the Lord "that the Son of Man might have a place to manifest himself to his people" ( D&C 109:5 ). A week later the Savior appeared in the temple and said to Joseph Smith and Oliver Cowdery who stood before him, "I will appear unto my servants, and speak unto them with mine own voice, if my people will keep my commandments, and do not pollute this holy house" ( D&C 110:8 ).

209This notion that all were entitled to the kind of sacred experiences enjoyed by Joseph Smith found common expression in the hymns the Saints were singing. The hymn entitled "The Glorious Day" contained this stanza:

209The saints will then, with pure delight, Possess the holy land; And walk with Jesus Christ in white, And in his presence stand. [^6]

210The seventh stanza of the hymn "In Ancient Days" was as follows:

210Now we by faith, like Paul and John, May see the Father and the Son, And view eternal things above, And taste the sweets of boundless love. [^7]

210And again, in a hymn simply designated as "No. 48," the last lines of the third stanza read:

210Ye pure in heart, obtain the grace To see without a veil his face. [^8]

210In fact, the assumption is that such experiences will be sufficiently common among the faithful that a revelation is included which gives instruction as to the grand keys whereby false or counterfeit messengers from the unseen world can be detected (see D&C 129 ).

A Revelation Must Reveal

A simple test by which we can determine the legitimacy of any purported revelation is to ask, "What does it reveal?" By definition a revelation must reveal something-it must be more than a clap of the hands and a hallelujah, for that adds nothing to our knowledge. If God reveals himself to someone they must, through that experience, obtain some sure knowledge about him. All claimants must pass the same test. If traditional Christianity claims no sure knowledge about God it is because they are without the light of heaven. If they are without that knowledge they have no business acting as his mouthpiece. Such is the second test we apply to Joseph Smith. If he is a true prophet then we can rightfully expect him to be the source of sure knowledge about Deity.

The Lectures on Faith

One very important source for us in attempting to understand the God of Joseph Smith is the Lectures on Faith delivered to a class of the elders in Kirtland, Ohio, in the winter of 1834-35. There are some who are quite anxious that we be advised that these lectures were not written by the Prophet himself but rather by others acting under his direction. If the lectures presented represent the revelations of heaven to the Prophet, the concern is unfounded. That is the principle behind baptism and all the ordinances of salvation. Someone commissioned by Jesus Christ acts in the first person for the Father, the Son, and the Holy Ghost and does the actual baptizing. Yet the baptism represents the mind and will of the Godhead. The whole system of salvation centers in and around this system-we call it "divine investiture of authority." Without this principle God would be required to do everything personally that is done for the salvation of his children. Every baptism would have to be performed by him. Because of this principle all who are part of the household of faith are called upon to do things in his name. If done under the direction of the Spirit such actions are approved by him and are binding on us. The point is simply that if a sixteen-year-old priest in the Aaronic Priesthood can stand in the stead of the Father, Son, and Holy Ghost, in the performance of an ordinance of salvation, surely elders of Israel, men holding the Holy Melchizedek Priesthood, can prepare lectures under the direction of the Prophet and enjoy the sanction and direction of the Spirit in so doing. This cannot be used as an argument against the validity of the Lectures on Faith any more than it can be argued that a p.212] baptism is not valid unless God himself performed it, or a sermon uninspired unless he gave it.

212Everything the Lectures on Faith teach about Deity is a natural extension of truths learned by Joseph Smith in the First Vision or in subsequent revelations. Furthermore, all that they teach about God stands in contradistinction to the creeds of Christendom. For instance, the Lectures attest that the man Adam was real, the Garden of Eden was real, and that the man Adam walked and talked there with a God who was real. There was no "vail to separate between them, the story is not represented as a metaphor or a myth" (see Lecture 2:12). Thus the announcement that man was created in the image and likeness of God also becomes real (see Lecture 2:8) and, as long as we hold these things to be real, we as Latter-day Saints stand outside of the circle of Christian orthodoxy and will be denominated by them as non-Christian.

212Further, the lectures declare that the exaltation of Christ was to be shared by all faithful Saints. As he became a partaker of his Father's fulness, in his resurrected state, so we are privileged to do the same. The privilege of advancing from grace to grace, to "become heirs of the heavenly kingdom, and joint heirs with Jesus Christ; possessing the same mind, being transformed into the same image or likeness, even the express image of him who fills all in all; being filled with the fulness of his glory, and become one in him, even as the Father, Son and Holy Spirit are one," is the doctrine of these lectures (see Lecture 5:2-3). Again, Christian orthodoxy holds this to be heresy. By revelation the Prophet had previously announced that those who obtain the celestial glory would be equal with him "in power, and in might, and in dominion" ( D&C 76:95 ). It can hardly be argued that these are notions that the early brethren brought with them from their various sectarian backgrounds.

212I am not unmindful of the difficulties that some see in the Fifth Lecture, wherein the Father is spoken of as a personage of spirit while the Son is described as a personage of tabernacle. The Lecture also fails to identify the Holy Ghost as a distinct person and suggests, in one instance, that he is the mind of the Father, though later the lecture does speak of the three members of the Godhead (see Lecture 5:2). Some have suggested that these statements were the reason that the Lectures were dropped from the 1921 edition of the Doctrine and Covenants. This is simply a supposition on their part.

213The difficulties in Lecture Five are certainly no greater than those facing the reader of the Book of Mormon in Abinadi's remarks recorded in Mosiah 15:1-7 . In fact, these verses may be the perfect source to resolve the problem. What both Joseph and Abinadi are teaching is (1 ) that the defining characteristic of the Son is his tabernacle of flesh which enables him to accomplish the office to which he was ordained in the councils of heaven, (2) that the defining characteristic of the Father is Spirit or as Joseph states in the Lectures, "glory" and "power," (3) that the Son, by obedience, becomes spirit, or one, with the Father. That is, he obtains the glory and power of the Father as we, in like manner, are to do.

213Abinadi captured the whole system of salvation with the announcement that the Father and the Son are one God because "the flesh [became] subject to the SpiritÂ�the will of the Son being swallowed up in the will of the Father" ( Mosiah 15:5, 7 ). That is, "the flesh," meaning Christ, became "subject to the Spirit," meaning God, or, as Abinadi says in the parallel phrase, "the Son to the Father" ( Mosiah 15:5 ). Flesh personifies the Son and Spirit personifies the Father. Thus each of us obtains salvation when the desires of the flesh are "swallowed up in the will of the Father." In that process we become Spirit, meaning we obtain the fulness of the glory and power of God. [^9] D&C 88, which was received in 1832, we will have "a spiritual body" which is identified as the same body we had in mortality save it has been quickened by the glory of the Lord ( D&C 88:27-28 ).

We need not misunderstand this lecture. Joseph said there are two personages in the Godhead. That is, as my father explained to me, "two corporeal beings." It may well be that the reason the Holy Ghost is not referred to as a "personage" in this lecture is because, according to the common usage of the day, the word "personage" referred to "the physical form or appearance of a person" (see Webster's 1828 Dictionary). As Latter-day Saints came to understand that the unembodied and the disembodied have form and are individuals, we have extended the meaning of the word "person" to embrace spirits, but that would not have been common usage in the early 1830s. Joseph Smith explained, "The spirit, by many, is thought to be immaterial, without substance. With this latter statement we should beg leave to differ, and state the spirit is a substance; that it is material, but that it is more pure, elastic and refined matter p.214] than the body; that it existed before the body, can exist in the body; and will exist separate from the body, when the body will be moldering in the dust; and will in the resurrection be again united with it." [^10]

214The notion that spirits are actual beings first came to the Prophet in the Sacred Grove. His struggle there was not with an imaginary power but with an "actual being from the unseen world" ( JS-H 1:16 ). Perhaps no prophet in earth's history conversed more freely with those on the other side of the veil than did Joseph Smith as he became the recipient of all the keys, powers, and authorities of past dispensations. In none of this are we sharing ground with the Christian world. There is not a scrap of evidence anywhere indicating that Joseph Smith ever, for so much as a moment, supposed that God was the invisible, formless, bodiless, incomprehensible, abstract being described in Christian creeds. From the time of the First Vision the God of Joseph Smith had form and was confined to one place in time and space. This has nothing to do with the formless and immaterial essence known as the Good to Plato, as God to Philo, and formally adopted by Christianity at Nicea.

214That Joseph grew in knowledge and understanding is obvious; that he borrowed doctrines from the Protestant or Catholic world is ridiculous. He knew that all the doctrines relative to God in sectarian creeds were an abomination and he did not teach them or borrow from them. The very notion that he would speak about the characteristics and attributes of Deity is an offense to the doctrines of the creeds which declare God not just to be invisible and immaterial, but to be an incomprehensible mystery. The theology that sustains the creeds of Christendom does not allow God and Christ to be separate and distinct, nor does it allow them to be "personages," nor does it allow them to have attributes in common with mortal men or even attributes men are capable of comprehending. Let me briefly illustrate by quoting from a recently published work written on the Apostles' Creed. Relative to the personhood of God it states: "We call God `one.' We call God `three.' One what? Three whats? We might, in either case, say simply `thing' or `things' (for this is, after all, what the ancient terms amounted to). This would, admittedly, be inelegant but, if we insist on having an answer to the question `three whats?' then there is something to be said for choosing an expression which does not misleadingly appear (as `person' does), to be informative." [^11]

215As to the matter of attributes we are told, "The words Father, God, Lord, are not real names `but appellations derived from his good deeds and functions.' God cannot appear, cannot go about, cannot be seen." [^12] [ He has no attributes knowable to man. The Oxford Dictionary of the Christian Church reaffirms the doctrine that God cannot be seen and then declares that "All affirmations of Scriptures and the [ante-Nicene] Fathers are but metaphors devised for the ignorant." [^13] Orthodox Christianity does not deem it proper to describe God in terms of attributes. This is referred to as ffirmative theology that is, theology in which we affirm something about Deity. Standing opposite affirmative theology is negative theology. Negative theology grows out of the doctrine of God's transcendence. The notion is that because all of God's attributes so transcend man's ability to understand them, to profess to know him, or something about him, is to misunderstand him. Thus the safe course is to eliminate from our thinking any discussions of God's characteristics and attributes. Thus it is held that all affirmative theology is figurative or metaphorical and it is "more correct to say that God is not that which is predicted concerning him than to say that he is." Thus, "He is known better by him who does not know him, whose true ignorance is wisdom." [^14] God, such a view holds, is best described in such terms as invisible, incorporeal, immutable, immense, and incomprehensible. [^15] God transcends essence, truth, wisdom, justice, mercy, judgment, etc. As more commonly stated, he is "without body, parts, or passions." "Indeed, so highly is he exalted above all creatures," it is held, "that the name `nothing' may justly be ascribed to him." [^16]

215Before leaving the Lectures on Faith, two other observations ought to be made. These lectures were first published in the Doctrine and Covenants in 1835. In October 1840 the announcement was made relative to a new addition of that scriptural text. [^17] It was published in 1844, shortly after the Prophet's death. Joseph Smith had ample opportunity to correct or edit what was written in the Lectures had he chosen to do so. Following his death they continued to be published by those who had been his associates in life and who had inherited the prophetic mantle.

The second observation is relative to the incomprehensible God of the sectarian creeds. That is the God whose "character, perfections, and attributes cannot be described."As long as it can be claimed that God is unknowable it can also be claimed that the p.216] laws of God are unknowable. This, in turn, has the effect of releasing all who claim God to be unknowable from the responsibility to conform to his law which effectively does away with the law. There could be no more perfect way to bring about a universal apostasy than to have a transcendental or incomprehensible God. All would be darkness. Such was the theological background against which Joseph Smith stood on the 7th of April in 1844 to give the King Follett Discourse. Amasa Potter, who was present, recalled the circumstances in which the sermon was delivered:

Consolation to the Bereaved

Now suppose you were called upon, as the Prophet was, to preach a funeral discourse, a discourse of consolation, faith, and hope. Suppose also that your knowledge of God was that of those who professed an allegiance to the creeds of Christendom. Your testimony would be of a God who was incorporeal, incomprehensible and invisible. You would be able to do no better in describing him than the declaration that he was the grand, divine mystery. As to the departed dead you could not speak of his state with any more confidence or knowledge than you can of his God. After whatever you say, you will be reduced to the admission that it is all a mystery. True, there are some stories and texts in the scriptures that extend some consolation to the bereaved, but then such texts, by all but those described as hopelessly ignorant, are to be understood as metaphorical. You can know nothing of God or the world to come with certainty.

220By contrast consider the light and faith kindled in the hearts of men by the doctrines taught by the Prophet. At death the earthly tabernacle returns to the dust from whence it came but only for a short time and season. The entity and integrity of each individual spirit is retained. They are, in reality, in the image and likeness of their God. Indeed, the righteous, those sealed by the Holy Spirit of promise, are his heirs and joint heirs with Christ. Thus every blessing and exaltation enjoyed by Christ is to be enjoyed by them. It will be their privilege to advance from grace to grace until they "arrive at the station of a God, and ascend the throne of eternal power, the same as all exalted souls have done before them." Now, do we mourn for such? Death becomes, as Jacob described it, a step in the "merciful plan of the great Creator" ( 2 Ne. 9:6 ). The mortal and immortal are separated, but only by a thin veil. "The spirits of the justÂ�are not far from us, and know and understand our thoughts, feelings, and motions," Joseph taught, and unfortunately "are often pained therewith." [^36]

220One is a doctrine of light and hope, the other a doctrine of darkness and uncertainty. As the God of Joseph Smith can have attributes and characteristics so can the doctrines that come from him have characteristics, and chief among them is the fact that they lift, enlighten, and encourage; they bring faith, solace, and purpose.

To Know God Is To Offend the World

When I was a young missionary my mission president gave me a little book entitled Why I Believe. It contained over fifty evidences that Joseph Smith was a prophet. [^37] With the passing of years the reasons listed in that kind of book have become less meaningful to me. At the same time I have gained a sensitivity to other evidences, things that have gone unnoticed and uncommented on, which, in what I hope is a more mature judgment, are profoundly impressive. I have suggested three of those reasons in this paper. First, the Prophet's insistence that all drink at the fountain-head is crucial. All are to ask of God for themselves. I have had enough experience with those of other faiths and have sufficient understanding of their history and doctrines to say with confidence that this is a distinctive characteristic of the Restored gospel. The second evidence is an extension of the first. It is the emphasis of Joseph Smith's ministry to get us all to share in the fulness of the revelations of heaven. Joseph Smith was truly a prophet among his brethren, not aloof or apart from them. Every spiritual experience that was his he wanted also to become ours. And third, the completeness and fulness of the doctrine he taught. If a true prophet is to receive revelations and if those revelations are to bring us closer to God, then Joseph Smith was indeed a prophet of God.

221It was here in the City of Joseph that these truths came to fruition, particularly as they found such masterful expression in the Follett Discourse. Never has a sermon been given that contains more eternal truth. But there were prerequisites to the Prophet's understanding. They included the restoration of the endowment, a fuller understanding of the sealing power, the doctrines of eternal marriage and continuation of the family unit. Only in the revelation of these principles could the understanding of the nature of God, as evidenced in the King Follett Discourse, fully settle on the Prophet. And only with the temple as a background could the principles he taught be understood by the Saints.

221The doctrines that were taught in Nauvoo both justify and sanctify it as holy ground. May we be true to these doctrines as were those to whom they were first preached.

Now one final observation. One of the comfortable things about worshipping a God who is both invisible and unknowable is that no one can be certain who is right or who is wrong relative to p.222] theology. Thus peaceful coexistence can be enjoyed by any who are willing to avoid dogmatic assertions about the nature of God. The assertion of a sure knowledge of God is inevitably followed by the identification of a "straight and narrow path," or a one true church concept, which in turn unsettles the hope for peace among those of various systems of worship. Thus it is that Joseph Smith received the promise that his name would be had for good and evil among every nation, kindred, tongue and people ( JS-H 1:33 ). Like the Savior before him he stood as a light in a dark place, declaring the peaceable doctrines of the kingdom while the storms of controversy howled around him. Such is the reception we anticipate to our testimony in the world of which we are a part.
