Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHCJoseph Smith: Candidate for President of the United States

Arnold K. Garr

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHCOn the morning of 29 January 1844, the Quorum of Twelve Apostles met with Joseph Smith and others at the mayor's office in Nauvoo, Illinois, to consider what course they should pursue relative to the coming election for president of the United States. During that meeting Willard Richards made a motion that Joseph Smith should run for the presidency on an independent electoral ticket and that those present should use "all honorable means in [their] power to secure his election." [^1] Thus formally began one of the most intriguing third-party campaigns for president of the United States in the history of the nation.

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHCThe Road to Candidacy

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHCPerhaps the first event that ultimately led to Joseph Smith's decision to run for president took place as early as 29 November 1839, when he and Elias Higbee met with Martin Van Buren, president of the United States, in Washington, DC. The purpose of that meeting was to seek help from the federal government in obtaining redress for the property lost and afflictions suffered by the Saints during the Missouri persecutions. Van Buren treated them rudely and showed them little sympathy so they spent the next two months trying to gain support from various senators and representatives who might be willing to espouse their cause. [^2] Finally, in February 1840, Joseph and Elias met with President Van Buren one last time. Again he treated them with indifference, saying: "Gentlemen, your cause is just, but I can do nothing for youÂ�. If I take up for you I shall lose the vote of Missouri." [^3]

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHC152This experience infuriated Joseph Smith. "On my way home," wrote the Prophet, "I did not fail to proclaim the iniquity and indolence of Martin Van Buren, toward myself and an injured peopleÂ�and may he never be elected again to any office of trust or power." [^4] This prophecy came true. Martin Van Buren lost the election of 1840 to William Henry Harrison, the Whig candidate.

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHC152Joseph Smith's disillusioning trip to Washington, DC, seemed to be a turning point in his life. It was the first time he became involved in politics on a national level and he came away frustrated and angry. His people had been horribly abused and unjustly treated in Missouri and the president of the United States did nothing to help. After 1840, the Prophet chose to endorse only those presidential candidates who would pledge their support to the Saints. This decision only led to further frustration.

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHC152In anticipation of the 1844 presidential election, The Times and Seasons published an editorial on 1 October 1843, entitled "Who Shall Be Our Next President?" It did not suggest any specific names but concluded that the candidate must be a "man who will be most likely to render us assistance in obtaining redress for our grievances." [^5] On 4 November 1843 Joseph Smith wrote letters to John C. Calhoun, Lewis Cass, Richard M. Johnson, Henry Clay, and Martin Van Buren, the five leading candidates for the presidency of the United States. Each letter talked of the persecutions the Mormons suffered at the hands of the state of Missouri and then asked the pointed question, "`What will be your rule of action relative to us as a people,' should fortune favor your ascension to the chief magestry?" [^6] [ All of the letters said essentially the same thing with one exception. To Martin Van Buren, the Prophet could not resist adding the following postscript: "Also whether your views or feeling have changed since the subject matter of this communication was presented you in your then official capacity at Washington, in the year 1841 [1839-40] and by you treated with coldness, indifference, and neglect, bordering on contempt." [^7]

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHCVan Buren and Johnson never responded to Joseph Smith's letters but Calhoun, Cass, and Clay did. Yet those who replied were not willing to pledge their support to the Church. The correspondence between Joseph Smith and John C. Calhoun is of particular interest because it brings into focus their contrasting views on the powers of the national government, something that would become a vital issue during the Prophet's campaign for president. Calhoun p.153] wrote, "The case [of the Mormon persecution in Missouri] does not come within the jurisdiction of the Federal Government, which is one of limited and specific powers." [^8] To this, Joseph Smith responded with a much different interpretation: "Congress has power to protect the nation against foreign invasion nd internal broilÂ�and the President has as much powerÂ�as Washington had to march against the `whiskey boys at Pittsburgh,' or General Jackson had to send an armed force to suppress the rebellion of South Carolina." [^9] Calhoun made no further reply.

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHC153When it was determined that none of the leading candidates for president would pledge his support in gaining redress for the Saints, the Prophet held the historic meeting in the mayor's office on 29 January 1844, where the Quorum of the Twelve and others present decided that Joseph Smith, himself, would run for president of the United States. The Prophet was now about to embark upon one of the most fascinating episodes in Church history.

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHCThe Platform

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHCJoseph wasted little time in preparing a platform for his campaign. On the same day he formally decided to run for president, he met with William W. Phelps and dictated to him the headings for a pamphlet entitled General Smith's Views Of The Powers And Policy Of The Government Of The United States. [^10] This pamphlet became the foundation document for his presidential platform.

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHC153Many have written about William Phelps's involvement in writing Joseph Smith's Views pamphlet. Bruce Van Orden, in his excellent article on Phelps as a political clerk, maintains that "Joseph Smith relied heavily upon" Phelps in writing the Views pamphlet as well as other political documents. [^11] Phelps had a superficial knowledge of foreign phrases which he loved to insert in many of the documents he wrote.Views is full of such phrases and B.H. Roberts attributes them to Phelps. [^12] These literary antics were annoying to Roberts who often edited them out in his istory of the Church [On one occasion, Roberts wrote that Phelps "had some smattering knowledge of languages, which he was ever fond of displayingÂ�. [However, these injected phrases were] in no way germane to the subjects of which they treat, and are not really the work of President Smith." [^13]

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHC154This raises the question, how much of Views did Joseph Smith actually write? This same question could be asked about the greater part of all the writings attributed to the Prophet. Dean Jessee explains that "the Mormon leader depended upon clerks to do most of his writing," hence "the variety in the Smith papers, fluctuating between hologram, dictated, and ghostwritten prose, creates a challenge for those whoÂ�face issues of actual authorship." [^14] Joseph Smith only made two statements about howViews was written. On 29 January 1844, he said, "I dictated to Brother Phelps the heads of my pamphlet, entitled, `Views on the Powers and Policy of the Government of the United States.'" [^15] Then, nine days later on 7 February, he wrote "I completed and signed my `Views.'" [^16]

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHC154I believe it is correct to assume that Phelps did most of the actual writing. However, I am also persuaded that Joseph Smith gave considerable guidance and input to the project, espoused the basic concepts of the platform, and assumed ultimate responsibility for the ideas in the document. Joseph Smith did dictate the headings of the pamphlet to Phelps, and almost every time Joseph refers to the document he calls it "My `Views'" or "My Pamphlet." For example, on 5 February he wrote: "Called at my office in the evening and revised My `Views.'" [^17] The fact that he signed the document also signifies that he accepted responsibility for its contents. As we analyze the pamphlet, therefore, it seems that we are duty bound to assume that the basic concepts of the platform either originated with Joseph Smith or, at the very least, were espoused by him. On the other hand, much (if not most) of the actual writing and a great deal of the phraseology can probably be attributed to William W. Phelps.

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHCGeneral Smith's Views Of The Powers And Policy Of The Government Of The United States is strictly a political document. Joseph Smith made no mention of his religious teachings in the pamphlet. The use of the title General (of the Nauvoo Legion) rather than Prophet or President set the tone for the tract. The pamphlet said nothing about Joseph Smith's spiritual experiences and did not mention The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. In fact, just a few months before the booklet was published, Joseph declared: "The Lord has not given me a revelation concerning politics. I have not asked him for one." [^18] Views was, essentially, p.155] an attempt to give pragmatic solutions to many of the nation's most pressing problems. Joseph advocated giving power to the president to suppress mobs. He also favored abolishing slavery, reducing both the number and pay of the House of Representatives, reforming the prison system, eliminating courts martial for desertion, forming a national bank, and annexing Oregon and Texas.

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHC155I am convinced that the most important plank in Joseph Smith's platform concerned the powers of the president of the United States. Surprisingly, some who have written about the platform did not consider this issue at all, [^19] while others assert that "the major plank of the Prophet's platform was the elimination of slavery." [^20] I respectfully disagree with both of these positions. When the Prophet met with President Van Buren in 1840, he was not angered by Van Buren's position on slavery but rather by his refusal to exercise presidential powers to help the Mormons. Similarly in November 1843 when the Prophet wrote the five leading presidential candidates, the only question he asked them was "What will be your rule of action relative to us as a people" if you are elected? [^21] These five letters asked nothing about their position on slavery. Furthermore, Joseph Smith attacked Senator Calhoun for his conservative views on the "limited and specific powers" of the federal government. [^22] Again we should note that the Prophet said nothing about Calhoun's views on slavery.

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHC155Joseph Smith put this matter of presidential powers in perspective on the evening of 8 January 1844, the first night his Views were publicly read. The Prophet addressed the meeting as follows:

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHC155I would not have suffered my name to have been used by my friends on anywise as President of the United States, or candidate for that office, if I and my friends could have had the privilege of enjoying our religious and civil rights as American citizens, even those rights which the Constitution guarantees unto all her citizens alike. But this as a people we have been denied from the beginning. Persecution has rolled upon our heads from time to time, from portions of the United States, like peals of thunder, because of our religion; and no portion of the government as yet has stepped forward for our relief. And in view of these things, I feel it to be my right and privilege to obtain what influence and power I can, lawfully, in the United States, for the protection of injured innocence. [^23]

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHC155Notice that the only motivation the Prophet cited for running was to gain power to protect his injured people. He said nothing about slavery or any other issue.

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHC156Specifically, Views proposed to give "the president full power to send an army to suppress mobs; and the states the authority to repeal and impugn the relic of folly, which makes it necessary for the governor of a state to make the demand of the president for troops, in case of invasion or rebellion." [^24] Here the Prophet was referring to Article IV, ection 4, f the United States Constitution which states that the federal government shall protect the states against "domestic violence" only on application of the state legislature or governor. Speaking of this article, Joseph Smith observed, "The Governor himself may be a mobber and, instead of being punished, as he should be for murder and treason, he may destroy the very lives, riches and property he should protect." [^25] [ The Prophet felt so strongly about this matter that on one occasion he even advocated capital punishment for public officials who declined to protect the lives and property of citizens: "The Constitution should contain a provision that every officer of the government who should neglect or refuse to extend the protection guaranteed in the Constitution should be subject to capital punishment; and then the president of the United States would not say, `Your cause is just, but I can do nothing for you' [or] a governor issue exterminating orders." [^26]

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHC156Slavery, though not the primary focus, was another important issue in Joseph Smith's platform. The Prophet recommended the abolition of slavery by the year 1850, at the latest. He would have Congress "pay every man a reasonable price for his slaves out of the surplus revenue arising from the sale of public lands, and from the deduction of pay from members of Congress. Break off the shackles from the poor black man, and hire them to labor like other human beings." [^27] Some might criticize this proposal as being economically impractical. Such critics should be reminded that the way this nation ultimately settled the slavery issue was through civil war-a conflict that cost more than 15 billion dollars and over 600,000 lives. [^28] It was the most devastating war in American history, a conflict that left the South in economic ruin and implanted bitterness and hatred in the hearts of millions. As historian David Potter once put it, "Slavery was dead, secession was dead, and six hundred thousand men were dead." [^29] In retrospect, it would seem that Joseph Smith's solution to the slavery issue was certainly more sensible than the Civil War.

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHC157According to Views, the slavery problem and the issue of congressional reform were closely related. The Prophet wanted to raise money to purchase slaves from their owners, in part by reducing the number of congressmen and their pay. Joseph advocated keeping two senators from each state but having only two representatives for every one million people. [^30] In 1844, there were 223 representatives and 52 senators representing 26 states. The population of the United States was approximately 20 million. The Prophet's proposal would have reduced the House of Representatives from 223 to 40. [^31] He believed a legislative body this size would "do more business than the army that now occupy the halls of the national Legislature." [^32]

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHC157Joseph also suggested that the pay for congressmen be reduced to two dollars a day (except Sundays). "That is more than a farmer gets, and he lives honestly." [^33] The pay for members of Congress in 1844 was eight dollars a day. [^34] The Prophet's suggested cut-backs in congressional membership and pay would have generated $553,384 for buying slaves. The above proposal would seem to imply that Joseph Smith believed that the issues of emancipation, as well as efficiency and economy in government, were more important than having large numbers of local representatives in the national Congress.

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHC157Views also recommended reform for the nation's prison system: "Let penitentiaries be turned into seminaries of learning," the Prophet declared. "Rigor and seclusion never do as much to reform the propensities of men as [would] reason and friendship." [^35] [ Joseph believed those who had broken the law should be put to work on "roads, public works, or any place where the culprit [could] be taught more wisdom and more virtue; and become more enlightened." [^36] He also wanted to do away with imprisonment for debt, which was still a lingering problem in some states. [^37] The Prophet's progressive position on prison reform reflected not only his desire to improve society in general but also his compassion for those who had broken the law.

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHCMilitary reform was another plank. The Prophet wanted to abolish the practice in the army and navy of trying men by court martial for desertion: "If a soldier or marine runs away, send him his wages, with this instruction, that his country will never trust him again, he has forfeited his honor. Make HONOR the standard with all men." Joseph continued, "Be sure that good is rendered for p.158] evil in all cases: and the whole nationÂ�will rise up with righteousness: and be respected as wise and worthy on earth." [^38]

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHC158The concept of a national bank was a plank that the Mormon leader seemed to be particularly pleased with. On the evening of Monday, 5 February 1844, two days before the Views pamphlet was completed, the Prophet wrote, "I was the first one who publicly proposed a national bank on the principles set forth in [the] pamphlet." [^39] He recommended, first, that a national bank be established with branches in each state and territory; second, that the officers be elected yearly and paid two dollars a day for their services; third, that the several banks not be permitted to issue any more bills than the amount of capital stock in their vaults; fourth, that the net gain of the mother bank would be applied to the national revenue, and that of the branches to the states and territories; and fifth, that the bills would be par throughout the nation, which he believed would cure the problem of "brokerage." [^40]

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHC158The Prophet's feelings about a strong national banking system, no doubt, stemmed from his experience with the Kirtland Safety Society in 1837-an attempt to establish a banking institution that failed miserably and disillusioned numerous members of the Church. [^41] Joseph hoped to prevent such misfortune in the future.

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHC158The final plank of the platform had to do with foreign affairs. Joseph Smith was an expansionist who believed in the immediate annexation of the Oregon Territory: "Oregon belongs to the government honorably, and when we have the red man's consent, let the union spread from east to the west sea." [^42] His position on Texas, however, was somewhat different. He advocated annexing the Lone Star State only if it first petitioned the United States Congress to be admitted. He even extended the same invitation to Canada and Mexico, but only on petition. [^43] The Prophet's motivation seemed to be a desire to bring these political entities under the influence of the United States Constitution which, he taught, was an inspired document ( D&C 101:77, 80 ).

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHCGeneral Smith's Views is an intriguing document. With the luxury of hindsight, we can see that many of his proposals came to pass, although not necessarily in the way he hoped: The power of the presidency was increased by Abraham Lincoln during the Civil p.159] War; that same bloody conflict also emancipated the slaves; the penal system has improved, although not to the extent that Joseph prescribed and, of course, Oregon and Texas soon became part of the Union. Elder John A. Widtsoe's evaluation of Views seems appropriate: "This campaign document is an intelligent, comprehensive, forward-looking statement of policies, worthy of a trained statesman. Many of the Prophet's recommendations have been adopted in the progressive passage of the years. All of them are reasonable and sound." [^44]

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHC159On the evening of 8 February 1844, William Phelps read Views publicly for the first time. Then Joseph Smith, Orson Hyde, and John Taylor spoke; afterwhich, all in attendance voted unanimously to support the Prophet's platform. [^45] By 24 February, Joseph had 1,500 copies of the pamphlet printed, and two days later he had copies mailed to the president of the United States and his cabinet, the justices of the Supreme Court, senators, representatives, editors of principle newspapers, postmasters, and other prominent citizens. [^46]

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHCThe Campaign

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHCOn 9 April 1844, during a regular session of general conference, the campaign began to accelerate and take on its unique nature, as several Church leaders chose to interweave their spiritual sermons with presidential politics. In the same speech, Hyrum Smith not only admonished the Saints to build their temple, but also encouraged them to use their influence to elect Joseph Smith president. [^47] Heber C. Kimball and Brigham Young announced that they were going to call elders to both "preach the gospel and electioneer." [^48] During the latter part of the meeting, President Young called for volunteers to serve these political/religious missions and 244 people stepped forward. [^49] Hence began something unique in American as well as Mormon history-people called on missions to campaign for their presidential candidate and, at the same time, proselytize for their church.

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHCDuring the next few days additional missionaries were called, bringing the grand total to at least 340. On 15 April they were assigned to all twenty-six states in the Union and the Wisconsin Territory. The state of New York was assigned the most missionaries (47) while Connecticut received the fewest (two). [^50] The Quorum p.160] of the Twelve scheduled a series of 47 conferences to be held all over the United States, starting in Quincy, Illinois, on 4 May and ending in Washington, DC, on 15 September. [^51]

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHC160Surprisingly, little has been written about the relative size and strength of this missionary force. These 340 electioneer missionaries, added to the number of those who had been called to serve traditional missions during the same year, brought the total number of missionaries to 586 for the year 1844. This was, by far, the largest number of missionaries called to serve in a single year, up to that time. To put this number into perspective, the average number of missionaries set apart in a single year prior to 1843 was sixty-one. The fewest missionaries set apart in a year during the same period of time was 16 (in 1830 and 1838). The most missionaries set apart in any single year prior to 1843 was 111 in 1834. Looking ahead after 1844, it would be over 50 years before the Church would call more than 586 missionaries in the same year (746 were called in 1895). [^52] Even though missionary statistics in the early years of the Church were not nearly as accurate as they are today, the numbers for the year 1844 are nevertheless quite impressive. Not only the number but also the quality of missionaries called was striking. All the members of the Quorum of Twelve, except John Taylor and Willard Richards, were electioneer missionaries; and this included Brigham Young, Heber C. Kimball, Wilford Woodruff, Parley Pratt, and Orson Pratt, some of the greatest missionaries in the history of the Church.

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHC160The next important event of the campaign was the State Convention held on 17 May 1844 in Nauvoo, Illinois. Delegates representing all 26 states and ten Illinois counties attended. Most delegates, however, were citizens of Nauvoo, representing states they had previously lived in. During that convention, Joseph Smith was formally nominated for president of the United States and Sidney Rigdon was nominated to be vice-president. The delegates made plans to have a national convention in Baltimore, Maryland, on 13 July and appointed Willard Richards, John M. Bernhisel, William W. Phelps and Lucian R. Foster to serve as a central committee of correspondence for the campaign. The featured speaker was John S. Reid, a non-Mormon lawyer who had defended the Prophet in his first prosecution in the state of New York. [^53]

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHCSome of the most humorous aspects of the campaign were the reports Joseph Smith occasionally received of straw polls or mock- p.161] voting that took place on the river boats of the Mississippi, perhaps the closest thing they had to the Gallup polls of our day. The Prophet recorded the findings of several of these straw polls in his history but the following, published in the Nauvoo Neighbor, is perhaps the most engaging:

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHC161General Joseph Smith, the acknowledged modern Prophet, has got them all in the rear; and from the common mode of testing the success of candidates for the Presidency, to wit. By steamboat election, he (Smith) will beat all the other aspirants to that office two to one. We learn from the polls of the steamboat Osprey, on her last trip to this city, that the vote stood for General Joseph Smith, 20 gents and 5 ladies; Henry Clay, 16 gents and 4 ladies; Van Buren, 7 gents and 0 ladies. [^54]

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHC161In an attempt to get a feel for the activities of an electioneer missionary, it is interesting to read the journal of Wilford Woodruff. At that time he did not realize that this would be the last time he would see the Prophet before the martyrdom, but, after the fact, he inserted his feelings about that last meeting: "I this day took the last parting hand & look with the Prophet and Patriarch at their own dwelling. O O what a look Joseph gave me. Ah he knew what I did not." [^55]

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHC161Elder Woodruff's electioneer mission lasted two months. During that time he recorded that he spoke in at least six "political meetings." He spoke at many more religious meetings than he did at political gatherings, and he always seemed to keep his religious sermons and political speeches separate. The political gatherings were usually held the night before or very soon after the traditional Church conferences. [^56]

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHC162Finally, on 1 July 1844, Wilford Woodruff, Brigham Young, William Smith, Lyman Wight, Orson Pratt and others attended a state convention for Joseph Smith, held in Boston, Massachusetts. The proceedings were published by the Boston Daily Times. Wilford wrote in his journal: "The Melodeon was crowded in the evening and it was soon evident that a large number of rowdies were in the galleries & felt disposed to make [a] disturbance." [^63] Thus ended the final political rally for Joseph Smith that Wilford Woodruff was ever to attend.

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHC162A brief profile of Elder Woodruff's two month, electioneer mission indicates that he was involved in political meetings an average of once every week and a half. Four of the six political rallies were evidently well received while two were marred by disorderly conduct. According to his journal, at least two of the political gatherings were attended by fairly large crowds and the proceedings of one convention were reported in a big-city news- paper.

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHC162In the meantime, back in Illinois, William Law and others were plotting to take the life of Joseph Smith. A Dr. Southwick recounted a meeting he attended wherein the enemies of the Prophet had gathered together from every state in the Union but three. They were concerned that Joseph's "views on government were widely circulated and took like wildfire." [^64]64 According to Southwick, they believed that if the Prophet "did not get in the presidential chair this election, he would be sure to next time; and if Illinois and Missouri would join together and kill him, they would not be brought to justice for it." [^65] If this statement is accurate, it leads one to believe that the Prophet's presidential campaign was at least one cause for his death.

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHC163At any rate, Joseph Smith was shot and killed by a mob on 27 June 1844. On one occasion the Prophet seemed to intimate that he might be killed during the campaign. On 8 February, at a political gathering for his candidacy, Joseph declared: "If I lose my life in a good cause I am willing to be sacrificed on the altar of virtue, righteousness and truth, in maintaining the law and Constitution of the United States, if need be, for the general good of mankind." [^66]

Joseph Smith's Campaign for PresidentHC163When the news of the Prophet's martyrdom eventually reached the electioneer missionaries, his campaign came to an end. All the members of the Quorum of the Twelve hurried back to Nauvoo as did many of the other missionaries.

163One question that is often raised about the campaign is whether or not Joseph Smith considered himself a serious candidate. Did he really think he could be elected? The venerated B. H. Roberts gave the traditional view: "Of course President Smith could have no hope that he would be elected to the presidency" and "he usually referred to his candidacy in a jocular vein." [^67] Roberts justified his position by quoting the Prophet's statement, "I care but little about the presidential chair. I would not give half as much for the office of president of the United States as I would forÂ�Lieutenant-General of the Nauvoo Legion." [^68]

163More recently, several historians have argued that Joseph's campaign was much more serious than Roberts would have us believe. To support this view they cite a talk the Prophet gave on 29 January: "If I ever get into the presidential chair, I will protect the people in their rights and libertiesÂ�. There is oratory enough in the Church to carry me into the presidential chair the first slide." [^69] Also, on 8 February, he said: "I feel it to be my right and privilege to obtain what influence and power I can, lawfully, in the United States, for the protection of injured innocence." [^70]

163I am most sympathetic, however, with the position of James B. Allen, who cautions us not to take an "either-or position," because both notions "are based on circumstantial evidence and subjective reasoning." [^71] His opinion is that the Prophet did not think he could win the presidency, but "he was serious in attempting to influence opinion by using every possible means to promote his own political views," [^72] specifically regarding the power of the federal government.

164There is an important corollary to this question, however, that has seldom, if ever, been addressed. It concerns the attitude of the other high-ranking leaders of the Church. Specifically, how serious were the General Authorities about the Prophet's campaign? A brief recitation of some of their remarks leads us to the conclusion that they were most serious about the candidacy. Willard Richards admonished the Quorum of the Twelve to "use all honorable means in [their] power to secure [the Prophet's] election." [^73] Richards also referred to Joseph Smith as "the greatest statesman of the 19th century," and upon encouraging one brother to become involved in the campaign he said, "If you act well your part, victory's the prize." [^74] On one occasion Brigham Young stated, "We are acquainted with the views of Gen. Smith, the Democrats and Whigs and all factions. It is now time to have a President of the United States. Elders will be sent to preach the gospel and electioneer." [^75] To this Hyrum Smith added, "Lift up your voices like thunder: there is power and influence enough among us to put in a President." [^76] On 8 April, Heber C. Kimball affirmed, "We design to send elders to all different states to get up meetings and protracted meetings, and electioneer for Joseph to be the next President." [^77] Finally, on 18 May, Elder George A. Smith spoke to a large political rally with several hundred citizens assembled. He said General Smith was "the smartest man in the United States, and best calculated to fill the presidential chair." [^78] Skeptics might dismiss the above comments as political rhetoric but, if we are to take these men at their literal word, it is difficult to believe that they were anything less than serious about the campaign.

Conclusion

In retrospect, it appears that Joseph Smith's decision to run for president of the United States was motivated primarily because of his disillusionment with the federal government's refusal to act on the Mormon's petitions for redress regarding the Missouri persecutions. It follows logically, therefore, that the most important plank in the Prophet's platform dealt with strengthening the powers of the presidency. The campaign was unique inasmuch as the Church called "electioneer" missionaries to campaign for their presidential candidate and, at the same time, proselytize for their Church. The 340 political/religious missionaries, in addition to p.165] those called to serve traditional missions during the same year, made the 1844 missionary force the largest in the first 65 years of Church history.

165The Prophet's martyrdom tragically ended his campaign, but not without first winning our admiration. When we consider his campaign we are reminded of a statement by Edmund Burke: "All that is necessary for the triumph of evil is for good men to do nothing." [^79] To this, President Ezra Taft Benson has said, "It is not enough that we wring our hands and moan about conditions of America. We must become 'anxiously engaged' in good causes and leave the world a better place for having lived in it." [^80] Joseph Smith literally gave his life to make the world a better place in which to live-both spiritually and politically.
