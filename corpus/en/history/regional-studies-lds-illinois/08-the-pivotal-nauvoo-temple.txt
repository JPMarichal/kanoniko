The Pivotal Nauvoo Temple

Richard O. Cowan

The temple at Nauvoo occupied a pivotal position in the history of Latter-day Saint temples. To appreciate the pivotal nature of this structure one must first have an understanding of what earlier temples were like.

Functions of Earlier LDS Temples

In Old Testament times temples served two distinct functions: (1) God promised to manifest himself there to his people ( Ex. 25:8, 22 ); and (2) in them sacred ordinances were performed ( D&C 124:38 ). Elder James E. Talmage declared that a temple is "characterized not alone as [1] the place where God reveals Himself to man, but also as [2] the House wherein prescribed ordinances of the Priesthood are solemnized." [^1] Both of these temple functions would need to be part of the latter-day restoration of all things. The restoration of temple service unfolded one step at a time. Elder Talmage explained that there has been "a definite sequence of development" from one age to another in mankind's understanding of temple service, and that this fact has been reflected in changing temple designs. [^2]

113In June 1833 the Prophet drew a plat for the City of Zion. He envisioned 24 holy buildings at the city's center. These "temples" were to serve a variety of functions-"houses of worship, schools, etc." [^3] Shortly afterward the Lord revealed that the temple in Zion was to be a "place of thanksgiving for all saints, and for a place of instruction for all those who are called to the work of the ministry" ( D&C 97:13 ). Because the inhabitants of Zion were to be living on a celestial plane ( D&C 105:5), all these buildings would serve the first function of temples-places of communication between heaven and earth.

On 1 June 1833 the Lord also admonished the Saints to move forward with the building of the Kirtland Temple, and he revealed its basic arrangement. The chapel on the main floor was to be used p.114] for the sacrament, preaching, and prayer. A similar hall on the second floor was to house "the school of mine apostles" ( D&C 95:13-17 ). Five small rooms in the attic would be used as offices or classrooms.

114Thus, "the design and construction of the Kirtland Temple," Elder Boyd K. Packer explained, "was different from that of all other latter-day temples because its purpose was different." [^4] Specifically, President Brigham Young pointed out, the Kirtland Temple "had no basement in it, nor a font, nor preparations to give endowments for the living or the dead." [^5] These ordinances-the second basic function of temples-had not yet been restored. Hence, the temple was more of a multi-purpose building intended for general functions.

114Great spiritual blessings and remarkable manifestations followed the period of sacrifice during the temple's construction. For example, on 21 January 1836 during a meeting of Church leaders in the temple attic, "the heavens were opened," and the Prophet Joseph Smith "beheld the celestial kingdom of God, and the glory thereof." When he saw his brother Alvin in that kingdom, he "marveled" because Alvin had died before the gospel was restored, so had not been baptized. But the Lord declared: "All who have died without a knowledge of this gospel, who would have received it if they had been permitted to tarry, shall be heirs of the celestial kingdom of God" ( D&C 137:7 ). This assurance became the foundation of the Church's doctrine of salvation for the dead and of its great vicarious service in the temples.

114In several subsequent meetings, Saints reported seeing heavenly messengers and even the Savior himself. [^6] D&C 109:5 ). Thus the Kirtland Temple would serve the first function of temples-a place of revelation between God and man.

Just one week later, on 3 April, Joseph Smith and Oliver Cowdery beheld a series of remarkable visions. The Lord Jesus Christ personally accepted the temple and promised to manifest p.115] himself therein "if my people will keep my commandments, and do not pollute this holy house" ( D&C 110:8 ). Moses then bestowed "the keys of the gathering of Israel," (v 11), and Elias next conferred "the dispensation of the gospel of Abraham" (v 12). Finally, in fulfillment of Malachi's prophecy ( Mal. 4:5-6 ; see also D&C D&C 2 ), Elijah committed "the keys of this dispensation" in preparation for the "great and dreadful day of the Lord" (v 16). Through the sealing keys restored by Elijah, priesthood ordinances performed on earth can be "bound" or "sealed" in heaven. Latter-day Saints can also perform saving priesthood ordinances in behalf of loved ones who died without the opportunity of accepting the gospel in person. In this way the hearts of the children are turning to their fathers.

115Not long after these glorious occurrences had lifted the Saints, the forces of persecution increased. In less than two years the faithful were compelled to flee from their temple and homes in Kirtland, most gathering at Far West in northern Missouri. Here, in 1838, the Lord commanded the Saints "to build a house unto me, for the gathering together of my saints, that they may worship me" ( D&C 115:8 ). Once again the Lord promised to reveal "a pattern" for this temple (v 14). It is apparent that, like the Lord's house in Kirtland, the Far West Temple would not be intended primarily for sacred ordinances-the second function of temples which was not yet but about to be revealed.

115Before this temple could be built, however, the Saints in Missouri were ordered to leave the state or be exterminated. By 1839, they found a new haven in Illinois where they built the city of Nauvoo. Here the Lord would unfold the doctrine of salvation for the dead and restore vital temple ordinances. These developments would have far-reaching significance, and in turn would be reflected in future temple design.

Baptism for the Dead IntroducedHCAlthough the concept of salvation for the dead had been revealed as early as 1836, the specific practice of vicarious baptisms was taught for the first time in this present dispensation on 15 August 1840, at Nauvoo during the funeral of Seymour Brunson, a faithful member of the high council. [^7] The Prophet Joseph Smith p.116] read from Corinthians 15:29 n baptism for the dead and indicated that the Saints could "now act for their friends who had departed this life, and that the plan of salvation was calculated to save all who were willing to obey the requirements of the law of God." [^8] Almost immediately Church members began receiving the ordinance of baptism in the Mississippi River in behalf of deceased loved ones.

Baptism for the Dead IntroducedHC116The Prophet Joseph Smith continued to give emphasis to this principle. Preaching at a conference on 2 October 1841, he referred to "the wisdom and mercy of God in preparing an ordinance for the salvation of the dead." As they are baptized by proxy, "their names [are] recorded in heaven." Then, in the spirit of warning, he concluded: "Those Saints who neglect it in behalf of their deceased relatives, do it at the peril of their own salvation." [^9] With such encouragement, the Saints eagerly took advantage of the opportunity to make gospel ordinances and blessings available to their departed loved ones.

Construction of Nauvoo TempleHC116While these ordinances were being inaugurated, the Saints had already turned their attention to building the temple. [^10] On 19 January 1841, approximately one month after the governor of Illinois signed charters providing guidelines for the establishment of Nauvoo, Joseph Smith received a revelation ( D&C 124 ) which dealt with the building of the city in general, but gave particular emphasis to building the temple.

Construction of Nauvoo TempleHCJoseph Smith invited interested individuals to submit designs for the proposed temple. Several were received, but none suited him. When William Weeks, a recent convert who was an architect and builder from New England, came in with his plans, "Joseph Smith grabbed him, hugged him and said, `You are the man I want.'" [^11] Weeks became the general superintendent of temple construction. The Prophet testified that the basic plan had been given to him by revelation. For example, architect Weeks questioned the appropriateness of placing round windows on the side of the building. Joseph Smith, however, explained that the small rooms in the temple could be illuminated with one light at the center of each of these windows, and that "when the whole building was thus illuminated, the effect would be remarkably grand. `I wish you to carry outmy designs,'" the Prophet insisted. "`I have seen in vision the splendid appearance of that building illuminated, and will have it built according to the pattern shown me.'" [^12] This was one p.117] fulfillment of the Lord's promise to show the Prophet "all things pertaining to [that] house" ( D&C 124:42 ).

Construction of Nauvoo TempleHC117At first the Saints performed all their vicarious baptisms in the Mississippi River. The January 1841 revelation, however, declared that the ordinance of baptism for the dead should be performed in the Lord's House, and that He had temporarily allowed the Saints to perform this ordinance outside of the temple only in the days of their poverty. He therefore commanded them to provide an appropriate font in the temple. He would grant them "a sufficient time" to accomplish this, during which period he would continue to accept their baptisms performed in the river ( D&C 124:25-32 ).

Construction of Nauvoo TempleHC117During the summer and fall of 1841, the Saints eagerly pushed the temple's construction. On 2 October, when the Prophet preached on salvation for the dead, he emphatically declared: "There shall be no more baptisms for the dead, until the ordinance can be attended to in the Lord's House; and the Church shall not hold another General Conference, until they can meet in said house. For thus saith the Lord!" [^13]

Construction of Nauvoo TempleHC117Just five weeks later, the basement rooms were enclosed by frame walls and were covered by a temporary roof. Joseph Smith dedicated these facilities including a temporary wooden font and on Sunday, 21 November, the first baptisms were performed in the basement of the Nauvoo Temple. A large congregation gathered at 4 p.m. to witness this event. Elders Brigham Young, Heber C. Kimball, and John Taylor baptized about forty persons "in behalf of their ancestors." Elders Willard Richards, Wilford Woodruff, and George A. Smith performed the confirmation ordinances. Hereafter, the Prophet and members of the Twelve frequently officiated in the temple. [^14]

Endowment IntroducedHCThe Endowment and Other Blessings

Endowment IntroducedHCThe Saints had received a preliminary or partial endowment in Kirtland, and the time had now come to unfold these blessings more fully. Elder James E. Talmage described the temple endowment as a "course of instruction," reviewing key events in the history of mankind-the creation, fall of Adam, apostasy, restoration, and our eventual reunion with God-giving emphasis to the plan of redemption and to our living according to the high standards of the gospel. [^15] These instructions would need to be given in a place p.118] ( of privacy because they were sacred and would make known "things which have been kept hid from before the foundation of the world" ( &C 124:41 . Such a facility became available when Joseph Smith opened his store early in 1842. The second story of this 25 by 44 foot red brick structure included the Prophet's small personal office and a large area which came to be known as the "assembly room." Here the Relief Society was organized and the first endowments were given seven weeks later. [^16]

By means of two letters ( D&C 127 -128) written during the first week of September 1842, the Prophet gave yet further instructions concerning work for the dead. He emphasized the importance of having a recorder present, not only to keep an accurate record, but also to assure that each ordinance was done properly ( D&C 127:6 ; 128:3). The Prophet linked this keeping of proper records with the power to bind or loose on earth and have this action recognized in heaven ( D&C 128:8-9 ; compare Matt. 16:18-19 ). Because temple baptismal fonts symbolize the grave, the Prophet explained, they should be located "underneath where the living are wont to assemble" ( D&C 128:12-13 ). Finally, expanding on the writings of Paul, Joseph Smith declared that "they [the fathers]Â�without us cannot be made perfect-neither can we without our dead be made perfect," and that "their salvation is necessary and essential to our salvation" (v 15). This is so because in the celestial kingdom we will be organized as God's family according to the patriarchal order. Hence he taught that there must be "a welding link of some kind or other between the fathers and the children" p.119] (v 18). Vicarious ordinances for the dead, he concluded, were the means of establishing this link.

119Among the other blessings unfolded during these years was eternal marriage. Even though the first couple was married for eternity as early as 5 April 1841, there were relatively few of these "sealings" at first. In May 1843 the Prophet instructed the Saints that in order to attain the highest degree of the celestial kingdom, one must enter "the new and everlasting covenant of marriage" ( D&C 131:1-4 ). Two months later he recorded a revelation which, among other things, declared: "If a man marry him a wife in the world, and he marry her not by me nor by my word, and he covenant with her so long as he is in the world and she with him, their covenant and marriage are not of force when they are dead, and when they are out of the world" ( D&C 132:15 ). Following these instructions, the number of marriages for eternity increased.

The Temple Edifice as a Teaching Aid

As had been the case at Kirtland, the temple consisted of two large assembly rooms, one above the other. Visitors to the Nauvoo Temple referred to the room on the main floor as the "grand hall for the assemblage and worship of the people." Once again there were pulpits at each end of the room representing various offices in the priesthood. Its seats could accommodate 3,500, and were "arraigned with backs which [were] fitted like the seats in a modern railroad car so as to allow the spectator to sit and look in either direction, East or West." [^18]

119Comparing the initials on the Nauvoo pulpits with those of earlier temples reveals how the Saints' understanding of Church organization had developed during the intervening decade. Two years after plans had been drawn in 1833 for the temple in Zion, a revelation specifically identified the office of bishop with the Aaronic Priesthood ( D&C 107:87-88 ). Hence, at Kirtland the bishopric was grouped with the Aaronic rather than Melchizedek Priesthood. The initials "B.P.A." refer to the bishop as president of the Aaronic Priesthood. The increased importance of stakes and quorums was reflected in there being a pulpit in the Nauvoo Temple for the "Presidency of the Stake of Zion" (P.S.Z.), and the specific use of the letter "Q" to emphasize that the various priesthood presidencies headed quorum organizations.

Construction of Nauvoo TempleHC120The Nauvoo Temple's attic had an 88 by 29 foot central hall. On each side of this hall were six rooms, about 14 feet square, which served as private offices for Church leaders, as places where priesthood quorums could gather for prayer, or for the initiatory ordinances connected with the endowment. Some of these side rooms contained altars at which sacred sealings were performed. The attic facilities, like the font in the temple's basement, were intended to accommodate the temple ordinances which had just been revealed.

Construction of Nauvoo TempleHC120Unique features of the Nauvoo Temple's exterior were 30 pilasters containing symbolic ornamental stones. The base of each pilaster was a large stone depicting the crescent moon. Each capital featured the sun's face. In the cornice above each pilaster was a five-pointed star. Although a contemporary explanation as to what these symbols represent has not been found, these stones couldn't help but remind the Saints that there are three degrees of glory, and that faithfully receiving the ordinances of the temple is essential to attaining the highest exaltation in heaven.

Construction of Nauvoo TempleHC120Specific parts of the temple were completed and dedicated piecemeal so that ordinance work could begin as soon as possible. On 30 November 1845, for example, Brigham Young and twenty others who had received their endowments from Joseph Smith gathered to dedicate the attic story for ordinance work. During the next ten days they were busy preparing the attic for the presentation of the endowment. As had been done in the red brick store, the "council chamber" in the temple was divided by means of canvas partitions into separate areas representing distinct stages in man's eternal progress. Saints throughout the city contributed furnishings for these rooms. Potted plants were gathered for the area representing the Garden of Eden. The room on the east had a large gothic window and was furnished with fine carpets and wall hangings. This most beautiful area represented the celestial kingdom. When Joseph Fielding entered this part of the temple for the first time, he felt as though he had truly "gotten out of the world." [^19]

Endowment IntroducedHCEndowments were given beginning 10 December. During the next few weeks, even though the Saints were under increasing pressure to leave Nauvoo, they were eager to receive their long- anticipated temple blessings. February 4 had been selected as the day on which the exodus would begin. As this date drew closer, the p.121] pace in the temple intensified. On 3 February Brigham Young recorded:

Endowment IntroducedHC121Notwithstanding that I had announced that we would not attend to the administration of the ordinances, the House of the Lord was thronged all day, the anxiety being so great to receive, as if the brethren would have us stay here and continue the endowments until our way would be hedged up and our enemies would intercept us. But I informed the brethren that this was not wise, and that we should build more Temples, and have further opportunities to receive the blessings of the Lord, as soon as the saints were prepared to receive them. In this Temple we have been abundantly rewarded, if we receive no more. I also informed the brethren that I was going to get my wagons started and be off. I walked some distance from the Temple supposing the crowd would disperse, but on returning I found the house filled to overflowing.

Endowment IntroducedHC121Looking upon the multitude and knowing their anxiety, as they were thirsting and hungering for the word, we continued at work diligently in the House of the Lord. [^20]

Endowment IntroducedHC121Nearly 300 persons received their endowment on that day alone. During the eight weeks prior to the exodus, approximately 5,500 were endowed, fulfilling the Prophet Joseph Smith's compelling desire to make these blessings available to the Saints in Nauvoo. Erastus Snow testified: "The Spirit, Power, and Wisdom of God reigned continually in the Temple and all felt satisfied that during the two months we occupied it in the endowments of the Saints, we were amply paid for all our labors in building it." [^21]

121During the hectic time just before and following the exodus, some of the Saints ate, tended babies, or slept in the unused small rooms on the mezzanines above the two main halls. Some people even used the unfinished second-floor auditorium for recreational purposes. Concerned that such irregularities did not fit with the temple's function as a place where sacred ordinances are performed, Elder Heber C. Kimball insisted that only persons with official invitations be admitted to the temple. In this way he was able to restore proper order. This may be the origin of issuing "recommends" to those judged by local Church leaders to be worthy of temple activity. [^22]

Subsequent Developments

The process of unfolding the Saints' understanding of temples and temple service has continued. Vicarious endowments for the dead were inaugurated with the completion of the St. George p.122] Temple in 1877. For the first time the Saints had the privilege of returning to the temple and, while officiating in behalf of others, renewing their own understanding and appreciation of the teachings in the endowment.

122The St. George Temple, like the Kirtland and Nauvoo Temples, consisted basically of two large assembly rooms. Brigham Young and other Church leaders, however, indicated that future temples would contain facilities more especially suited for presenting temple ordinances. The Logan, Manti, and Salt Lake temples, dedicated during the next two decades, replaced the lower assembly room with a series of muraled rooms designed for presenting the endowment. Temples dedicated during the first half of the twentieth century eliminated the upper assembly room, retaining only the facilities linked with temple ordinances. Hence the changes begun with the pivotal Nauvoo temple have now been carried to their fullest extent. Twentieth century temples are not only places where God might reveal himself (the first function of temples), but they are specifically designed to accommodate sacred priesthood ordinances (the second and unique function of temples).
