Almon W. Babbitt and the Golden Calf

A. Gary Anderson

A familiar biblical story is the golden calf. While Moses was on Mt. Sinai receiving instructions from the Lord for ancient Israel, Aaron and the Israelites fashioned a golden calf, patterned after Egyptian idols. We wonder how the Israelites could so quickly revert to idol worship, ignoring the Lord's intervention in their behalf and forgetting that Moses was a prophet. Certainly idol worship could happen only in ancient Israel, not in an age when idol worship seems primitive. But idols still exist, although not made of gold or stone, and modern prophets continue to denounce idolatrous practices.

35One such incident occurred when the Lord, through Joseph Smith, reprimanded Almon W. Babbitt, a member of the Church:

35And with my servant Almon Babbitt, there are many things with which I am not pleased; behold, he aspireth to establish his counsel instead of the counsel which I have ordained, even that of the Presidency of my Church; and he setteth up a golden calf for the worship of my people. ( D&C 124:84 ; 19 January 1841)

35Let us examine the historical background of this admonition to understand why the Lord made such a strong statement and what difference it made in Almon W. Babbitt's life.

35Almon W. Babbitt, the son of Ira and Nancy Babbitt, was born 1 October 1812 in Cheshire, Berkshire County, Massachusetts. He was baptized into the Church in 1833, married Julia Ann Johnson, and settled in Kirtland that same year. Julia was the older sister of Benjamin Franklin Johnson, whose parents were Ezekiel and Julia Hills Johnson.

We are not sure whether Almon Babbitt joined the Church while still residing in Massachusetts or in Amherst, Ohio, where he became acquainted with Joel and David Johnson, brothers of his wife to be. In any case, he is mentioned in the history of the Johnson family when he accompanied David and Joel to Pomfret, New York, as a missionary. He carried a copy of the Book of Mormon which brought the Johnson family into the Church. [^1]

36In 1833, complaints were made against several couples in Kirtland, including the Babbitts. The couples were summoned to a bishop's council on 27 December 1833. Although the couples all appeared at the appointed time, the accusers failed to appear, so the council was adjourned. [^2] The nature of the accusations was not recorded. When Joseph Smith organized Zion's Camp in 1834, Almon W. Babbitt responded. Joseph Smith blessed him as a member of Zion's Camp, as Benjamin F. Johnson relates:

36[Joseph] told him of much good he would do in preaching the gospel, and how the hearts of people would be drawn towards him, and the greatness to which he would attain, etc., but that he would at last be overpowered and fall by the hand of an enemy. This Brother Babbitt also saw in a dream, which he related some years previous to his death. [^3]

36Babbitt and Frederick G. Williams were assigned to precede the main body of Zion's Camp to ascertain the attitudes of residents of Springfield, Illinois, toward the Saints and to obtain gun powder. Babbitt was ordained a member of the First Quorum of Seventy in Kirtland on 28 February 1835. Elder Babbitt went forth to preach the gospel in the state of New York, but was rebuked by the Kirtland High Council on 19 August 1835:

36A charge was preferred before a council of the Presidency, against Elder Almon W. Babbitt, for not keeping the Word of Wisdom; for stating the Book of Mormon was not essential to our salvation, and that we have no articles of faith except the Bible.

36Elder J. B. Smith testified that Elder Babbitt had assumed the prerogative of dictating to him in his preaching; and that he was not keeping the Word of Wisdom.

36Elder Babbitt said that he had taken the liberty to break the Word of Wisdom, from the example of President Joseph Smith, Jun., and others, but acknowledged that it was wrong; that he had taught the Book of Mormon and commandments as he had thought to be wisdom, and for the good of the cause; that he had not intended to dictate to Elder J. B. Smith, but only to advise with him.

36The council reproved Elder Babbitt, and instructed him to observe the Word of Wisdom, and commandments of the Lord in all things; also that it is not advisable for any Elder to take his wife with him on a mission to preach. [^4]

Babbitt returned from his mission in New York and visited Kirtland. He invited his seventeen-year-old brother-in-law, Benjamin F. Johnson, to accompany him on a mission to Benjamin's birthplace in Pomfret, New York. They departed in October of 1835, walking 150 miles through mud and storm. [^5]

37Later the same year, on 28 December, Babbitt came into conflict with Joseph Smith, as recorded in the History of the Church:

37Having previously preferred a charge against Almon W. Babbitt, for traducing my character, he was this morning called before the High Council, and I attended with my witnesses, and substantiated the charge against him; and he in part acknowledged his fault, but not satisfactorily to the Council; and after parleying with him a long time and granting him every indulgence that righteousness required, the Council adjourned without obtaining a full confession from him. [^6]

37Joseph Smith expressed concern for the opposition he and the work faced at that time, not only from Church members, but also from members of his own family. He was gratified on 2 January 1836 when his brother William and Almon Babbitt confessed their sins and were granted fellowship by the Kirtland High Council. [^7]

37Elder Babbitt served a mission to Canada and returned in 1838, leading a company of Saints to Missouri. Joseph Smith recorded:

37This morning I left Far West, with my council and Almon W. Babbitt, to visit the brethren on the Forks of Grand River, who had come from Canada with Elder Babbitt, and settled at that place contrary to counsel. [^8]

37Despite rejecting the Prophet's counsel as to where the Canadian Saints should settle, Babbitt and Benjamin F. Johnson traveled to Richmond, Missouri, to learn about the welfare of Joseph Smith and others who had been imprisoned there. [^9] Babbitt, his brother John, Benjamin Johnson and others found government work in Ft. Leavenworth when the Saints were being driven from Missouri. [^10] Babbitt was called home following the death of his brother-in-law, Lyman Sherman, in February 1839. After this time of persecution, Babbitt fled to Illinois and attended a Church conference in Quincy on 4 May 1839, at which time he received a church assignment:

37Almon W. Babbitt, Erastus Snow and Robert B. Thompson were appointed a traveling committee to gather up and obtain all the libelous reports and publications which had been circulated against the Church. [^11]

37In the fall of 1839, Almon Babbitt and his wife left Springfield for a mission to the eastern states. They stopped in Pleasant Garden, Indiana, and established a small branch of the Church. [^12]

38Oliver Granger, who was appointed president of the Kirtland Stake in 1840, mentioned Almon Babbitt in a letter to Joseph Smith. On 22 July 1840, Joseph Smith responded as follows:

38I am sorry to be informed not only in your letter, but from other respectable sources, of the strange conduct pursued in Kirtland by Elder Almon W. Babbitt. I am indeed surprised that a man having the experience which Brother Babbitt has had, should take any steps whatever calculated to destroy the confidence of the brethren in the Presidency or any of the authorities of the ChurchÂ�.

38I cannot forget the treatment I received in the house of my friends. These things continually roll across my mind, and cause me much sorrow of heart; and when I think that others who have lately come into the Church should be led to Kirtland instead of to this place, by Elder Babbitt; and having their confidence in the authorities lessened by such observations as [Elder Babbitt] has thought proper to make, as well as hearing all the false reports and exaggerated accounts of our enemies-I must say that I feel grieved in spirit, and cannot tolerate such proceedings-neither will I; but will endeavor to disabuse the minds of the Saints, and break down all such unhallowed proceedingsÂ�.

38If Brother Babbitt and the other brethren wish to reform the Church, and come out and make a stand against sin and speculation, &c., they must use other weapons than lies, or their object can never be effected; and their labors will be given to the house of the stranger, rather than to the House of the Lord.

38The proceedings of Brother Babbitt were taken into consideration at a meeting of the Church at this place, when it was unanimously resolved, that fellowship should be withdrawn from him until he make satisfaction for the course he has pursued: of which circumstance I wish you to apprise him without delay, and demand his license. [^13]

38Two letters Joseph Smith received from Kirtland in response to this letter led to further charges preferred against Almon Babbitt before the High Council in Nauvoo on 5 September 1840. The History of the Church lists three charges:

38First. For stating that Joseph Smith, Jun., had extravagantly purchased three suits of clothes while he was at Washington City, and that Sidney Rigdon had purchased four suits while at the same place, besides dresses in profusion for their families.

38Second. For having stated that Joseph Smith, Jun., Sidney Rigdon and Elias Higbee had said that they were worth one hundred thousand dollars each, while they were at WashingtonÂ�.

38Third. For holding secret councils in the Lord's House, in Kirtland, and for locking the doors of the house, for the purpose of prohibiting certain brethren in good standing in the Church, from being in the Council, thereby depriving them of the use of the house. [^14]

39The Council adjourned and met the following day at which time Joseph Smith withdrew the charges and the parties were reconciled. In spite of the conflict with Church leaders on three occasions in Kirtland, once in Missouri, and twice with the Prophet in Nauvoo, Almon Babbitt was called on 19 October 1840 as president of the Kirtland Stake. This calling came in a letter addressed to the Kirtland Saints, signed by Joseph and Hyrum Smith:

39The situation of Kirtland was brought before the general conference, held at this place on the 3rd instant, when it was resolved that Elder Almon W. Babbitt should be appointed to preside over the stake of Kirtland, and that he be privileged to choose his own counselors. We therefore hope that the Saints will hold up the hands of our beloved brother, and unite with him in endeavoring to promote the interests of the kingdom.

39It has been deemed prudent to advise the eastern brethren who desire to locate in Kirtland, to do so; consequently you may expect an increase of members in your stake, who probably will be but young in the faith, and who will require kind treatment. We therefore hope the brethren will feel interested in the welfare of the Saints, and will use all their endeavors to promote the welfare of the brethren who may think proper to take up their residence in that place. [^15]

39Three months later, on 19 January 1841, Almon Babbitt was specifically mentioned in a revelation given to Joseph Smith ( D&C 124:84 ): "There are many things with which I am not pleased; he sets up a golden calf for the worship of my people," i.e., he follows his own counsel instead of the counsel of the First Presidency. I have noted six separate times of conflict from 1833 to 1841. From these fragmentary items, we begin to sense an independence of mind in Brother Babbitt that led him not only to brilliant accomplishments, but into controversial disagreements with Church authorities. He apparently became so zealous in presiding over the Kirtland Saints that he could not abandon them and move with the Church to Nauvoo.

39Benjamin Johnson, after completing his mission in the East, was invited to visit the Babbitts in Kirtland; he was there when the revelation of January 19 was communicated to Brother Babbitt. Babbitt was devastated, realizing that his business in Kirtland would suffer as the Saints gathered to Nauvoo. Johnson recorded:

Brother Babbitt now saw that he would be broken up in business. He felt hurt by the rebuke in the revelation, and he was in great temptation to complain, and to turn his heel upon the Prophet. I now saw it wisdom by every influence to keep him from an unwise step and induce him to return to Nauvoo. He had brought many notes and claims against the Prophet or the church, and with these he might be tempted to do a great wrong to himself; and such was my love for him that I felt to make any sacrifice to promote his love for the gospel and his fellowship in the Church. [^16]

40Benjamin Johnson was invited to study law with Brother Babbitt. Almon graduated as an attorney-at-law from the State University at Cincinnati, eventually obtaining licenses to practice in six states. [^17] The partners bought a small law library and commenced their study. Benjamin hoped to promote his brother-in-law's love for the gospel and his fellowship in the Church. While in Kirtland, Johnson met Melissa Bloomfield LeBaron and they were married by Brother Babbitt on Christmas Day, 1841. The wedding took place in the house in which the Prophet Joseph had lived while he was in Kirtland. [^18]

40I will now examine the influence of this revelation on Brother Babbitt's life. The Lord pinpointed a problem that had surfaced in Babbitt's first eight years in the Church, and warned him about insisting on doing his "own thing" instead of following the counsel of the Lord given through appointed leaders.

40The latter-day work seemed to prosper under Babbitt's direction. In October 1841, he was again sustained as president of the stake by the Kirtland Saints. Babbitt wrote to the Prophet:

40There is now about 500 members in this place a good many churches adjacent to this stake. We have got the House of the Lord in mideling good repair. We are now about lying a new bell deck. We have made provisions for the poor. [^19]

span class="pg">40-41These poor included 100 English Saints who lacked the resources to travel to Nauvoo. The Kirtland Saints had bought some good farmland, according to Babbitt, and were converting the old steam mill into a furnace. Babbitt also proposed that a printing press be established in Kirtland to publish a religious paper, entitled The Olive Leaf, and that the poor Saints do the work. While optimistic about events in Kirtland, Babbitt complained of rumors from Nauvoo that Kirtland was to be abandoned: "There is so much written from your place that it would require more than the influence of an angel to keep down the excitement." [^20] In Babbitt's mind clear directions were needed. Should they plan to stay? What should be done with the temple and other property? Babbitt had reason to be concerned, for at the October Conference in Nauvoo he had been disfellowshipped, although he didn't know this when he wrote the letter. [^21] The minutes of the 2 October 1841 conference in Nauvoo outline the reason for this action:

41The Patriarch Hyrum Smith made remarks disapproving of the course pursued by some Elders in counteracting the efforts of the Presidency to gather the Saints, and in enticing them to stop in places not appointed for the gathering; particularly referred to the conduct of Elder Almon W. Babbitt of Kirtland.

41Elders Lyman Wight and Henry W. Miller testified that they had travelled in places where Elder A. W. Babbitt had, on his return from his visit to Nauvoo, taught doctrine contrary to the revelations of God, and detrimental to the interests of the Church.

41Moved and carried, that Elder Almon W. Babbitt be disfellowshipped until he shall make satisfaction. [^22]

41After receiving Babbitt's letter of October 19th, Hyrum Smith replied with a letter of instruction to Babbitt:

41All the Saints that dwell in that land are commanded to come away, for this is "Thus saith the Lord" therefore pay out no moneys [sic], nor properties for houses, nor lands in that country, for if you do you will lose them, for the time shall come, that you shall not possess them in peace, but shall be scourged with a sore scourge; yet your children may possess them, but not until many years shall pass away; and as to the organization of that branch of the Church, it is not according to the Spirit and will of God; and as to the designs of the leading members of that branch relative to the printing press, and the ordaining of Elders, and sending out Elders to beg for the poor, are not according to the will of God; and in these things they shall not prosper, for they have neglected the House of the Lord, the baptismal font, in this place, wherein their dead may be redeemed, and the key of knowledge that unfolds the dispensation of the fullness of times may be turned, and the mysteries of God be unfolded, upon which their salvation, and the salvation of the world, and the redemption of their dead depends; for "thus saith the Lord, there shall not be a general assembly for a general conference assembled together until the House of the Lord and the baptismal font shall be finished; and if we are not diligent the Church shall be rejected, and their dead also, saith the Lord." Therefore, dear brethren, any proceedings of the Saints otherwise than to put forth their hands with their might to do this work, is not according to the will of God, and shall not prosper; therefore, tarry not in any place whatever, but come forth unto this place from all the world, until it is filled up, and polished, and sanctified according to my word, saith the Lord. Come ye forth from the ends of the earth, that I may hide you from mine indignation that shall scourge the wicked, and then I will send forth and build up Kirtland, and it shall be polished and refined according to my word; therefore your doings and your organizations and designs in printing, or any of your councils, are not of me, saith the Lord, even so. Amen.

41HYRUM SMITH, Patriarch for the whole Church. [^23]

42Joseph Smith then sent a letter revoking the power of attorney given to Almon W. Babbitt in Kirtland. [^24] Zebedee Coltrin and Lester Brooks, counselors to President Babbitt, replied to Hyrum Smith's letter, indicating a desire to follow counsel and asking for further direction in connection with affairs in Kirtland:

42Dear Brethren in the Lord: We address you in the name of the Lord and in humility, for we truly feel humble in our present condition, and think it necessary to inform you how we are situated and as brethren of the same chosen family, ask what we shall do in future, so that a more perfect understanding may exist between this stake and Nauvoo. We hold this truth the dearest of all, that if we are not one, we are not the Lord's, and if we do not uphold each other's hands, the Lord will not uphold ours.

42Our president, Elder Almon W. Babbitt, as appears by the minutes of your October conference, has been disfellowshipped till he makes satisfaction, etc. Having the spirit of the Lord and his Prophet to guide you in all your doings, we know you go for righteousness; and as we live to grow wiser and better, we do sincerely hope and pray, that not only satisfaction on Brother Babbitt's case, but on everything relative to Kirtland, may be made. [^25]

42Coltrin and Brooks further affirmed that Babbitt had disengaged himself from his mercantile business, then explained their motives in establishing a printing office. They asked for forgiveness and expressed a desire to be in harmony with the counsel of the Brethren in Nauvoo.

42President Joseph Smith replied to this letter on 15 December 1841:

42It remains for Almon W. Babbitt to offer satisfaction, if he wishes so to do, according to the minutes of the conference. You are doubtless all well aware that all the stakes, except those in Hancock county, Illinois, and Lee county, Iowa, were discontinued some time since by the First Presidency, as published in the Times and Seasons; but as it appears that there are many in Kirtland who desire to remain there, and build up that place, and as you have made great exertions according to your letter, to establish a printing press, and take care of the poor, &c., since that period, you may as well continue operations according to your designs, and go on with your printing, and do what you can in righteousness to build up Kirtland, but do not suffer yourselves to harbor the idea that Kirtland will rise on the ruins of Nauvoo. It is the privilege of brethren emigrating from any quarter to come to this place, and it is not right to attempt to persuade those who desire it, to stop short. [^26]

Almon W. Babbitt persisted in rejecting the counsel of the First Presidency and even predicted the downfall of Nauvoo before Kirtland, specifically its demise in 1843. Five letters were written to the high council in Nauvoo to illustrate this counsel given in Springfield, Illinois, on 10 August 1842:

43To the High Council in Nauvoo,

43Whereas many false reports have gone abroad which have a tendency to scatter the work of God, we, the constituted authorities of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints in this place, take the liberty to represent to you by letter, and through our beloved Brother Lyman Wight the instruction we have received from President Almon W. Babbitt, when on his way from your place to Kirtland, Ohio.

43He stated in this place that the people both in Illinois and Missouri were making preparations to drive the saints from Nauvoo, and that notwithstanding there was a scourge prepared for Kirtland, there was one also for Nauvoo, and would fall on them first, and that the brethren ought not to be moved by every whim, but stay where they are, for there was a combination of the two States against Nauvoo and the Saints would be driven from there within one year, and Kirtland must be built up and would be and that too immediately; therefore Kirtland was the place for him, although he would not advise us to the course the saints should go. Edwin T. Meriam, President. [^27]

43Similar letters were written by David Elliot, Aaron Palmer, George Stringham, and John Priest.

43Almon W. Babbitt moved from Kirtland, Ohio, in 1842 and eventually settled in Ramus, Illinois. Benjamin Johnson had preceded Brother Babbitt to Nauvoo and immediately visited the Prophet Joseph Smith to defend his brother-in-law, Bro. Babbitt. He recorded,

43I conversed with [Joseph Smith] upon the business matters between him and Brother Babbitt, told him it had been a time of test to Brother Babbitt's integrity, but with his arm around him I felt he would remain true to the cause. He said he loved Brother Babbitt, that he was capable of great good, and that the troubles should all be bridged over, and Brother Babbitt should have no reason to complain. It was the first time I had ever spoken to the Prophet with feelings and opinions of my own, and he seemed to love me more, because of my love for Brother Babbitt. [^28]

43When Brother Babbitt arrived in Nauvoo, Benjamin Johnson recorded the following:

43We found Brother Joseph in a happy mood and glad to see Brother Babbitt. When business matters were brought forward relating to notes bought from outsiders against him or the Church, Brother Joseph said to him, "Now, Brother Almon, we will not disagree, for here is Brother Benjamin; you have all confidence in him and so have I; and now let us leave all our differences to him and stand by it, and be good friends forevermore," to which Brother Babbitt agreed. [^29]

44What a great example of the Prophet Joseph's ability to forgive! Brother Babbitt was appointed presiding elder of the Ramus Branch on 13 March 1843, and was restored to full fellowship at a conference in Nauvoo on 10 April 1843. [^30] While Almon Babbitt was in Nauvoo, he apparently taught a Sunday School class. Joseph Smith III recalled attending Sunday School in the grove on the hillside near the temple.

44Here I was a scholar in a class of about sixty boys under the teaching of Almon W. Babbitt, who as my memory now recalls him, was a kindly, friendly pleasant teacherÂ�. He was a man of good presence, and quite able to teach. [^31]

44The Smith family must have had confidence in Brother Babbitt, because when Joseph Smith III was baptized by his father in November of 1843, Almon W. Babbitt was asked to confirm him a member of the Church. [^32]

44Many appointments and assignments came to Brother Babbitt in 1843 and 1844. He was called on a mission to France on 6 May 1844, but did not fulfill this assignment. He was elected to the state legislature for Hancock County, Illinois. He was among those to receive his endowment in the Nauvoo Temple on 12 May 1844 and became a member of the Council of Fifty by 6 May 1843. [^33] Apparently, Babbitt had also counseled Joseph Smith to order the auvoo Expositor destroyed as a public nuisance. [^34]

44Just seven days before the martyrdom, Joseph Smith sent orders to Captain Almon W. Babbitt, commander of the company of Ramus, to come to Nauvoo and defend the city. He refused to go, saying it was foolish and objected to any of the company going. When the company was marshalled into line, Babbitt warned, "If any of you go, not one will ever get to Nauvoo alive." But John Smith stepped forward and promised, "Every man that goes at the call of the Prophet shall go and return safe, and not a hair of his head shall be lost; and I bless you in the name of the Lord." The company arrived safely in Nauvoo, although the mob fired at them along the way. [^35]

44Heber C. Kimball referred to the rebellion of Babbitt and others at a conference in which members of the Twelve spoke in Boston in September 1843:

<_INDEX none_="">Regional Studies, Illinois, Anderson-Almon W. Babbitt, pg. 44-45We do not profess to be polished stones like Elders Almon W. Babbitt, George J. Adams, James Blakeslee, and Eli P. Maginn, &c.; but we are rough stones out of the mountain; and when we roll through the forest, we knock the bark from the trees, it does not hurt us, even if we should get a corner knocked off occasionally; for the more we roll about, and knock the corners off, the better we are; but if we were polished and smooth when we get the corners knocked off, it would deface us. [^36]

45The day before the martyrdom, John Smith visited his nephew, Joseph, in Carthage Jail, who gave him the following instructions: "Tell Almon W. Babbitt I want him to come and assist me as an attorney at my expected trial tomorrow before Captain R. F. Smith." Patriarch John Smith left the jail and three hours later delivered the message to Babbitt in Macedonia. But Babbitt replied, "You are too late, I am already engaged on the other side." [^37] This statement, with no further comment, might seem to indicate that Babbitt did not support the Prophet at the time of the martyrdom, but a letter by Joseph E. Johnson, a brother-in-law, indicates otherwise. According to Johnson, they visited Governor Ford the day he arrived in Carthage and delivered a message from Ford to Joseph Smith in Nauvoo at considerable risk to themselves because of mob action. Babbitt also visited with Joseph Smith on the day of the martyrdom. [^38]

45The Saints supported Babbitt in the fall of 1844 as a state representative from Hancock County. [^39] Andrew Jensen summarizes Brother Babbitt's activities in Nauvoo:

45He visited the Prophet Joseph in Carthage jail on the day of the martyrdom and remained with the Twelve as against the claims of Sidney Rigdon and others. He rendered efficient legal service to the Church during the persecutions and mobbings in Illinois, and when the Illinois legislature, in January, 1845, was discussing the unconditioned surrender of the Nauvoo city charter, Elder Babbitt was at Springfield laboring diligently as a lawyer in defending the rights of his people, but to no purpose; the charter was repealed. As a member of a committee appointed to formulate a petition to the Federal Government, in behalf of the Saints, we find Almon W. Babbitt's name attached to the historical document addressed to Pres. James K. Polk, dated April 24, 1845. The petition, which was unheeded by the chief executive asked for redress on behalf of a "disfranchised and long afflicted people" and asked the president to assist the Saints to obtain a home where they could enjoy their "rights of conscience and religion unmolested." After the departure of the Apostles into the wilderness, in February, 1846, the affairs of the Church at Nauvoo were left in charge of a committee, consisting of Almon W. Babbitt, Joseph L. Heywood and John S. Fullmer; and after the famous battle of Nauvoo, in September, 1846, these three men signed the treaty, by which the Saints agreed to surrender the city to the mob. [^40]

46Heber C. Kimball was upset by the amount of money that Babbitt's committee received for the Kimball home in Nauvoo. At Garden Grove, Heber learned that his home had been sold for thirty-five yoke of oxen. Kimball wrote the Church trustees, requesting that the poor remaining in Nauvoo use the oxen to come West. [^41] But in a later sermon, he said: "My house was sold for seventeen hundred dollars intended to be used to help gather the Saints; but Almon W. Babbitt put it in his pocket, I suppose." [^42]

46Among other things, Babbitt, under Brigham Young's direction, attempted to obtain the Egyptian mummies which Joseph Smith had acquired in Kirtland. Interest had developed from the writings of Abraham which Joseph Smith published in conjunction with scrolls found with the mummies. He wrote to Brigham Young on 31 January 1847: "William Smith has got the mummies from Mother Smith and refuses to give them up." [^43]

46Orson Hyde, as a member of the Quorum of the Twelve, had been placed in charge of the Saints after the exodus in February 1846. He tried to persuade the Saints to leave Nauvoo to avoid further conflict with their enemies. An article in the St. Louis American boldly stated that if A. W. Babbitt and those who were trying to persuade the Saints to remain in Nauvoo were successful, "There will be trouble after June next, and it will not be so easy to allay the storm as it was last year." Babbitt was trying to complete his business as trustee of the Church in disposing of property and felt that he would be protected from the mob as long as sufficient numbers of Saints remained in Nauvoo. Orson Hyde told Babbitt that an angel had appeared to him to confirm that the complete exodus from Nauvoo was urgent. Apparently, however, even hearing this did not deter Elder Babbitt from encouraging Saints to remain in Nauvoo, and this added to Elder Hyde's frustrations. [^44] Wandle Mace describes this situation in his autobiography:

46The farmers on the Iowa side of the river were wanting help to harvest their grain. Elder Hyde was writing, urging the brethren to get over the river while Babbitt, sneering, remarked, "I [guess] Old Hyde's getting religion." Instead of using his influence to have the people comply with the council of an Apostle he laid every obstacle in the way to prevent them from complying. [^45]

46In spite of this conflict with Elder Hyde, when the Nauvoo Temple was dedicated on 1 May 1846, under Orson Hyde's direction, Brother Babbitt addressed "the meeting which He did do to the edefycation [sic] of the Saints." [^46]

47Before Babbitt left Nauvoo, he received the fulness of the priesthood and was sealed to his first wife, Julia Ann Johnson, and to Mary Tulley. He was also sealed for time to Dulcena Didamia Johnson Sherman on 24 January 1846. Sister Johnson was his sister-in-law. They had four children: Don Carlos, Almon W., Julia, and Nancy Marie. In 1846, he also married Maria Lawrence, who had been sealed to Joseph Smith in 1843, but she and their only child died the next year. [^47] Babbitt left Nauvoo for Utah in 1848. Andrew Jensen summarized Babbitt's activities upon his arrival in Utah:

47Elder Babbitt came to the Valley in 1848, and when a memorial praying for Statehood had been prepared by the Saints, he was, by a joint vote of the "General Assembly of the State of Deseret," elected a delegate to Congress to convey the memorial to Washington. He left for that city in the fall of 1849, and arriving at the capital he "sought the earliest opportunity to present to Congress the public documents of which he was the bearer, as well as his own credentials as delegate from the Provisional State of Deseret," but Congress would not permit Col. Babbitt to take a seat, and instead of granting Statehood, as prayed for, the Territory of Utah was created in 1850. [^48]

47Almon Babbitt spent some time with the Saints in Kanesville, Iowa, in connection with his trip to Washington, DC. Orson Hyde, of the Quorum of the Twelve, was the leading Church official in Kanesville at the time and also the editor of the local periodical, The Frontier Guardian. Babbitt was mentioned several times in the Guardian, having conflicted with Orson Hyde and others in Kanesville. Orson Hyde wrote a letter to Missouri Republicans, apologizing for Babbitt because of his open opposition to the Republican party and his support of the Democrats: "Mr. A. W. Babbitt is a member of our Church, but professes to be no religious Mormon." [^49]

About 1,500 Saints were settled on Pottawattomie lands at Kanesville. Even though they were assembling in Utah, they considered it to be an asset to those migrating to be able to receive assistance at Kanesville. The Iowa legislature had provided for the organization of counties on these lands, and the Saints petitioned for a post office and county government. Both the Whig and Democratic parties solicited the Mormon vote in the upcoming elections. Orson Hyde and others supported the Whig party, and candidates urged the Saints to do likewise, hoping to receive some government protection and rights. That course embittered the Democratic Party of Iowa, even though the Whig Party won nationally. Almon W. Babbitt advocated the Democratic Party. He charged that Orson had shown him a $1,000 check from the Whig party, paid to Orson for services rendered. The Guardian accused Babbitt of trying to sway the vote during a drinking party thrown by the Democrats, in which "whiskey, brandy, and champaign ran as freely as water." [^50]

48Babbitt was consequently disfellowshipped by a conference of Seventies. [^51] This controversy created dissension among the Saints but, in 1849, Orson Hyde announced in the Guardian a reconciliation between himself and Babbitt:

48We have concluded to drop and settle the matter, and be friends so far as the Church is concerned. Yet we confess that we have not made a Whig of him; and we think that he will do us the justice to say that he has not made a very deep democratic impression upon us. [^52]

48As a consequence of these political incidents, Church leaders saw the futility of trying to live peaceably on the Pottowattomie lands. By 1852, Kanesville and the surrounding country were practically deserted by the Saints. The name of the principal settlement, Kanesville, was soon changed to Council Bluffs. [^53]

48Almon Babbitt made ten trips between Salt Lake City and the East in the year 1852. [^54] He spent much of his time in Kanesville, Iowa. He was the editor and proprietor of the Western Bugle in Kanesville from 28 April to 21 July 1852. The following advertisement in the Bugle demonstrates his continued interest in supporting himself through his law training:

48A.W. Babbitt Attorney at Law, Counceller and Solicitor in Chancery. Kanesville, Iowa

48Will attend to all business entrusted to him in the 6th Judicial District; he will also attend to business in the Supreme Court of the State and United States. Apr. 28, 1852

48While in Kanesville, Mary Winters Pratt appreciated help given to her by Brother Babbitt:

One bright Monday morning in the early days of May, the Honorable A.W. Babbitt called at our door and said, "Sister Pratt, I am just starting for the valley, and shall expect to meet you on my return journey, in some of the companies that are going out this yearÂ�. He then said, "I have put a hundred dollars in the emigration fund with the express purpose that you have a good, substantial, comfortable wagon to make the journey in across the plains, and I want you to be sure that you get it." She thanked him very sincerely for this kind thoughtfulness in our behalf, and he was off, on his long journey to the westward. We looked at each other in astonishment, mother and I, for this promised help and blessing had seemed to drop right down from heaven in our behalf. [^55]

49Thomas L. Kane of Philadelphia, good friend to the Mormons, kept Brigham Young informed of affairs in Washington, DC, which related to the Mormons. Among other things, Kane felt obligated to advise Brigham Young concerning Almon W. Babbitt:

49Mr. A. W. Babbitt has proved himself unfit to be trusted with the care of any of your interests and this alike by an abasement of his personal character, and an unfaithfulness to his trusts.

49I was first unfavorably impressed as to Mr. Babbitt's integrity, about the time of his first calling upon me; by learning that, with the very mistaken view of thereby raising himself in the opinion of strangers, he made light of his religion and intimated his connection with it to be of the slightest. After this, I heard of his associating freely with a rabble of dissolute persons at Washington, whose acquaintance was necessarily detrimental to the public estimate of his character. But it was not till a visit to Washington (undertaken at his entreaty to work for his seat in the House) made me essay a cooperation of effort with him, that his graver faults were fully disclosed. He not only manifested himself a bad man, but a very weak one. Washington after all, is a place where even men that intrigue, intrigue like men. Mr. Babbitt evinced an almost morbid fondness for such as you might expect in a mischievous childÂ�. Thus he alienated and disgusted serious and sober men of both sides, and himself mainly defeated his own electionÂ�. I cannot put aside the conviction that Mr. Babbitt has also been striving to advance private interests of his own, regardless if not at the direct expense of yours. Let me now leave this matter; I hope for once and for all. I find I have written upon it more fully than I thought to. Judge from this of my anxiety lest accident or misapprehension should give this unhappy man another opportunity to misrepresent, and betray you. [^56]

49On 19 July 1851, John M. Bernhisel confirmed Kane's report to Brigham Young: "The senators in Congress could not comprehend how we (the people in Great Salt Lake Valley) could select such an immoral man as Babbitt for our delegate." [^57] Elder Babbitt returned to Utah, and despite these negative reports, was appointed secretary of the Territory of Utah by President Franklin Pierce in 1852, which position he filled until his death. Thomas L. Kane wrote to President Pierce in 1854 about the appointment of Brigham Young as Utah Territorial Governor and other government affairs:

I should not risk exerting myself further in the matter of the Utah Governorship, but for the intelligence that "Mr Babbitt is pressing a hostile nomination." This I fear to be Mr. A. W. Babbitt, Secretary, late of Iowa; and it becomes my duty to warn you against this individual, known by me as an entirely untrustworthy and unprincipled person, whose word should in no extremity be relied upon. I am your authority for this fact-to be named as such to any who may wish it stated under a personal responsibility. [^58]

50In a letter to Brigham Young in July 1856, William Smith, Joseph's brother, noted that Almon Babbitt was accepted by the Saints. But Babbitt had stolen from William and had advised his wife to separate from him. William also said that if President Young approved of such acts of injustice and meanness, the Lord would judge him for his "damnable deeds and send [him] to Hell." [^59]

50Almon Babbitt's final trip to Washington, DC, was to procure supplies for the new statehouse in Salt Lake City. He left in April 1856 and stopped at Council Bluffs on his return journey. Local Mormons informed Babbitt that there had been some Indian problems in Nebraska of late, and that it would be wise to wait for a large wagon train for safety. John Jacques, a member of the Martin Handcart Company, described Babbitt as follows:

50A. W. Babbitt, dressed in corduroy pants, woolen overshirt and felt hat, called as he was passing west. He seemed in high glee, his spirits elastic, almost mercurial. He had started with one carriage for Salt Lake City with the mail and a considerable amount of money. He was very confident that he could be in Salt Lake within fifteen days. [^60]

50Four of Babbitt's teamsters and a Mrs. Wilson and her child started off for the West, rejecting the counsel to wait to travel with a larger company. They were attacked by Indians on 25 August 1856 while encamped on Prairie Creek. The Indians killed Mrs. Wilson, her child, and two teamsters. One teamster was wounded and one escaped unhurt. Colonel Babbitt, following days later, discovered the tragedy. Captain Wharton at Ft. Kearny offered him a mounted escort if he would only wait a few days, but Babbitt refused to listen, arguing that he could not be delayed. He left the next day with his own outfit. He and three other men were killed by Cheyenne Indians near Ash Hollow (100 miles northwest of Ft. Kearny, Nebraska) on 7 September 1856. The description of Babbitt's death indicated that he was a big man and went down fighting:

50The Colonel fought like a tiger, fired all his arms, then clubbed his rifle and fought the whole twelve savages, disputing every inch, as he slowly backed up to his carriage for protection behind. He had seriously wounded several, when one, more cowardly than the others, jumped up into the wagon, and, with the tomahawk, killed a brave and noble man. [^61]

51Brigham Young advised Joel H. Johnson to take his sister Julia Babbitt and her four children to Council Bluffs to investigate her husband's death. Sister Babbitt died there on 23 October 1857, and was buried near relatives. She died in full fellowship of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, beloved and respected by all who knew her, both saints and sinners. [^62] Benjamin F. Johnson cared for the Babbitt children after their mother's death; ten years later he donated a Utah farm worth $1,000 to the family. [^63]

51This is a tragic story of a very talented man who had many opportunities to serve in the Church and government. One marvels at the patience of Church leaders, who continued to trust him despite his opposition. The Lord has certainly warned us about putting our self-interests before him and the things of the kingdom. Although Almon Babbitt was warned by the Lord in a revelation to surrender his will to the will of the Lord, his independent personality seemed to dominate him. Prior to his death, Babbitt even referred to the blessing given him by Joseph Smith at the time of Zion's Camp, which also warned him that in spite of his greatness, he would at last be overpowered and fall by the hand of an enemy. Ringing loud and clear from the Book of Mormon are these words:

51O that cunning plan of the evil one! O the vainness, and the frailties, and the foolishness of men! When they are learned they think they are wise, and they hearken not unto the counsel of God, for they set it aside, supposing they know of themselves, wherefore, their wisdom is foolishness and it profiteth them not. And they shall perish.

51But to be learned is good if they hearken unto the counsels of God. ( 2 Ne. 9:28-29 )

51The following words of counsel from Jacob are also appropriate:

51Wherefore, brethren, seek not to counsel the Lord, but to take counsel from his hand. For behold, ye yourselves know that he counseleth in wisdom, and in justice, and in great mercy, over all his works. ( Jacob 4:10 )

51Certainly golden calves are among us. "Do your own thing" rings loudly in our modern society. Many look upon obedience to the Lord's inspired servants as weakness. It is hoped that we can learn to follow the Lord's prophets and avoid the consequences of sin. We must avoid the pitfall of insisting that we are right and that the Lord and his servants are wrong.
