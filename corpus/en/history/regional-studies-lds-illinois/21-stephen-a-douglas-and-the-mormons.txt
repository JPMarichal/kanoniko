Various authors, Regional Studies in LDS History: Illinois ( 1995)

Stephen A. Douglas and the Mormons

Bruce A. Van Orden

From the beginning, members of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints have naturally been defensive about slanderous attacks against their faith, their church, and their leaders. Especially is this true when a prominent individual lashes out against them. Governors Daniel Dunklin and Lilburn W. Boggs of Missouri, Governors Thomas Carlin and Thomas Ford of Illinois, and United States Presidents Martin Van Buren, James Buchanan, and Ulysses S. Grant are among those who offended the Saints because of their statements and actions. Another prominent nineteenth century political figure, Senator Stephen A. Douglas of Illinois, has also been categorized an enemy to the Mormons.

359In an editorial in the Church Section of the Deseret News on 5 December 1936, Senator Douglas was held up as an "example of infamy" and an "enemy of truth." "The case of Stephen Arnold Douglas, aspirant to the presidency of the United States, is a classic instance of man's impotency to rise on an appeal to prejudice, intolerance and falsehood," the editorial read. "As warned the Prophet Joseph Smith, Douglas' chances of election to the presidency were doomed when he attacked the character of the Prophet and the Latter-day Saints in his election appeal. He went down to defeat before the great Lincoln and today is merely a memory, a figure flitting across the political scene in a crucial period of America's history." [^1]

Does Stephen A. Douglas deserve this severe indictment? What did he do or say against the Mormons that was so terrible? What actually did Joseph Smith say to Douglas? Was Douglas ever a friend to the Saints, or was he always an enemy? How do the actions and words of Douglas toward the Mormons compare with those of Abraham Lincoln, with whom he is so often unfavorably compared? Do today's Mormons generally understand the trying circumstances that were wrenching the Union in the 1850s? Could p.360] all of us better understand Stephen A. Douglas and his place in history, both in Mormon and American history, by analyzing the historical milieu which affected his actions and words, both favorable and unfavorable, toward the Mormons?

360 Mormon traditions regarding Stephen A. Douglas go something like this: When old Missouri enemies sought to extradite Joseph Smith to Missouri, the Prophet repeatedly called upon a young country judge, Stephen A. Douglas, to help keep him (Joseph Smith) out of the clutches of the Missourians. On one occasion in 1843, Joseph Smith dined with Judge Douglas and rehearsed the depredations of the Saints at the hands of their Missouri enemies. Douglas agreed that the Saints deserved redress for their grievances, whereupon the Prophet solemnly prophesied to the thirty-year-old politician that he would later aspire to the presidency of the United States and that if he ever turned against the Prophet, the Lord would punish him and he would fail in his presidential bid. Sure enough, in later years Douglas did become a presidential aspirant, but he deliberately and maliciously turned against the Saints and their leaders by accusing them of all manner of evil and said that they should be punished by the United States. Flaunting Joseph Smith's prophecy, Stephen A. Douglas ran for the presidency and had every reason to believe that he would be successful in his quest. But according to the justice and wisdom of God, Douglas was ignominiously defeated by previously unknown and unappreciated Abraham Lincoln, who would go on to preserve the Union under the influence of the Almighty. The unrepentant Stephen A. Douglas was brought low to his death bed while still in the prime of his life by a just God. [^2]

360This paper attempts to cast aside these traditional beliefs by detailing the relationships of Stephen A. Douglas with the Mormons and placing them in their greater historical setting. I thus seek to create a greater understanding of both the Saints and Douglas and their respective positions during the difficult two decades between 1840 and 1860.

I

Stephen A. Douglas, born in 1813 in Brandon, Vermont, and a resident in Canandaigua, New York, between 1830 and 1832, was a contemporary, both in location and in age, of many of Mormonism's p.361] founders. When at age twenty he realized that his family would be unable to support him in his education, Douglas, who was always energetic and ambitious, set out for the great West to make his fortune. He settled in central Illinois in 1833, became involved in local Democratic Party politics, and by 1836 was elected a member of the Illinois legislature. Two years later, when only twenty-five, he became a candidate for the United States Congress and lost by a mere thirty-seven votes. Undaunted, he continued to exercise leadership in the state's Democratic Party and was known as the "Generalissimo" of the party. [^3] In 1840 he was appointed as Illinois' secretary of state and in the following year was named as the state's youngest ever Supreme Court justice by Governor Thomas Carlin, a fellow Democrat. Not remaining long in any position, Douglas was elected to the U.S. House of Representatives in 1843 and to the Senate in 1847. He rose to national prominence during the sectional conflict that tore at the nation in 1850 and, more than any other single senator, led the forces which succeeded in securing the famous Compromise of 1850. Douglas was a leading contender for the Democratic nomination to the presidency in 1852 and 1856. Both times various political forces prevented him from obtaining the nomination, although he was better known and generally conceded as better qualified than either Franklin Pierce or James Buchanan. He received his party's nomination in 1860, but the Democratic Party divided asunder immediately after his nomination, which in effect denied him any legitimate hope for victory. Throughout his tempestuous political career, Stephen A. Douglas neglected his health, particularly in 1860 and 1861 during his struggles to preserve the Union. Finally his health totally failed him and he died at age forty-eight on 3 June 1861, a few months after the beginning of the Civil War.

II

In 1839, when the exiled Mormons began to stream across the Mississippi River from Missouri into Illinois, Stephen A. Douglas, though but twenty-six years old, was already recognized as the foremost Democrat in western Illinois. The Whig Party enjoyed a narrow margin of support at that time in that region, and with the influx of thousands of new citizens, both the Whigs and the Democrats wooed the Mormons to capture political power. Douglas, p.362] representing the Democrats in this effort, quickly built friendships with the leaders of the Saints, particularly gaining the confidence of the Prophet Joseph Smith. [^4] Douglas assisted John C. Bennett in steering the Mormons' Nauvoo City Charter through the state legislature and, as the new secretary of state, signed the seal for the charter on 16 December 1840. Shortly after he became a Supreme Court justice, Douglas visited Nauvoo in the spring of 1841 and inspected the Nauvoo legion. In a public address, he praised the Mormons for the improvements they had made in Nauvoo and commended them for their enterprise and industry. In answer to outside criticism, he officially ruled that the legion's organization was in harmony with governmental regulations. This visit elicited the following laudatory comment about Douglas from Joseph Smith: "No man stands more deservedly high in the public estimation, as an able and profound jurist, politician, and statesman." [^5]

362Stephen A. Douglas served two years as a state Supreme Court justice, spending the majority of his time on his nine-county circuit in the west-central part of Illinois, which included Nauvoo in Hancock County. "Douglas attacked the circuit court dockets with vigor and characteristic energy," [^6] arousing the admiration of the citizens and undeniably enhancing his chances for future political advancement. By 1843 Douglas was in the U.S. House of Representatives, but his judicial service tagged him with the title of "Judge" for the rest of his career. It was as circuit judge that Douglas best aided Joseph Smith and the Nauvoo Saints.

In early June 1841, when Joseph Smith temporarily left Hancock County, the Prophet was arrested by the Adams County sheriff on charges of being a fugitive from justice from the state of Missouri. Joseph hastened to the county seat, Quincy, obtained a writ of habeas corpus, and met with Judge Stephen A. Douglas, who had just arrived at his residence in town. Douglas consented to give Joseph a hearing in Monmouth, seventy-five miles northeast of Nauvoo. At the hearing, the circuit court was filled with spectators and great excitement about a possible lynching prevailed in the anti-Mormon town. Judge Douglas twice fined the Warren County sheriff for not controlling the rabid crowd. When defense arguments concerning the atrocities against the Mormons in Missouri were passionately presented by a leading Whig politician of the area, Orville Browning, many in the courtroom were moved to tears, including Judge Douglas. Without considering the validity of p.363] the charges against Joseph Smith, Douglas dismissed the case on procedural grounds the next day. His decision aroused strong suspicions in the area that he had entered into a political arrangement with Joseph Smith. Whig newspapers sharply accused Douglas of openly courting the Mormon vote. [^7]

Thereafter the Whigs forgot to woo the Mormons and stepped up their attacks on the Saints as the election year 1842 approached. Douglas, now identified with the Mormons, became a target for many of the opposition's blasts. He appointed partisans of the Church to court positions in Hancock County, thus helping arouse the intense anti-Mormon opposition in Warsaw, Nauvoo's rival for supremacy in the county. The Mormon gratitude toward Judge Douglas appeared in a political letter of Joseph Smith, published 1 January 1842 in the Church's publication Times and Seasons. "We care not a fig for Whig or Democrat: they are both alike to us; but we shall go for our friends, our TRIED FRIENDS, and the cause of human liberty which is the cause of God," Joseph wrote. "We claim the privileges of freemen, and shall act accordingly. Douglas is a Master Spirit, and his friends are our friends-we are willing to cast our banners on the air, and fight by his side in the cause of humanity, and equal rights-the cause of liberty and the law." [^8] A month later at a city council meeting in Nauvoo, Joseph Smith spoke of the great privileges of the Nauvoo Charter and prophesied that Judge Douglas would never set aside a law made by the city council. [^9] The Prophet's attitude toward Douglas was publicized in Whig Party journals throughout 1842 and the judge was cast for the next several years as a "friend of the Mormons." The Whig candidate for governor that year was former governor Joseph Duncan. Duncan tried to raise a "crusade" against the Mormons in the campaign because the Democrats had so avidly supported the Church. Whig newspapers published lurid accounts of Mormon "enormities" and of a "corrupt bargain" between Joseph Smith and the Democratic party, principally in the person of Judge Douglas. Douglas replied in kind, inveighing against Duncan's demagoguery and warned that anti-Mormons in western Illinois were arming themselves to repeat the Missouri scenes of violence against Church members. Douglas also emphasized that the Mormons had their rights to worship as they pleased. When Democrat Thomas Ford won the governorship in August, the Whigs wailed that their defeat was due to "Joe Smith's power." [^10] In December 1842, when p.364] Joseph Smith was once again sought by authorities on Missouri charges, the Prophet went to the state capital, Springfield, and sought out counsel from his friend Stephen Douglas before he was tried before the United States District Court and released.

364On 18 May 1843, a few months before he was elected to Congress, Douglas dined in Carthage with Joseph Smith and several of the elders of the Church on their return trip from the Mormon settlement Ramus, where the Prophet had given his now well-known set of instructions found in Doctrine and Covenants D&C 131. After dinner, Douglas asked Joseph to relate the history of the Church's persecution in Missouri. Douglas listened with great attention and agreed with the Saints that they had been wronged by Governor Boggs and other authorities in Missouri and that these authorities should be brought to justice. Joseph Smith, grateful to speak to a prominent politician who would listen, prophesied that unless the United States redressed the wrongs committed against the Saints in Missouri and brought the offending parties to justice, in a few years the government would be utterly "overthrown and wasted." Concluding, the Prophet uttered another prophecy, the aftermath of which has led to this essay. "Judge, you will aspire to the presidency of the United Sates," prophesied Joseph Smith. "And if you ever turn your hand against me or the Latter-day Saints, you will feel the weight of the hand of the Almighty upon you; and you will live to see and know that I have testified the truth to you; for the conversation of this day will stick to you through life." Douglas "appeared very friendly, and acknowledged the truth and propriety of President Smith's remarks." [^11]

Stephen A. Douglas was busy campaigning for Congress the rest of 1843 and, after his victory, Joseph Smith and the Mormons had no immediate occasion to interact with their political friend. In the spring of 1844, Orson Hyde of the Twelve Apostles was sent as the Church's emissary to Washington to learn of potential places for the Saints to relocate beyond the borders of the United States. Hyde found few individuals in Washington who were helpful, but in Representative Douglas, whose philosophy enthusiastically breathed of expansion for the republic, he found a still-faithful friend. Douglas gave Hyde a map of Oregon and a copy of John C. Fremont's report of his second expedition to the west. "Judge Douglas, who is full of familiarity and good-will, proposes to visit Mr. Smith on his return to Illinois upon the subject of Oregon, Texas p.365] and California," Hyde wrote back to Nauvoo. [^12] That opportunity did not arise owing to the assassination of Joseph Smith by his political enemies in Illinois in June. Douglas and his fellow Democrats demanded that the Prophet's murderers be brought to justice, while the Whigs slipped into an increasingly anti-Mormon stance. [^13]

365The summer and fall of 1845 witnessed the continued deterioration of relationships between the Mormons and their Illinois neighbors. Characteristically, Stephen A. Douglas played an important role in the final events for the Church in that state. Governor Thomas Ford, convinced that neither Mormons nor Gentiles could be trusted with power to keep the peace, commissioned four leading politicians in Illinois, including Congressman Douglas, to raise an armed volunteer force to halt the disorder and to negotiate the removal of the Mormons from Illinois. The Twelve Apostles naturally looked upon this force with distrust and prepared to fight back if necessary as the militia approached Nauvoo. Douglas volunteered to act as the intermediary with the Mormon leadership. While his contacts with Brigham Young and other members of the Twelve had not been as frequent nor as cordial as they had been with Joseph Smith, Douglas still felt his friendship with the Mormon leaders would pay dividends. Through intense negotiations, Douglas convinced Brigham Young and others of the Twelve to agree to terms of peace and to leave the state the next spring. Douglas also helped convince the citizenry in Illinois to back off from their outrageous demands and behavior toward the Mormons. [^14] Given the volatile nature of the Illinois situation in October 1845 and the willingness of the Illinois state militia to use their firepower, Stephen A. Douglas's achievement in overcoming potential civil war is certainly noteworthy.

365As preparations were made for the Mormon exodus to the west, Congressman Douglas continued to receive appeals from Church leaders for assistance and support. Brigham Young urged Douglas to secure contracts for the Church members to construct blockhouses along the Oregon Trail in order to protect the emigrants and for the establishment of a monthly mail service to the west. While Douglas did not succeed in obtaining favorable appropriations for the Church, he continued to serve as a contact in Congress for the Mormons for many years afterward. The Saints recognized him as their best friend in Washington as they sought out their new home in the Rocky Mountains. [^15]

III

By 1849 the Mormons had become sufficiently settled in the Salt Lake Valley to begin making plans for a permanent government structure. On 6 January, the provisional state of Deseret was established formally and in the early part of the year plans went forward to petition Congress for territorial status. Delegate John M. Bernhisel left for the East on 4 May 1849 to apply for territorial government and lobby for the appointment of its officers from among the leaders of the Church. Bernhisel was armed with a letter of introduction from Brigham Young to Senator Stephen A. Douglas of Illinois. "With a pleasing remembrance of our former acquaintance, and the philanthropic feelings you have ever manifested towards us as a people," began President Young's epistle to Douglas. [^16]

366In the summer, Church leaders realized that they should petition for statehood instead. In December Bernhisel approached Douglas with the desires of the Saints and was graciously received. If 1849-50 had been an uneventful time in Washington, the Mormons' requests for statehood in the West would likely have been accepted by Congress and the president, since the feelings of expansion and "manifest destiny" were still rife in the country. But by the fall of 1849, sectional hostility between the southern and northern states had moved to a new and dangerous extreme. "The utter failure of Congress to reach a settlement on the slavery question during the previous winter left both Northerners and Southerners bitter and frustrated." [^17] The feeling that the thirty-first Congress, scheduled to convene in December 1849, might well decide the question of whether the Union should continue to exist spread rapidly throughout the country. Stephen A. Douglas was one of the few in Washington "equally distant from both extremes" [^18] on the slavery question and he quickly assumed the role of a mediator. Moreover, his chairmanship of the Committee on Territories placed him at this critical time in a post of considerable power and influence in the Senate. [^19] So even though the Mormons, including even the politically astute Bernhisel himself, hoped they would receive a favorable response to their requests for statehood, they would soon learn that their case would be swallowed up in the greater and, for the nation, infinitely more serious sectional squabbles.

367Actually the petition of Deseret for statehood was subservient in Washington to the statehood drives for California and New Mexico. Senator Douglas was determined to assume leadership in the struggle to provide governmental organization in the West, and he initiated his soon-to-become-famous doctrine of "popular sovereignty" to solve both questions-expansion in the West and whether slavery should be extended to the territories owned by the United States. He skillfully engineered a plan through the Senate to admit California, whose rapidly expanding population because of the gold rush required statehood, as a free state and to admit New Mexico and Deseret as territories. These two would choose for themselves whether or not they would institute slavery. Douglas wrote the territory bills for New Mexico and Utah, arbitrarily choosing the latter name to avert opposition to the bills in the Senate, particularly from Mormonism's old foe, Senator Thomas Benton of Missouri. Douglas also convinced Mormon delegate Bernhisel that there was no hope for statehood for the Saints, although in retrospect it is obvious that Douglas was more concerned about pushing through his unique plan for solving the nation's woes than for meeting the needs of the Mormons. [^20] After months of wrangling and compromising, Congress barely succeeded in September 1850 in passing the several laws, including the Organic Act establishing Utah Territory, which made up the Compromise of 1850. Senator Douglas was either the architect or the main force behind the passage of these laws and thereafter assumed the leadership of a major wing of the national Democratic Party. Many Mormons attributed the failure of Deseret to become a state to anti-Mormon sentiment in Washington when in fact it was the nation's sectional controversy that primarily had to do with the outcome.

Douglas continued to be a friend of the Saints for the next five years. When four of the first federally appointed officials to Utah Territory in 1851 became the infamous "run-a-way officials," Senator Douglas was among the prominent men in Washington to ameliorate feelings toward the Saints, thus allowing Brigham Young to remain as Utah's territorial governor. And in 1854, in the midst of the agitation over the organization of Nebraska and Kansas territories, of which Douglas was the main congressional figure, Brigham Young wrote to "friend Douglas" asking him not to give heed to the complaints of Jim Bridger against the Mormons. [^21] As p.368] ( late as early in the election year of 1856, esteem for Douglas was still so high in Utah that Mormon First Presidency member Jedediah M. Grant proposed, somewhat naÃ¯vely, that the best presidential ticket both to solve the nation's problems and to be fair to the causes of the Mormons would be Millard Fillmore (a Whig) for president and Stephen A. Douglas (a Democrat) for vice-president. [^22]

IV

Douglas's rising political career took an interesting turn after 1854 in the aftermath of the Kansas-Nebraska Act that he engineered through Congress to promote his "popular sovereignty" doctrine. Douglas was assailed from two sides-from southern Democrats, who looked upon the act as a further attempt to rob them of their rights to continue slavery, and from the emerging Free-Soilers, who wanted to outright forbid the continuation of slavery in any of the territories of the United States. It is amazing that Douglas personally survived the storm that Kansas awakened in the nation and is a credit to his adroitness and perseverance as a politician. Douglas was forced into a corner; he chose to fight for his very political base in Illinois, the seat of some of the most persuasive Free-Soilers.

368About this same time, Mormons in Utah were gaining a bad name for openly advocating their plural marriage doctrine and for their theocratic governmental practices. Although neither the Saints nor Stephen A. Douglas probably realized it at the time, they had both chosen directions that would lead to a permanent fissure in their previous, mutually beneficial relationship.

Beginning in 1854 Douglas's opponents in Illinois, remembering that he had been identified as a friend of the Mormons, charged that his Kansas-Nebraska bill and popular sovereignty were to be used by Douglas to bring Utah into the Union as a polygamous state. To these opponents, polygamy was a fate even worse than slavery. The national Republican Party was born in 1854, and in 1856 the party fielded its first presidential candidate. p.369] The Republicans, hoping to make political hay out of Mormon polygamy, which was universally unpopular, adopted a platform labeling slavery and polygamy the "twin relics of barbarism." Up to 1856 Douglas Democrats in Illinois argued for toleration of the Mormons. But gradually Douglas himself came to realize that the charges that he was defending polygamy were becoming dangerous politically. [^23]

369Saddened that he was bettered by his long-time Democratic nemesis, James Buchanan, in his quest for the presidency in 1856, Douglas and his wing of the Democratic Party were even more jolted in March 1857 by the Dred Scott Supreme Court decision that attacked the heart of Douglas's popular sovereignty ideas. The Kansas issue also heated up and by the end of the legislative session in May 1857, when Douglas returned to his constituents in Illinois, he was surly because of the criticisms he seemed to be receiving from all sides. In June the "Little Giant" was in Springfield, where he was asked by the Grand Jury of the United States District Court, then in session, to express his views on the three most important topics then agitating the minds of the American people-Kansas, the Dred Scott decision, and conditions in Utah Territory. The senator, taken by surprise, spoke extemporaneously before a large audience in the statehouse, later writing out his comments for publication. He entitled this speech, one of the most noteworthy of his career, "Kansas, Utah, and the Dred Scott Decision." [^24]

Douglas's comments on Kansas were brief and optimistic. Over half of his speech was dedicated to his views on the Dred Scott decision. But his remarks on conditions in Utah were also extensive. Just weeks before this speech President Buchanan, bowing to public sentiment and relying upon reports from biased sources, ordered a military expedition to the Salt Lake Basin to replace Brigham Young with a non-Mormon, territorial governor. "Douglas was swept up in the tide of denunciation," evaluates his most recent biographer. [^25] Douglas gave credence to wild reports that nine-tenths of Utah's inhabitants were aliens who refused to become naturalized citizens. As chairman of the Senate Committee on Territories and being more familiar with Mormons and their ways than most politicians in Washington, Douglas must have known that this was untrue. [^26] He also charged that Mormons were "bound by horrid oaths and terrible penalties" to recognize Brigham Young's authority as paramount to that of the United States and that the Mormon p.370] government was stimulating the Indians to acts of hostility as well as prosecuting "a system of robbery and murder upon American citizens." If investigation should prove these charges to be true, Douglas asserted, then "the inhabitants of Utah, as a community, are outlaws and alien enemies." The senator called on Congress "to apply the knife and cut out this loathsome, disgusting ulcer." In a move to diffuse the arguments against his popular sovereignty principles, Douglas proposed repealing Utah's Organic Act and blotting out the territorial government so that the offenders could be apprehended and punished. He conditioned his proposals, probably not well-thought-out in the first place: "If official reports and authentic information shall change or modify these facts, I shall be ready to conform my action to the real facts as they shall be found to exist. I have no such pride of opinion as will induce me to persevere in an error one moment after my judgment is convinced." [^27]

370Immediately after Douglas sat down, the leading Republican spokesman in Illinois, Abraham Lincoln, arose and promised to answer Douglas's remarks in two weeks. Lincoln spent most of his rebuttal on the Dred Scott matter, but in turning to the Utah question, he accepted, like Douglas, most of the reports that purported to describe the "open rebellion" in that territory. Republicans could easily support Douglas's solution for Utah, Lincoln pointed out. [^28] Lincoln and other Republicans who spoke out against Douglas's Springfield speech argued primarily that Douglas's proposal for Utah proved that "popular sovereignty" was ineffective, rather than that his attitude toward the Mormons was in error. [^29]

Douglas's proposed solution toward the Mormons, however, was not universally accepted. On 4 July 1857, Harper's Weekly responded to Douglas's Springfield speech indicating that there was no evidence that the Mormons had become "alien enemies" to the Republic. "Till the late riots, no single occurrence in the history of Salt Lake settlement can be said to have shaken the bond which united the Territory to the Union. Where, then, is the evidence of alienage? Where the ground for disfranchising these people?" Harper's recommended that the U.S. populace hear the Mormons' story before utterly condemning them. "As to rebellion against the United States, we suspect the bulk of the Mormon community have far too much to do to make an honest living to have time for such fooleryÂ�. The ultimate difficulties of Mormonism may be surmounted p.371] by a temperate exercise of discretion and firmness, and by the effect of time, without violating any such vital principle as the inherent sovereignty of the people of each and every separate community within the United States." [^30]

371Once the Saints in Utah heard of their former friend's condemnation, their reaction was one mixed with sadness and self- justification. In a meeting in the Tabernacle on 27 August 1857, at a frenzied time when the approach of federal troops was even more of an issue than Douglas's speech, Brigham Young, Heber C. Kimball, Orson Hyde, and John Taylor spoke defiantly against the attempts of the United States to dominate the affairs of the citizens in Utah. Elder Taylor sarcastically referred to Stephen A. Douglas as a "friend," who was now not a particle better than the rest in Washington. President Kimball exclaimed, "Many of you have sustained Judge Douglas as being a true friend to this people, and he is just as big a damned rascal as ever walked, and always has been; he has taken a course to get into the chair of State, and that is what he is after; he will try to accomplish that if he goes to hell the next day, but he will not go into the chair of State, he will go to hell." In this time of severe tension in the Salt Lake Basin, which would ultimately lead to the abandonment of the northern settlements before the "Utah War" was settled, these Church leaders, who had all personally known Douglas, expressed a sense of deep betrayal of one who had been a friend. [^31]

On 2 September, a lengthy editorial in the Deseret News responded to the Springfield speech. The editorial, probably written by editor Albert Carrington, separately refuted each of Douglas's assertions. "Your frenzy for office has at last placed you with no backing save false `rumors and reports,' barefacedly and undeniably in the ranks of foul libelers and maligners of a people loyal and patriotic far beyond the short ken of your dark and feeble comprehension," assailed the editorial. Regarding Douglas's accusation of "Danites" or "Destroying Angels" in Utah, the editorial responded, "If any such person or persons are here, they must have been exceedingly slack in the performance of the duties assigned them by the Senator, for of all our enemies who have passed and re-passed, tarried, traded, lied, drank, plotted our destruction, &c, &c, they have all escaped to tell the horrible tale about the `Mormons.'Â�No doubt there are `Destroying Angels,' but they do not reside in Utah at present. They are on another planet, waiting for p.372] permission from the Father to go and reap down the earth. When they appear, such men as you are will feel that power." [^32]32 The editorial also reminded Douglas of Joseph Smith's 1843 prophecy concerning his aspiration for the presidency and that he should never turn his hand against the Saints. This prophecy had been published for the first time only the year previously in the Deseret News as part of the serialized "History of Joseph Smith."

V

Stephen A. Douglas may have had second thoughts about his onslaught upon the Mormons in his Springfield speech, since he did not bring up the subject on the floor of the Senate or in any of his campaign speeches thereafter. On the other hand, he did not publicly apologize for his incorrect accusations against the Mormons, even when he could have done so in 1858 and not have hurt himself politically when the tide of popular opinion had swung against "Buchanan's Blunder" toward Utah.

Douglas continued to lead the northern Democrats up to and including the election year of 1860. Following his nomination to the presidency in Charleston, South Carolina, sectionalism led the Democratic Party to divide hopelessly into different factions. By summer's end there were three Democratic candidates. Republicans were elated by the prospects of victory and, in a compromise move, nominated Abraham Lincoln for the presidency. Throughout the campaign, Senator Douglas came to realize that he had no chance for victory himself, that Lincoln would win, and that drastic steps needed to be taken to preserve the Union from secession of the southern states. [^33] Following congressional elections in some states in early October, Douglas remarked, "Mr. Lincoln is the next President. We must try to save the Union. I will go South." [^34] During the remaining month of the campaign, Douglas drove himself to complete exhaustion pleading with the southern people over the heads of their political leaders to support the Constitution and not to withdraw from the Union after the election. Never before in American history had a presidential candidate campaigned before the people in this manner. In the assessment of Allan Nevins: "Douglas, indomitable, indefatigable, never so formidable as when meeting hopeless odds, turned to a far greater object than popular sovereignty-to the cause of national unity. This, even more than p.373] the Lecompton struggle, was his finest hour." [^35] On the other hand, the Republicans, including Abraham Lincoln, did not at this time realize the severity of the threat of secession. Lincoln made no appearances in the South at all in 1860, when he might have tried to calm southern fears.

373With regard to the Mormons during the campaign of 1860, general sentiment was still negative toward the Saints throughout the nation in both political parties, although the mood was less hostile than in 1857. The institution of polygamy particularly came under universal attack and the Republicans promised in their platform that they would legislate against it. But with slavery and secession issues dominating the political landscape, Mormons received little attention in most states during the campaign. Possible exceptions were Illinois and Missouri, where anti-Mormon feelings were still acute. [^36] A Mormon missionary in New England in 1860, James Dwyer, according to his own testimony fifty-three years later, reported a conversation he had with Stephen A. Douglas following a campaign appearance. Allegedly Douglas spoke warmly of his relationships with Joseph Smith, but was "defiant" toward Brigham Young and the Mormons in Utah. [^37]

373After the election, Senator Douglas disregarded his health while continuing to strive for compromise with the South, then for peace, and finally for support of the North in the Civil War, all in the attempt to preserve the Union, which was the greatest love in his life. He returned to Illinois in April in broken health. On April 25 he spoke to a large gathering in Springfield. In the same tone as President Lincoln, Douglas urged his audience to put aside their party loyalties in preference to uniting in the common cause of bringing the nation back together. "I see no path of ambition open in a bloody struggle for triumph over my own countrymen. There is no path of ambition open for me in a divided country, after having so long served a united and glorious country. Hence, whatever we may do must be the result of conviction of patriotic duty-the duty that we owe to ourselves, to our posterity, and to the friends of constitutional liberty and self-government throughout the world," he intoned. [^38] Suffering from the effects of acute rheumatism and excessive smoking and drinking, Douglas went to his home in Chicago and never regained his health. He died on 3 June.

VI

I have tried to correct several misconceptions concerning Stephen A. Douglas. We have learned that he and Joseph Smith developed a mutual respect and close bond during their experiences together in the early 1840s. Both the Church and Douglas were willing to use each other's influence to fulfil their unique goals. We have learned that Senator Douglas was a faithful and trusted friend of the Mormons for a decade after the death of Joseph Smith. We discovered that when Douglas spoke so disparagingly against the Mormons, he did so in a period of great personal, political stress and at a time when popular opinion was virtually universal in its condemnation, or at least distrust, of the Mormon situation in faraway Utah Territory. We also noted that the Republican Party, including one of their leaders and a political opponent to Douglas, Abraham Lincoln, was no friendlier toward the Mormons and, if anything, took more political advantage of attacks against the Saints than did Douglas. When Douglas did attack the Mormons, he did not discuss the Prophet Joseph Smith, as popularly believed, although, admittedly, he did slander President Brigham Young. Another misinterpretation was that Douglas turned his hand against the Saints while he was a presidential candidate. His speech against the Saints was actually made on 12 June 1857, which was hardly during a presidential campaign season. In reality, Stephen A. Douglas already had unsuccessfully sought the presidency in 1852 and 1856, and on both occasions had not spoken against the Church nor its leaders. When he was an official candidate in 1860, he did not speak against the Church. According to Mormon tradition, Douglas had every reason to believe that he would win the presidency, but would lose according to Joseph Smith's prophecy. Actually, because of sectional strife within the Democratic Party itself, Douglas had no realistic chance for victory, and he recognized this himself midway through the campaign. The last misconception is that Douglas remained unrepentant to the end and was brought to his death by a just and vengeful God. In reality it was Douglas's own poor health practices that brought about his demise. [^39]

Stephen A. Douglas remains a controversial character in American history. His contributions were many; so were his deficiencies. Historian Stephen B. Oates has observed, "Douglas was p.375] a man of the political arena, an improviser and tough little pragmatist who never had the time nor the inclination for deep reflectionÂ�. He was a man of contradiction and paradox." [^40] Allan Nevins has described Douglas: "Alert in retort, crafty in the manipulation of an argument, redoubtable and unscrupulous in attack, he seemed momentarily to sweep all opposition before him; yet he never quite convinced wary men, for his ideas lacked deep sincerity, and his manners had always a touch of the barroom."
