Various authors, Regional Studies in LDS History: Illinois ( 1995)

"Two from Judah Ministering to Joseph"

D. Kelly Ogden

A study of two lives: Major Howard Egan and Dr. Alexander Neibaur-both of whom received patriarchal blessings naming them of the tribe of Judah. A description of their service to the Prophet Joseph Smith and to the people of Joseph in fulfillment of said blessings during the Nauvoo period and beyond.

Major Howard Egan

On 24 Sept 1843, Howard Egan received his patriarchal blessing from Hyrum Smith, the Patriarch of the Church. The blessing revealed that Egan was of the lineage of David, and of the tribe of Judah, and that he would "magnify an holy calling, and come up to the anointing and the endowment, and be numbered with the called and chosen." [^1] The patriarchal blessing would also be "consonant" with, or similar to, the one given to Judah. Judah's blessing, in part, reads "thy hand shall be in the neck of thy enemies" and "unto him shall the gathering of the people be." [^2] Howard Egan served the Church and its leaders in many different callings: he was a defender of the Saints against their enemies; and he assisted in bringing hundreds of people safely across the plains to the Salt Lake Valley. Egan's service to the Church, especially to the Prophet Joseph Smith, was a reflection of the blessings promised in his patriarchal blessing.

225Howard Egan was born in Tullemore, Kings County, Ireland, on 15 June 1815. [^3] His parents were Howard and Ann Meade Egan. [^4] When he was only eight years old, his mother died, leaving ten children for her husband to care for alone. [^5] Howard Sr. moved his family to Montreal, Canada, but in 1828-just five years later-he, too, died, and the children were orphaned. [^6] Shortly after his father's death, young Howard became a sailor; he was just thirteen years old. [^7]

226Very little is known about Egan's life during this period. When he was about 21 years old, [^8] he left the sea and went to Salem, Massachusetts, where he went to work for a Mr. Chisholm, a rope maker. [^9] There he met and fell in love with Tamson Parshley, daughter of Richard and Mary Caverly Parshley. [^10] They were married on 1 December 1838. [^11] Egan was twenty-three; Tamson was barely thirteen [^12] or fourteen. [^13] They settled down and began to raise a family.

226In 1841, Elder Erastus Snow, who was then serving a mission for the Church in Philadelphia, was asked by Hyrum Smith and William Law to go to Salem and begin preaching the gospel there. The Prophet Joseph Smith had received a revelation five years earlier (1836) while he was in Salem, foreshadowing important results of future missionary work:

226I [the Lord] have much treasure in this city for you, for the benefit of Zion, and many people in this city, whom I will gather out in due time for the benefit of Zion, through your instrumentality. Therefore, it is expedient that you should form acquaintance with men in this city, as you shall be led, and as it shall be given youÂ�. For there are more treasures than one for you in this city. [^14]

226At first Elder Snow had little success, but gradually more people came to listen to his message, and baptisms increased. [^15] ( Two of those who listened to Elder Snow and were baptized were Howard and Tamson Egan. In light of his later work in the Church, it is clear that Howard Egan's conversion was part of the direct fulfillment of the above prophecy. During the year of their baptism (1842), the Egan family immigrated to Nauvoo to join the Saints there. [^16]

226In 1842 Nauvoo was a thriving frontier town, distinguished from other frontier towns by its cleanliness, its peacefulness, and its lack of ruffians and drunkards. [^17] It had a city charter which not only allowed the organization of the city, but contained other charters permitting the construction of a university and an independent military body, later called the auvoo Legion. [^18] This was no temporary establishment; the Saints and their families planned to stay.

It was during this time in Nauvoo that Egan received his patriarchal blessing. For a recent convert, the promises revealed in this blessing must have been almost overwhelming. The revelation that he was blessed through Judah was equally surprising. Later p.227] descendants of Egan used this information to trace the Egan line back through the Milesian kings (of Ireland) all the way to Adam. [^19]

227Drawing on his experience with Mr. Chisholm, Egan opened a rope factory in Nauvoo. [^20] The factory no longer exists, but historians have deduced from records and the accounts of people who lived in Nauvoo during this period that the rope factory was built on Water Street, across the street from Joseph Smith's store. [^21] In addition to manufacturing rope, Egan became a city policeman in December 1843 [^22] as well as a member of the Nauvoo Legion, [^23] achieving the rank of major [^24] this explains why most writers refer to him as Major Howard Egan. Egan faithfully fulfilled his assignments and was highly commended by his superiors. [^25] Egan also became well known to the Prophet Joseph and was assigned to guard the Prophet's home on several occasions. [^26] Joseph Smith was impressed by Egan's abilities and said once that he "never feared when Howard Egan was on guard." [^27] When not on duty, Egan worked on the construction of the Nauvoo Temple. He also paid a tithe required of all worthy members for using the temple. [^28] Egan was apparently a worthy member, for he was endowed on 16 December 1845, and was sealed to his wife Tamson on 23 January 1846. [^29] This was in partial fulfillment of Egan's patriarchal blessing which promised him that, if he remained worthy, he would "come up to the anointings and endowments." [^30] In addition, Egan was one of the members commanded to enter plural marriage. His character and worthiness must have been appropriate to qualify for this difficult and sensitive responsibility. Hyrum Smith sealed Catherine Clawson to Howard Egan in 1844, [^31] but since there is no mention of Catherine in Egan's journals, and no record that she bore him children, it is possible that the sealing was for eternity only and unconsummated. Egan eventually married two other wives who did bear him children-Nancy Redding in 1846 and Mary Ann Tuttle in 1849. [^32]

As well as serving the Church in a secular capacity, Egan served several missions to the Eastern states. One of these was a travelling general conference, from May to September 1844, with forty-seven conferences to be held in many states. [^33] On this occasion, Egan was sent to Peterboro, New Hampshire, and was part of the mission presidency for that state. The missionaries were to preach the gospel and to present the Prophet's ideas for government-in other words, to campaign for Joseph Smith's election as p.228] president of the United States. [^34] Unfortunately, the political proselytizing soon became unnecessary. The Prophet Joseph Smith was martyred while the conference took place; it was the one time Egan had not been able to guard his friend and Prophet.

228After the Prophet's martyrdom, hatred for the Church increased. The anti-Mormon leaders planned "wolf-hunts" in 1844, where the wolves to be hunted were Mormons. [^35] The danger to the Nauvoo Saints increased when the state of Illinois repealed their city charter in 1845 [^36] and they lost the government's protection. The Nauvoo Legion charter was repealed at the same time, but since the Saints had no other way to protect themselves, the Legion's existence continued as a Church-organized body. [^37]

228The people of Nauvoo were fortunate to have the Legion's protection, because few others were willing to help. Governor Ford of Illinois tried to raise 2,500 volunteers, but could only find 500; Sheriff Jacob Backenstos of Hancock County also tried to raise support, but failed. [^38] Again Egan was called on to defend the Saints and, as the blessing of Judah says, his hand was "in the neck of his enemies." [^39] As a captain in the Legion, in September of 1845, he helped control the mobs that were burning houses and destroying property. [^40] He also led a detachment of 20 men to help the fighters at Camp Creek, and was later stationed at Carthage to maintain the peace. [^41] Egan's responsibilities were a reflection of the trust the Church leaders and Egan's military superiors had in him. However, all his efforts and the efforts of other Nauvoo defenders could not stop the prejudice and hatred of the people surrounding them. Violence and mob activity in and around Nauvoo eventually forced the Saints to move. [^42] Their exodus from Nauvoo began on 4 February 1846, at which time there were between 12,000 and 20,000 people living there. [^43]

The people of Nauvoo had worked hard to build up their city. When they left, many of them-including Howard Egan-abandoned newly-built homes and businesses they had run for years. The people who left Nauvoo had been demoralized by the mob attacks and were disorganized. Not until the new camp at Winter Quarters was established were the people organized, and this time in Israelite fashion, with captains of hundreds, fifties, and tens. [^44] Egan assisted in establishing this order, and was elected a Captain of Fifty in the camp. He acted as a mediator between the camp p.229] leaders and the men and families under his care, and frequently advised the leaders on the problems of the camp. [^45]

229Egan's appreciated service to the Prophet Joseph Smith now continued in his service to Brigham Young. In 1846 he was asked to catch up with the Mormon Battalion at Santa Fe [^46] and carry mail and money from the men of the Battalion back to their families at Winter Quarters; this was his first job as a mail carrier, [^47] a career for which he would later become well known. Egan was also assigned to buy corn for the Church to distribute. [^48] He fulfilled his duties in a manner that caused Church leaders to appreciate his diligence and willingness to serve. When President Young and the Twelve Apostles made plans to send a group of men west to find a refuge for the Saints, Howard Egan was named a member of that company.

229The Pioneer Company, as it was later called, set out for the Great Basin on 8 April 1847. Howard Egan was again called on to serve; he was made captain of the Ninth Ten and was responsible for the nine other men in his group, including Heber C. Kimball and William Clayton. [^49] On the trip across the plains, Egan's friendship with the members of the company grew. He became close friends with Brigham Young, William Clayton, and Heber C. Kimball. [^50] Egan was one of the men adopted by Heber Kimball under the Law of Adoption, and Egan frequently called Kimball "Father." [^51]

229Egan's personal diary, detailing the long trek across the plains, is full of humorous anecdotes and insights. He interacted with Indians, hunted buffalo for the company, met (and distrusted) the famous pioneer Jim Bridger, and when Brigham Young became ill just before entering the Salt Lake Valley, Egan was one of the men who stayed behind with the prophet's wagon. [^52] As a result, he entered the valley with Brigham Young's group.

229In Egan's description of his first sight of the valley, he seemed to see its potential as the refuge the Saints needed. The description reads, in part:

229My heart felt truly glad, and I rejoiced at having the privilege of beholding this extensive and beautiful valley, that may yet become a home for the SaintsÂ�. It cannot be concluded to be otherwise than rich and very fertileÂ�. This valley is bounded by high mountains, some of them covered with snow, and from what knowledge we have of it at present, this is the most safe and secure place the Saints could possibly locate themselves in. [^53]

230After deciding on a site for their new city, the pioneers began building a fort. Egan helped to haul logs from the canyon to the building site. [^54] He and other men worked together to construct homes and other buildings in the protected area. The construction effort was well underway when, in late August, Egan accompanied Brigham Young and many of the company back to Winter Quarters. They arrived on 31 October 1847, and spent the winter there.

230In the "gathering of the people" to Salt Lake Valley, Howard Egan played an important part (as foreshadowed in his promised blessings of Judah). Egan helped to bring two other companies of Saints west in the next two years. His family accompanied him on the second journey, which started in May of 1848. [^55] ( This company consisted of over 600 wagons, the greatest number yet to make the trip. Egan had many responsibilities. On this trip, he was also asked by Orson Hyde, George A. Smith, and Ezra T. Benson (leaders of the eastern branch of the Church) to supervise the transportation of a printing press to the Salt Lake Valley. [^56] While watching over his family, assisting the company leaders, and taking care of the press, Egan kept very busy. The company arrived safely in the valley, and in April of 1849 the Egan family moved into a house in the growing community. [^57] No sooner did he see his family settled than Egan was off again, to return to Winter Quarters to help a third group cross the plains. His plural wife Nancy and their daughter Helen were in this group. [^58]

230Though this was the last company of pioneers Egan would help across the plains, his travels were definitely not finished. In the winter of 1849-50 he went to California, sent by the Church leaders to purchase livestock and supplies for the Saints. [^59] It was on this trip that he began noting possible camp sites for a trail from Salt Lake City to California. [^60] This route was later established and used by the Overland Mail and later by the Pony Express. [^61] Egan himself carried the mail over this route and established a reputation for speed and diligence by once traveling the long distance-over 650 miles-on muleback in ten days. [^62] Though this route was generally known to emigrants as the Simpson Route, Utahns called it Egan's Trail. [^63]

Egan's ability to get the mail through resulted in his being appointed to positions of responsibility with both the Overland Mail and the Pony Express. As a Pony Express officer, he had jurisdiction over the entire Utah route; it was Howard Egan who p.231] brought the first Pony Express mailbags into Salt Lake City. [^64] He also served as superintendent of the Overland Mail until 1869. [^65] At that time, the railroad's completion and the growing use of the telegraph made horse-carried mail obsolete. [^66] Egan retired from public duty and returned to the Salt Lake Valley.

231However, Egan's "retirement" did not apply to his church service. He became a missionary to the Indians and an intermediary between them and the white men moving into their territory. [^67] He served on the Salt Lake police force and as a deputy sheriff, as well as standing guard at President Brigham Young's home. [^68] He continued to serve Brigham Young, even after the prophet's death by standing guard over his grave. It was in performing this duty that Egan was stricken with pneumonia. He died on 16 March 1878, [^69] about six months after the prophet's death-as loyal in death as he was in life. The funeral sermon was preached by his friend Orson Pratt.

231Howard Egan's life was full of service. He performed many duties for the Church and its prophets without complaint, even when it meant losing time with his family. In serving others, he fulfilled many of the promises given him in his patriarchal blessing: he was "numbered with the called and chosen," he was endowed and blessed by the Spirit, he was a staunch defender of the Saints, and he assisted in the gathering of the people. In all ways, he honored the lineage of Judah through which he was blessed-and proved his worth as a descendant of Israel.

Dr. Alexander Neibaur

Alexander Neibaur was born in the fortress Ehrenbreitstein on 8 January 1808. [^70] The fortress is situated along the Rhine River and has at various times been a part of France and of Prussia, and is now part of the city of Koblentz, Germany. His parents, Nathan and Rebecca Peretz Neibaur, were prosperous Jews. Nathan Neibaur was a doctor and spoke several languages. He served Napoleon as a linguist and interpreter, but his proud and honest nature would not allow him to be a spy, despite the self-titled Emperor's inducements. [^71] Alexander inherited his father's integrity; throughout his life he acted in accordance with his beliefs, despite the opposition and threats he sometimes encountered.

232Alexander's family intended that he become a rabbi, but by the early age of seventeen he decided to study dentistry instead, and graduated by age twenty from the University of Berlin. [^72] After his graduation, young Dr. Neibaur traveled Europe, and was converted to Christianity. [^73] It is possible that his conversion estranged Neibaur from his Jewish family, because in 1830 he went to England to set up his practice and settled in Preston, Lancashire. [^74]

232In England, Neibaur met Ellen Breakel, daughter of Richard and Alice Bannister Breakel. Ellen and Alexander were married on 15 September 1834 and settled down in Preston to raise a family. [^75] They lived near the "Cock Pit," which was a famous gathering place because of its size-800 people could meet there. [^76] This seemingly irrelevant detail was to change the Neibaurs' lives dramatically.

232In 1837 the first Mormon missionaries came to England to preach the restored gospel. They arrived in Preston on 30 July 1837, having chosen that city because of the Cock Pit's potential capacity. [^77] They found many willing listeners, and Alexander Neibaur was among them. The story of his conversion shows Neibaur's spiritual sensitivity. Early one morning after the missionaries had arrived, Ellen Neibaur, in common with the other women of Preston, was whitewashing her front steps. A neighbor asked if she had seen the new ministers from America who claimed to have seen an angel. Alexander overheard this conversation from inside the house. He jumped up, threw open the window, and asked where he could find these ministers.

Upon learning their address, he dressed hurriedly and ran to find the missionaries; he met Heber C. Kimball, Willard Richards, p.233] Orson Hyde, and Joseph Fielding, and his first question to them was, "You have a book?" [^78]

233Neibaur had seen the Book of Mormon in his "night visions," as he called his unusual dreams. He secured a copy and, being able to neither eat nor sleep, read it in three days and returned to ask for baptism. The elders advised him to wait until the following spring, which he did impatiently. When told by the missionaries that he should wait for baptism until he was prepared, he replied, "Gentlemen, I am prepared." [^79] This statement characterized his whole life; Neibaur was, without hesitation, prepared to serve the Lord and his chosen prophets. He was baptized on 9 April 1838, by Isaac Russell. [^80]

233Though Alexander knew from the start the truth of the gospel message, Ellen Neibaur was not so quick to believe. She didn't oppose her husband's baptism, but would not accept the gospel herself, calling the Book of Mormon a "pretty story." [^81] Later, however, she had a dream in which she saw Elder Richards' face formed by clouds in the sky. She knew that it signified that she should listen to his message, and shortly thereafter she was baptized.

233Alexander Neibaur was not only one of the first people in England to hear the gospel preached, he was apparently the first male Jew to accept the gospel and be baptized. [^82] Many of his friends and associates relentlessly opposed his conversion to Mormonism; they thought he was destroying his life and tried to keep him from doing so. But Neibaur held fast to his convictions. He later testified, in reference to his conversion, "My trust was in the God of Abraham." [^83]

233Neibaur's trust in the Lord continued when he left a comfortable home and a good dental practice in Preston and immigrated to America with his family. Travel across the sea was exceptionally dangerous in those days; the voyage could take anywhere from 21 to 60 days, depending on the winds, and sometimes the frail sailing vessels never made it at all. [^84] Despite the hazards, Neibaur left for America on 7 February 1841 with Ellen-expecting a baby in May-and their three children: Joseph William, age 6; Margaret, age 5; and Isaac, age 2.

The company, which sailed from Liverpool on the ship Sheffield, was the first company of Saints to leave England for Nauvoo. The group was led by Hiram Clark and totalled 235 Saints; among p.234] them were the missionaries Brigham Young, John Taylor, and Willard Richards. [^85] The voyage was uncomfortable and crowded; the passengers were packed into the steerage, a large room lined with bunk beds, and had to cook their food over an open fire. They had no running water and nowhere to dispose of waste. [^86] However, despite their unsanitary and hazardous conditions, the emigrants for the most part stayed cheerful.

234Neibaur recorded many of the incidents of the voyage, using a detached observer's style that disguised their seriousness. He described murders, fornications, and other atrocities without disgust or horror. His reaction to shocking or distressing events sometimes makes him appear indifferent, but these reactions often masked his deep feelings and sorrows, as can be seen from later entries about the martyrdom of Joseph Smith.

234As Neibaur recorded in his journal, the trip from England to Nauvoo took 71 days and 4 boats. [^87] The emigrants were very kindly received in Nauvoo on 18 April 1841. Neibaur was ordained to the priesthood by Willard Richards and John Taylor on 18 January 1843 and ordained a seventy in 1844. [^88] Neibaur set up his dentistry practice-in Brigham Young's living room-and began the work that would continue until the Saints left Nauvoo in September of 1846. His business in Nauvoo served over 150 regular patients, including Brigham Young and his family, and the families of other Church leaders. [^89]

234Neibaur's dentistry business wasn't enough to support his growing family, so he used other means to support them without going into debt. Neibaur did hard physical labor when working on the Temple and the Nauvoo House, but his constitution was not suited to the work and he was frequently ill. [^90] In addition to his dentistry and construction work and service in the Nauvoo Legion, Neibaur was a poet; he wrote the words to the hymn, "Come, Thou Glorious Day of Promise" and several others, most of them containing Jewish imagery drawn from his unique heritage. [^91]

Just two days after his arrival in Nauvoo, Alexander Neibaur met the Prophet Joseph Smith. [^92] This meeting was the start of a close friendship. Neibaur loved the Prophet and would never let anyone speak ill of him. In fact, the quick-tempered Neibaur once threw a stone jug at a man who was cursing Joseph Smith's name, and another time was shot at by a man whom he had rebuked for blaspheming the Prophet-miraculously, none of the bullets fired. [^93] p.235] Neibaur was always ready to serve his friend: he replaced teeth for the Prophet after mobsters had tarred and feathered him, [^94] and he instructed Joseph in the German and Hebrew languages, in which Neibaur was fluent. The Prophet Joseph Smith trusted and loved his friend, personally giving Neibaur a blessing when he lay terribly ill. [^95] He also recognized his friend's devotion to the gospel; he once told Neibaur, "You are indeed one of us." [^96] One of the few journal accounts we have of the First Vision, besides the Prophet's own accounts, was recorded by Alexander Neibaur (see Appendices).

235Because of their great friendship, Neibaur grieved intensely over the Prophet's death-yet in his journal he hides this emotion, and the entry for 27 June 1844 reads only, "Joseph and Hyrum murdered in Carthage jail." [^97] Neibaur, with the rest of the Saints, felt the Prophet's loss even more deeply for fear of the persecutions to come. There was even hatred within the Church as many prominent men of the community left the Church in anger, and the remaining leaders argued over who the next prophet should be. [^98] When the mob attacks came, the Neibaurs wanted to leave Nauvoo, but Ellen's advanced pregnancy prevented that. Neibaur was ordered out with the Nauvoo militia; he waited only until Ellen bore her baby, then left to defend their community in what would later be called the Battle of Nauvoo. [^99]

235The mob attacks continued until it seemed they would never end. Neibaur felt weary, but his faith and his determination were unshaken. The shortest entry in his journal, for 3 July 1846, reflects the tension and weariness he and the others felt while under attack; it reads only, "Mob again." [^100]

235Like all the Saints in Nauvoo, the Neibaurs were constantly threatened during those two years of mob violence. Yet somehow they always felt protected from harm. They trusted in God to protect the family-He was the only one who could, because Ellen was sick with ague and Alexander was often gone with the militia. The children were hungry and terrified. [^101]

235In September 1846, the mobsters and the Saints made a temporary "peace" to negotiate; the outcome was that the Saints had to give up their arms and leave Nauvoo within five days time. [^102] The short preparation time they were given meant that the Neibaurs left Nauvoo with almost nothing, in horrible weather, with Ellen still sick and the family's wagon incompleted. [^103]

236On 17 September 1846, the Neibaurs crossed the Mississippi into Montrose, Iowa, and from there moved to Bonaparte, Iowa, where they stayed that winter. [^104]

236In 1847 they moved to Winter Quarters, and the next year traveled across the plains to the Salt Lake Valley. Neibaur had been struggling to outfit his family for their journey, and they still had no wagon or oxen as the departure date drew nearer. Neibaur was so determined to leave that he borrowed wagons and a yoke of oxen from the widow Lydia Knight, and another yoke of oxen from Joseph Young. [^105] The company left for the Rocky Mountains on 20 May and arrived on 24 September 1848; Neibaur called their new home "a fine looking country." [^106]

236Just days after arriving in the valley, Neibaur signed up for a plot of land and, soon after, he and his son began making adobe bricks for their new home. [^107] The construction process took months, and the family lived in a tent through the winter until the following May when their house was completed. [^108] It was small and later Neibaur moved his family into a better home on Second South and Second East in Salt Lake City. [^109]

236As in Nauvoo, Neibaur worked at many different trades to support his family. While building his house, he began his dental practice. He was Utah's first dentist. [^110] He was rarely paid in money; more often his patients gave him goods such as molasses and cornmeal as payment. Many of his clients were widows and others too poor to pay-he charged them nothing. [^111]

236In addition to dentistry, Neibaur learned to make good matches in a country where such luxuries were rare. [^112] He started a German class, held in Andrew Cahoon's house, in November of 1848. [^113] The German class began a tradition that continued every winter. Neibaur, like his father, was a linguist and knew seven languages: English, French, German, Latin, Greek, Hebrew, and Spanish. [^114]

Neibaur's industry and inner strength were manifestations of his character. He was industrious, frugal, debt-free, just, and honest; he had a strict, austere personality and a tremendous pride in his birth and intelligence, a pride that was mellowed by his acceptance of the gospel. [^115] Like his father, who refused to spy for Napoleon, Neibaur was relentless and full of integrity. When he was converted to Christianity, he followed Christ without hesitation-the same applied to his conversion to the Restored Gospel. Neibaur's spirituality and closeness to the Lord were evident throughout his life. p.237] He had the spirit of discernment to tell what people were really like, and was outspoken against liars. [^116] He bore testimony of the truth of the Bible, the Book of Mormon, and that Joseph Smith was a prophet. When writing an account of his life, he wrote, "My prayer is that my posterity might walk in the way of righteousness." [^117]

237Alexander Neibaur died in Salt Lake City on 15 December 1883. [^118] He was paralyzed for three weeks prior to his death, but his mind was still alert. Before he died, he saw a vision, and his last words were the names of Joseph and Hyrum. [^119] At the time of his death, these words were said of him: "He lived and died a faithful disciple of the Lord Jesus Christ, enduring all he was ever called to pass through, with an heroic, determined, uncomplaining spirit." [^120] Neibaur's own testimony in reference to the hardships he had endured was a reflection of his commitment to the gospel:

237I have seen my Savior. I have seen the prints in his hands! I know that Jesus is the Son of God, and I know that this work is true and that Joseph Smith was a prophet of God. I would suffer it all and more, far more than I have ever suffered for that knowledge even to the laying down of my body on the plains for the wolves to devour. [^121]

237Alexander Neibaur lived and died a servant of God and His appointed prophets. His steadfastness in the faith was a testimony both to those who knew him then and to those who learn of him now.
