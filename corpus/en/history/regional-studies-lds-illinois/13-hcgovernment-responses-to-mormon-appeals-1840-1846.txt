Various authors, Regional Studies in LDS History: Illinois ( 1995)

Appeals for RedressHCGovernment Responses to Mormon Appeals, 1840-1846

Clark V. Johnson

Appeals for RedressHCOrganized in April 1830 in New York, The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints soon moved its headquarters west to Ohio in February 1831. In June the Prophet Joseph Smith held a conference in Kirtland. During the annual conference, he received a revelation informing him that the next conference should be held in western Missouri ( D&C 52:2-3 ). By midsummer 1831 the Saints began arriving in Missouri. [^1] Within two years about 1,200 Mormons had settled five communities in Jackson County. [^2]

Appeals for RedressHC183This influx of new citizens created anxiety among the established citizens. They feared eventual Mormon political control as well as social and religious differences. In the fall of 1833, the old citizens organized themselves, and through extra-legal means they drove the Mormons from their county. After fleeing Jackson County, the Saints settled in ten counties, with the majority collecting in Clay County.

Appeals for RedressHC183For a time there was a careful co-existent peace between the Mormons and old settlers as the Saints settled Far West in Caldwell County. By 1838 about 10,000 Mormons had settled in Caldwell, Daviess, and Carroll Counties. Additional LDS migration increased the uneasiness of the Missourians, and in a series of clashes at Gallatin, DeWitt, Crooked River, Haun's Mill, and Far West, the Missouri State militia drove the Mormons from the state. During these incidents, Church leaders were arrested and, in November 1838, they appeared before a court of inquiry presided over by Judge Austin A. King at Richmond, Missouri. That court found evidence to bind some Church leaders over to be tried before the next circuit court and ordered some of the prisoners incarcerated in Liberty Jail and others in Richmond jail. The Richmond prisoners were later transferred to a jail in Columbia, Boone County, where they remained from about 1 December 1838 through midsummer 1839. [^3]

Appeals for RedressHC184In March 1839, while yet in Liberty Jail, the Prophet Joseph Smith received a revelation instructing the Saints to gather "up a knowledge of all the facts, and sufferings and abuses put upon them by the people of this State [Missouri]" ( D&C 123:1 ). Once the Saints had gathered the "statements and affidavits," the revelation instructed them to form a committee to "publish to all the world" and to "present to the heads of government" the affidavits that described the suffering, property losses, and abuses they had experienced in Missouri ( D&C 123:6 ).

Appeals for RedressHC184Acting upon these instructions, Parley P. Pratt wrote the most extensive petition; it consisted of 64 printed pages. Pratt's petition was entered according to an act of Congress in the year 1839 in the clerk's office of the district court of the United States for the district of Michigan, and its contents sworn to by Pratt before John Johnson, a justice of the peace at Nauvoo, Illinois, on 15 January 1842.

Appeals for RedressHC184This affidavit begins with a review of the Saints' suffering in Jackson County and ends describing how the Missourians drove them from the state in 1838-39. Pratt's petition contains his personal observations and experiences with the mobs and the militia in Missouri. He graphically describes his own arrest, trial, incarceration, and the atrocities perpetrated upon the Saints by state leaders. Pratt held the governor, the state, and local officials responsible for the actions of the citizens of Missouri.

Appeals for RedressHC184Also during 1839, John P. Greene published Facts Relative to the Expulsion of the Mormons or Latter Day Saints from the State of Missouri Under the "Exterminating Order" in Cincinnati, Ohio. This pamphlet contains a series of public statements and actual individual petitions that Greene presented to the Democratic Committee of Cincinnati. The committee voted to support Greene, and as far as we have been able to learn, they used their influence to publish Greene's document. It is certain that the Church and John P. Greene did not have the monetary means to publish the pamphlet at the time. Green's 60-page pamphlet is a collection of the sworn affidavits that he had gathered; it also includes some declarations of support made by a few members of the Democratic Society of Cincinnati. It is not known how many copies of Greene's pamphlet were published.

Appeals for RedressHC It is likely that Pratt's and Greene's publications were in circulation prior to Joseph Smith's 1839-40 visit to Washington, p.185] DC. The extent of these pamphlets' circulation is undetermined. However, in an interview with President James K. Polk, Jesse C. Little quoted from Pratt's petition. It appears that John P. Greene and Parley P. Pratt were the first who acted on the revelation received by Joseph Smith in March 1839 ( D&C 123 ). Greene and Pratt did their best to place the Mormon cause before the public. However, there is nothing to suggest that either of the documents were published before Joseph's escape from Liberty prison in 1839.

Appeals for RedressHC185 Several other affidavits of this type were written between 1839 and 1843. These petitions are holographic and were never published. Included are a 28-page document signed by Joseph Smith, Sidney Rigdon, and Elias Higbee dated 27 January 1840; a 24-page document signed by Elias Higbee, John Taylor, and Elias Smith dated 10 January 1842; and a 50-foot scroll signed by 3,419 people, dated 28 November 1843. These last three documents are general descriptions of the suffering of the Mormons in Missouri and were used to introduce 773 individual petitions or claims written by 678 people to Congress. [^4] While the individual petitions describe the Mormon experience in Missouri from 1834 to 1838, they especially detail the events leading to Governor Lilburn Boggs' 1838 expulsion order.

Appeals for RedressHCThe Saints' 1839-40 Appeals

Appeals for RedressHCThe major appeal to the U.S. Government occurred during the winter of 1839-40, when the Prophet led the delegation to Washington. That group consisted of Elias Higbee, Sidney Rigdon, Porter Rockwell, and Robert Foster. They left Nauvoo 29 October 1839. [^5] On 30 October they stopped in Quincy, where Sidney Rigdon became ill. In Quincy they met Robert D. Foster, who agreed to travel with them to help care for Sidney Rigdon. [^6] The party was delayed at Quincy because of Rigdon's illness and also because they contacted influential people in Quincy to obtain additional papers to aid them in presenting their cause before the U.S. government. [^7] When they reached Columbus, Ohio, Rigdon was still critically ill. At this time Joseph realized that the party had to travel faster to reach Washington, DC, in time for their appointment with Congress and President Martin Van Buren, [^8] and they split up. Joseph Smith and Higbee immediately left for Washington, while Rigdon, Foster, and Rockwell remained until Rigdon's health p.186] improved. On 17 November Smith and Higbee boarded a stagecoach bound for Washington, DC. During the journey, the stagecoach driver lost control of the horses. The Prophet calmed the other passengers and prevented a mother from throwing her infant out of the stage window in an attempt to save its life. Then he climbed up on the stage and brought the runaway team to a halt. Just before the team stopped, Elias had jumped off the stage to assist Joseph, and was "slightly injured." [^9] Joseph Smith was acknowledged a hero until it was learned that he was the Mormon Prophet, and there were no references made to his heroism before Congress. [^10]

Appeals for RedressHC186Smith and Higbee arrived in Washington on 28 November 1839. [^11] [^12]

Appeals for RedressHC186Historians generally write about a single visit with President Martin Van Buren, but the context of the 29 November visit implies that there might have been a second one. History notes another reference by the Prophet to President Van Buren. On 6 February 1840, the Prophet Joseph Smith indicated that "it was with great reluctance [that Van Buren] listened to our message, which when he had heard, said, `Gentleman, your cause is just,'" and then the famous quote, "`but I can do nothing for you. If I take up for you I shall lose the vote in Missouri.'" [^13] While this might have been an allusion by the Prophet to the original visit, it may refer to a firmer, more direct position taken by the president. [^14] After all, Van Buren had more than two months to survey Missouri's political temperament as presented by its congressmen. Joseph's statement that "he promised to reconsider the matter" suggests that perhaps there was a second visit. The fact that President Van Buren chose to do nothing for the Mormons because he wanted to preserve his political career revealed his final position on the matter.

Appeals for RedressHCDocuments found in the National Archives and at the Church Historical Department in Salt Lake City indicate that before the Brethren left Nauvoo and while at Quincy, they had prepared letters of introduction to the Illinois congressional delegation [^15] because p.187] they hoped to present their cause to Congress through the Illinois congressmen.

Appeals for RedressHC187After meeting with President Van Buren in the morning, the Prophet and Higbee turned their attention to the Illinois delegates that afternoon. Senator Richard M. Young led the Illinois contingent. In this first meeting the committee discussed "states' rights" versus federal intervention. The committee decided that Senator Young should consult various members of Congress to ascertain congressional backing. Apparently the Illinois delegation contacted a Judge White [^16] of Tennessee in the Senate and a Mr. Wright [^17] in the Senate; Joseph did not remember the other names. [^18] But the indication was that they were going to consult several more of the most powerful senators. The men who were consulted declined to give their opinion until they knew which way the political wind blew. [^19]

Appeals for RedressHC187In a letter to the Nauvoo High Council, Joseph Smith reported their efforts and included a brief evaluation of the congressmen from Illinois. He described them as "worthy men" who have "treated us with great kindness" and who were ready to "do all that is in their power" to help the Saints. [^20]

Appeals for RedressHC187At this point the Prophet, Higbee, and Young decided to draw up a memorial to be read before the U.S. Senate. Judge Young and the representatives from Illinois presented the memorial to the House and the Senate on 7 December 1839. [^21] At this same time Joseph wrote to Church leaders in Nauvoo to solicit their help in obtaining individual petitions from Church members. [^22]

Appeals for RedressHC187Porter Rockwell, Robert Foster, and Sidney Rigdon arrived in Washington on 23 December 1839. From 23 December 1839 to 6 February 1840, the Illinois delegation tried to present the Mormon cause before Congress, but the actual reading of the document was tabled by both the Senate and the House. Congress did order the petition printed; however, a printed copy of this twenty-eight-page memorial has never been found.

Appeals for RedressHC187In a report to Nauvoo, Joseph wrote concerning the prejudices, superstitions, and bigotry of an ignorant generation. [^23] Even at that early time, Joseph recognized that efforts were going to be limited because of prejudice on the part of those who were going to help them.

Appeals for RedressHCApparently the brethren went to Washington, DC, intending to publish a book containing the documents. Judge Young agreed p.188] to finance the publication with his own funds until the money could be raised to reimburse him. [^24] This project did not materialize; all we have are the original documents in manuscript form.

Appeals for RedressHC188Even though the Illinois congressmen worked in behalf of the Mormon delegation, progress was slow and the Brethren were often plagued by inconveniences. Joseph wrote, "We have met with but one accident since we started. The lock on our trunk was broken off and Brother Lyman Wight's petition is missing; but we trust there is a copy of it preserved." What else was taken from the trunk is not known. [^25]

Appeals for RedressHC188In a second letter, the Prophet and Higbee reported that they had made a presentation before a congressional delegation composed of Illinois and Missouri congressmen. During this meeting, Senator John M. Robinson of Illinois openly opposed their efforts to present the petition to Congress. Joseph Smith spoke against Senator Robinson, "asserting our constitutional rights" and "setting forth the injuries we have received." [^26] Senator Robinson accused the Mormons of not making an appeal to the state of Missouri. [^27] Following this confrontation, Smith and Higbee wrote to Nauvoo asking for more information about "any particular transaction of the outrages in Missouri." Smith asked that affidavits be "sworn to by the sufferers or those who were eyewitnesses to these facts." [^28] In particular, the Prophet asked for land certificates, "claims to pre-emption rights, and affidavits to prove that soldiers were quartered on us and in our houses without our consent." [^29] Acting on the Prophet's request, the Nauvoo high council appointed "Alanson Ripley, in Iowa; Seymour Brunson and Charles C. Rich, at Quincy"; and "Zenas H. Gurley, at Macomb," to gather the information. [^30] The high council also directed Hyrum Smith, Edward Partridge, and Vinson Knight to instruct the committee. [^31] At least 491 people responded with descriptions of suffering, brutality, and property losses; these descriptions were then sent to the delegation in Washington. [^32] The documents were sworn to before 41 county officers in ten counties in Illinois and two counties in Iowa. [^33]

Appeals for RedressHCThe opportunity to testify before the Senate occurred 28 January 1840. Senator Richard M. Young presented the memorial of Joseph Smith, Jr., Sidney Rigdon, and Elias Higbee and referred to the document as the Mormon grievance for redress against Missouri. [^34] Senator Young moved that it be printed and referred to the committee on the judiciary. [^35] After some remarks from Senator p.189] Lewis F. Linn of Missouri, Mr. Young called for the reading of the memorial, and it was then read before the Senate. [^36] The Congressional Globe reported that after "further remarks from Senators Thomas Hart Benton of Missouri, Preston [^37] and Henry Clay of Kentucky, the motion to lay the petitions on the table was agreed to with the understanding that it would be called up at an early date." [^38]

Appeals for RedressHC189On 6 February 1840, the Mormon delegation had its final interview with President Martin Van Buren. [^39] In a most intriguing political move, the Missouri legislature drafted a letter to President Van Buren supporting his renomination and thanking him for his efforts in helping them with the "recent problems" they had suffered in Missouri. Following this final interview with President Van Buren, the Prophet Joseph Smith concluded that it "was little use for me to tarry, to press the just claims of the Saints on the consideration of the President or Congress." [^40] Smith, along with Porter Rockwell and Dr. Foster, left Washington, DC, and returned to Nauvoo, Illinois, on 4 March 1840.

Appeals for RedressHCElias Higbee remained in the nation's capital for several more months to represent the Church's interests before Congress. Together with the Illinois delegation, Higbee kept pushing the Mormon cause before the Senate, the House of Representatives, and various congressional committees. On 12 February 1840, Senator Young presented additional documents "in the case of the Mormons,Â�which were referred to the committee on the judiciary." [^41] In a letter dated 20 February 1840, Elias Higbee described a presentation he made before the Judiciary Committee in behalf of the Mormons. [^42] Although he was disappointed that only three committee members were in attendance, he indicated that he had carried the day. [^43] Noting that Senator Linn and Representative John Jameson of Missouri were in attendance, [^44] ( Higbee covered the following points: (1) He represented a suffering people deprived of their rights as free citizens of the United States; (2) Referring to the extermination order issued by Governor Lilburn W. Boggs in October 1838, Higbee pointed out that even though Mormons were free citizens, "something like fifteen thousand" of them could not reenter Missouri without fear of being killed; (3) The Mormons suffered because of their religious beliefs. Senator Linn asked if the "legislature of Missouri did not refuse to investigate the subject" because of impending trials. Higbee replied that all he knew was p.190] that the legislature had refused to investigate the matter. [^45] Linn felt that it was an "injustice for Congress to take up the matter before the legislature had acted on it." [^46]

Appeals for RedressHC190The Judiciary Committee met on 21 and 22 February to discuss the Mormon cause. [^47] From Higbee's reports, it appears that the committee meetings were styled after a hearing. During the second meeting, Senator Lewis F. Linn and Representative John Jameson spoke, and Higbee responded. [^48] During the third meeting, held 22 February, the congressman from Missouri brought a Mr. Corwin of St. Louis, who apparently was a Democrat and an editor, to testify. [^49] During this meeting the Missourians accused "Joe Smith," as they called the Prophet, of controlling the Mormon people by revelation in temporal, civil, and political affairs. [^50] Elias Higbee denied the accusations, claiming that revelation guided Church members spiritually and that every man "exercised the right of suffrage according to his own judgement." [^51] When the Missourians insisted that the Prophet influenced his people politically, Higbee pointed out that Joseph held no political office in Missouri. [^52] Linn said that John Corrill, a Mormon and a Missouri legislator, had told him that the Mormons had burnt a number of houses in Daviess County. Higbee produced sworn statements that the Missourians had burned homes in Jackson County, in Clay County, in DeWitt County, and in Carroll County. [^53] He also referred to John Corrill's affidavit, which stated that no help would come from the Missouri legislature. [^54] Elias Higbee noted further that Jameson and Linn desired to paint the Saints as "treasonable characters by the constitution," and that the Mormons did everything politically and civilly by revelation. [^55] Higbee asked that Alanson Ripley, King Follett, Amasa Lyman, and Francis M. Higbee be sent to testify before the committee about their experiences at DeWitt. He also wanted Charles C. Rich and Seymour Brunson to testify about their experiences in Missouri. [^56]

Appeals for RedressHCOn 26 February 1840 Higbee wrote that the committee's decision was against the Saints and that the committee believed that "redress can only be had in [the] Missouri" courts and legislature. [^57] Higbee noted that he could do no more; once he had the committee's official report he intended to return to Nauvoo. [^58] Getting the report took longer than he had expected. When he wrote to Joseph from Washington on 9 March 1840, he was still waiting for the final resolution of the committee. [^59] Finally, on 23 March 1840, the p.191] Senate called for a report of the Judiciary Committee concerning the Mormon cause in Missouri. [^60] The committee members concluded that while they sympathized with the Mormons they also felt that "the case presented for their investigation is not such a one as will justify or authorize any interposition by this government." [^61]

Appeals for RedressHC191The committee pointed out that the wrongs had not been "committed by any officers of the United States," and thus Congress did not have any authority in the matter. [^62] The citizens were instructed to "seek relief in the courts of judicature of the state of Missouri, or of the United States," both of which had jurisdiction over the matter. [^63] However, the Saints had already sought redress through the courts and legislature of Missouri to no avail. They considered it absurd to seek redress from federal courts: it was not practical to appeal their cause through the judiciary arm of the government. The process had to begin in the lower courts in the state of Missouri, and Church leaders recognized the impossibility of taking such action.

Appeals for RedressHC191Even though Congress refused to investigate Mormon charges against Missourians on grounds of violation of state sovereignty, precedent had been set for such federal government action in 1786 and 1787, when a federal force marched into Massachusetts to put down Shay's Rebellion. [^64] Daniel Shay, who had led a group of disgruntled ex-Revolutionary War veterans against the arsenal at Springville, Massachusetts, was opposed by a federal army under the command of General Benjamin Lincoln, who successfully defeated the rebel forces. The Shayists had been intimidating local government officials and judges who were selling and confiscating the veterans' property because of their inability to pay back taxes. The federal force was marshaled under the pretext of an Indian rebellion. [^65]

Appeals for RedressHC191The day after the official resolution had passed the Senate, Higbee wrote, "Our business is at last ended here." [^66] He noted that the committee had been discharged, that he had withdrawn the documents they had presented, and that he intended to return to Nauvoo. [^67]

Appeals for RedressHCThe Saints' Second Appeal in 1840

Appeals for RedressHCIn October 1840, after the unsuccessful first appeal, Church members sustained the Prophet Joseph Smith's proposal that "Elias p.192] Higbee and Robert B. Thompson be appointed a committee to obtain redress for the wrongs sustained in Missouri." [^68] Higbee and Thompson drafted a memorial that was very similar to the first memorial prepared in 1839 by the Prophet, Sidney Rigdon, and Higbee. [^69] On 21 December 1840, this petition, possibly accompanied by some individual claims, was "presented to the House of Representatives," referred to the Judiciary Committee, and ordered to be printed. [^70] [^71] Apparently the petition was printed and circulated in Congress, because on 15 February 1841 Senator Linn of Missouri asked that the legal proceedings held in Missouri be printed so that his state's character might not be impugned. [^72] The Missouri document was ordered to be printed. [^73] The present location of the Higbee-Thompson memorial is not known. Neither the holographic nor the printed version has been found; nor have the individual petitions that apparently accompanied this memorial surfaced. [^74]

Appeals for RedressHCThe Saints' Appeal in 1842

Appeals for RedressHCIn 1842, Elias Higbee, John Taylor, and Elias Smith wrote another memorial. This memorial, nearly identical to the first memorial written in 1840, is in the National Archives. Like the 1839 document, this memorial was used to introduce individual petitions to the U.S. Congress. It is not known how or when the individual petitions were sent to Washington. Some may have been sent with the Higbee-Thompson memorial in late 1840, and some may have been sent with the Higbee, Taylor, Smith memorial in 1842.

Appeals for RedressHCIllinois Representatives John T. Stuart and John D. Reynolds and Illinois Senator Richard M. Young helped to present the Mormon cause in Congress. [^75] These attempts failed, and the petitions of this second appeal, along with the Higbee, Taylor, Smith memorial, were referred to the Judiciary Committee. The Judiciary Committee then sent them to the National Archives, where they p.193] remained until they were discovered by historian Paul Richards in 1968. [^76]

Appeals for RedressHC193Perhaps the petitions were not left in Washington through oversight or neglect but to keep the Mormon issue before Congress. It appears that these petitions formed the basis for later efforts by Orson Pratt, Orson Hyde, Jesse C. Little, and others to place the Mormon cause before the U.S. Congress and before the president of the United States.

Appeals for RedressHCThe Saints' Fourth Appeal

Appeals for RedressHCIn October 1843 the Prophet Joseph Smith received a personal letter from Joseph L. Heywood, a Church member residing in Quincy, Illinois. The letter introduced a Colonel John Frierson to the Prophet. Frierson felt that the Saints had been wronged in Missouri. [^77] Colonel Frierson, a political representative for John C. Calhoun, encouraged Smith to use the "Hon. B. Rhett of South CarolinaÂ�to present a petition" in behalf of the Mormons. [^78] Heywood enclosed with his letter a copy of a letter written by Frierson "to a distinguished citizen of South Carolina" and a "circular put out confidentially" by the friends of Mr. Calhoun, of South Carolina. John C. Calhoun was then actively campaigning to be the next president of the United States. [^79] Perhaps Brett's and Frierson's inquiries sparked Church leaders' interest in ascertaining the political positions of "the five candidates for the Presidency of the United States" toward the Mormons and their suffering in Missouri. [^80] During the next few days the Prophet Joseph Smith wrote letters to John C. Calhoun, Lewis Cass, Richard M. Johnson, Henry Clay, and President Martin Van Buren. [^81] Near the end of November 1843, John Frierson met with Joseph Smith at the Mansion House in Nauvoo. [^82] Joseph, Hyrum Smith, the Quorum of the Twelve and others met with Colonel Frierson on 26 November 1843. During the meeting the Brethren read to Frierson "the affidavits of Hyrum Smith, Brigham Young, Parley P. Pratt, Lyman Wright, George W. Patten, and Sidney Rigdon, taken before the municipal court" at Nauvoo, 1 July 1843. [^83]

Appeals for RedressHCIn 1844, Church leaders submitted at least three documents to Congress: a memorial asking that Nauvoo be made a territory of the federal government, an appeal to settle land in the Oregon p.194] Territory, and a memorial applying to Congress for redress for sufferings in Missouri. [^84]

Appeals for RedressHC194Orson Pratt arrived in Washington, DC, on 1 March 1844. Pratt presented some memorials on Missouri sufferings to Representative Joseph P. Hoge of Illinois. [^85] Following Pratt's request, Mr. Hoge called the Illinois delegation together. After listening to Pratt read a memorial, members of the delegation felt that the documents should be referred to the Judiciary Committee.

Appeals for RedressHC194Elder Pratt called on congressmen from "other states"; some of these men were members of the Judiciary Committee. [^86] According to Pratt, the prevailing opinion was that Congress had no power to act because Missouri was a sovereign state. Pratt also talked with government representatives about the Oregon proposal, which he noted was "a popular question." He added, "The fever of emigration begins to rage; if the Mormons become early majority others will not come; if the Mormons do not become an early majority, the others will not allow us to come." [^87]

Appeals for RedressHC194While he failed to get the desired response from government representatives, Pratt gave the memorial and other documents to Senator James Semple of Illinois. [^88]

Appeals for RedressHC194On 5 April 1844 Senator James Semple presented on the floor of the Senate two documents in behalf of the Mormons. The first, a petition from the mayor and alderman of Nauvoo, asked that Nauvoo be granted "the powers and privileges of a territorial government." The second memorial was a petition signed by four hundred and nineteen citizens of Hancock County, Illinois, "describing the persecutions the Mormons had suffered at the hands of the Missourians in the 1830s." Both memorials were referred to the Judiciary Committee. [^89] The Congressional Globe is in error on the number of people who signed the 1843 memorial: 3,419 signatures appeared on this petition, not 419. [^90]

Appeals for RedressHCIn mid-April 1844, Orson Hyde reported to the Prophet that, through the efforts of Senator James Semple of Illinois, he and Orson Pratt had met with President John Tyler. Hyde found President Tyler very sympathetic to the Saints, but unable to help them. They discussed the Oregon question and the need to maintain a balance of power between the free states and the slave states. Senator Stephen A. Douglas of Illinois gave Hyde a map of the Oregon country and a copy of Fremont's exploration of the West. Encouraged by the words of the president and by Stephen A. p.195] Douglas's generosity, Orson Hyde and Orson Pratt drafted a bill and presented it to the Senate Judiciary Committee. According to Hyde they asked for two million dollars to relieve the suffering Saints. [^91]

Appeals for RedressHC195The scroll appeal fared no better than the others. On 9 June 1844, Orson Hyde wrote to Joseph Smith that Congress had rejected their plea to present the petition. [^92] [ Still, Hyde had not given up, and he appealed once again directly to President John Tyler. In a letter dated 11 June 1844, he reported that for the second time President Tyler spent a "good deal of time with [me]" and "was very frank." He explained that the president had remarked, "The general government cannot interfere with the laws and regulations of the states." Hyde also noted that "President Tyler seemed to feel that I should be satisfied with a few words because of the press of business." In conclusion, Hyde observed, "We are now thrown back upon our own resources. We have tried every department of government to obtain our rights, but we cannot find them." [^93]

Appeals for RedressHCHelp Arrives

Appeals for RedressHCIt is apparent that in 1845 the Saints intended to make yet another appeal. [^94] The affidavits they prepared are similar to the individual petitions prepared earlier. However, it seems that the 1845 petitions were never used, probably because preparation for the exodus from Nauvoo to the Great Basin took precedence.

Appeals for RedressHC195On 4 February 1846 the Nauvoo Saints began to migrate west. By midsummer 1846 the Latter-day Saints were an army of pioneers strung out from Nauvoo, Illinois, across Iowa, to Winter Quarters, Nebraska. Even though they were moving west, lobbyists still continued to petition the government for "rights" lost in Missouri during the previous decade.

Appeals for RedressHC195On 26 January 1846, after the death of Joseph Smith, President Brigham Young wrote a letter to Jesse C. Little "appointing him agent of the Church in the eastern states." [^95] Little obtained letters of introduction from Governor John H. Steele of New Hampshire to George M. Dallas (vice president of the United States), George Bancroft (secretary of the navy), and Amos Kendall (ex-postmaster general of the United States). Armed with these letters, Little journeyed to Washington, DC, where he arrived 21 May 1846.

Appeals for RedressHC196Once in Washington he called upon Amos Kendall, who, after reading the letters of introduction and hearing Little's request for help from the federal government, suggested that President James K. Polk was interested in occupying California. Thus, "arrangements could be made to assist our emigration by enlisting one thousand" Mormon men in the U.S. Army. [^96] According to Kendall, the U.S. government would arm and equip these men and send them to California to defend the country. [^97] It appears that Kendall's motives were not entirely pure. In January 1846, as the New York Saints were preparing to sail from New York to San Francisco under the leadership of Samuel Brannan, Amos Kendall and a Mr. A. G. Benson insisted that Mormon leaders sign a contract with them. Kendall and Benson agreed to help the Mormons settle the West if the Saints would deed to A. G. Benson and Company "one-half of theÂ�lands" the Mormons acquired. For this consideration Kendall and Benson agreed to use their influence with the federal government to see that it did not interfere with the Church's westward migration. [^98]

Appeals for RedressHC196Under what authority Kendall made these suggestions to Little is not known. Kendall met with Jesse C. Little two days later to reaffirm his previous offer and to encourage Little to organize a military expedition to California. [^99]

Appeals for RedressHCApparently Jesse C. Little did not entirely trust Kendall. On 1 June 1846, Little wrote a lengthy personal letter to President James K. Polk informing him of his own background and describing the persecution of the Mormon people in Missouri and Illinois: "We have not only been robbed of our houses, lands and property, but of a jewel of far more value,Â�our good names." [^100] Continuing, he said, "We have been slanderedÂ�by our enemies" and they have been so successful that "many good men suppose us to beÂ�outlaws and thieves." [^101] Jesse assured the president that the Mormons were loyal American citizens who desired to defend the U.S. government, but he also warned that if help were not given by the government, but "proffered" by a foreign power, which the Saints would "disdain," the U.S. government would "compel us to be foreigners." [^102] Jesse Little told the president that from 12,000 to 15,000 Saints were on their way west from Nauvoo, and that some had already "gone around Cape Horn." He explained that there were still others in the East who desired to migrate, p.197] 40,000 more in the British Isles, and more in the "Society Islands" who were determined to join Church leaders in the West. [^103]

Appeals for RedressHC197On 3 June 1846, Jesse C. Little and Amos Kendall met with President James K. Polk for three hours. During the interview, President Polk assured Little and Kendall that he had "no prejudice against the Mormons, but believed [them] to be good citizens." [^104] [ He also requested that Little meet with the secretary of the navy, and noted that he wished to meet with Little the next day. However, when Little returned to the White House the following day, President Polk could not meet with him. When President Polk met with Little the following day, 5 June, he assured him that the Mormon people would be "protected in California," and he authorized "that five hundred or one thousand of our people [the Saints] should be taken into the service, officered by our own men." [^105] He promised to write letters to authorize the organization of the Mormon Battalion. Little "weighed" this offer carefully for the remainder of the day. Finally, that same evening he wrote a letter in which he accepted President Polk's offer. [^106]

Appeals for RedressHC197The following morning Little again visited with Kendall who informed him that "he had written to the President" in his behalf. [^107] Kendall's action must have upset Little, because Little went to the White House twice that day to see the president, but he was unable to meet with him.

Appeals for RedressHC197The following day, Thomas L. Kane, who was still recovering from an illness that had kept him bedridden for several weeks, arrived in Washington. After his arrival, things moved rapidly. During the next few days, Kane and Little had interviews with James Buchanan (secretary of state), George Bancroft (secretary of the navy), Mr. Marcy (secretary of war), and George M. Appeals for RedressHC197In Little's final interview with President Polk, the president expressed "good feelings toward the Saints" and informed Little that the papers authorizing the Mormon Battalion were being prepared by Mr. Marcy, the secretary of war. By 12 June 1846, Little and Thomas L. Kane started west for Council Bluffs. [^108]

[^109] Four companies of the Battalion had enlisted by 16 July. [^110] The fifth company mustered in a few days later. The five companies p.198] left Council Bluffs, Iowa, on 21 July 1846, and marched to Fort Leavenworth, Kansas, where they arrived 1 August 1846. [^111]

Appeals for RedressHCSummary

Appeals for RedressHCFrom November 1839 through July 1846, the Saints tried to secure help from the United States Government for the property they had lost and the abuses they had suffered in Missouri. Historians have found almost 800 petitions and letters for redress detailing the atrocities suffered by the LDS people at the hands of government officials and citizens in Missouri.

Appeals for RedressHC198Members of the Illinois congressional delegation such as Richard M. Young, James Semple, Stephen A. Douglas, John J. Stewart, John Wentworth, and Joseph Hoge represented the Saints and made at least four major attempts to obtain hearings before the Congress of the United States. The Saints also sought redress from Presidents Martin Van Buren, John Tyler, and James K. Polk. The congressional efforts were thwarted each time by Senator Thomas Hart Benton of Missouri and the Missouri congressional delegation, whose power in Congress and especially in the Senate was feared by such men as John C. Calhoun and Henry Clay. President Van Buren simply noted that it was not politically expedient for him to align himself with the Mormon cause; thus he refused to help. President Tyler felt that he was constrained by the political issues (states' rights) that confronted the United States at the time. Only James K. Polk found a way to help the beleaguered Mormons by authorizing the formation of the Mormon Battalion so that the Saints could participate in the Mexican War and by ordering the Saints to assist in the occupation of California.
