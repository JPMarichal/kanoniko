Howard and Martha Jane Knowlton Coray of Nauvoo

Charles D. Tate, Jr.

After Howard Coray and Martha Jane Knowlton were married on 6 February 1841 in her father's house at Bear Creek, Illinois, they settled a few miles north in Nauvoo, the City Beautiful. And Nauvoo was beautiful for the newlyweds. It was in Nauvoo that they first developed the working relationship which was to sustain them the rest of their lives. When Howard needed help in whatever he was doing, Martha happily assisted him. When Martha needed help in her projects, Howard then became her assistant. [^1] They worked together as they taught school, raised their family (which eventually included 12 children), recorded Mother Smith's remembrances of her prophet-son, and fled from Nauvoo in May 1846. But first let us see how Howard Coray and Martha Jane Knowlton came to join the Church and be in the Nauvoo area at the same time so they could meet, fall in love, and marry.

331Howard Coray (see Fig. 1) was born in Dansville Township, Steuben County, New York, on 6 May 1817. He was a sixth-generation American, his fourth great-grandfather, John Cory, having immigrated into New York from England in the 1630s. Apparently John's son Isaac changed the spelling of the family name to Coray. Howard's ancestors settled in Suffolk (on Long Island) and Orange Counties in New York, but his grandfather, John Coray, moved to Providence Township, Pennsylvania, when he married Phebe Howe in 1788. Howard's father, Silas Coray, was the first of four children born to that marriage. After an accidental gunshot left Phebe a 23-year-old widow with four children, she married a James Abbott and bore him five children. Together they raised both families.

Howard remembered that he was about 10 years of age (1827-1828) when his father, Silas, moved the family from New York to Pennsylvania, looking for a better place to live. After moving to several different places throughout eastern Pennsylvania during the

333next 10 years, Silas decided to go out west in 1838, "believing he could find a more desirable place in which to live." [^2] [^3]

333It was shortly after becoming disillusioned with the famous preacher that Howard came in contact with a member of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. An Elder Joseph Wood was preaching in the area, and Howard went to listen to him. After hearing Elder Wood on several occasions, and being impressed with the clarity of his message, Howard asked to be baptized. [^4]

333Less than two weeks later, Howard walked with others to Nauvoo to attend the April 1840 conference and to satisfy his curiosity to see the Prophet Joseph Smith, of whom he had already received a personal testimony. Two days after receiving the Gift of the Holy Ghost, Howard knew by a spiritual witness that Mormonism was true and that Joseph "was truly a Prophet raised up in the 19th Century to usher in the `Dispensation of the Fulness of Times.'" [^5] When the group got to Nauvoo, Joseph Wood introduced Howard to the Prophet as a "collegiate from Jacksonville College"; this embarrassed Howard because he knew it was not true. [^6] After conversing with Howard for a few minutes, Joseph asked if he would come to Nauvoo and clerk for him. Howard was thrilled to have the chance to work for the Prophet and shortly was copying "huge piles of letters into books." [^7]

Being employed in Joseph Smith's office and living for a time with his family gave Howard many opportunities to observe the Prophet. He watched as people from all walks of life came to both see the man who dared to call himself a prophet and to talk with p.334] him to test his depth. In his "Autobiography," Howard wrote the following:

334While thus engaged, I had many very precious opportunities-great and small, almost every day, were calling on him, some for one thing and some for another-politicians and preachers and of different persuasions-some with the view of testing the depth of his knowledge and if possible, confounding him and putting him to shame. Well, what did I discover-that he was equal to every occasion, that he had a ready answer for all questions. I heard him say that God had given him the key of knowledge by which he could trace any subject through all its ramifications.

334I had heard it remarked that Joseph Smith was Sidney Rigdon's cat's paw: soon after he returned from the east, he came to see Joseph and the thought went through my mind, now I will see who the cat's paw is. Well, I did see; after passing the usual compliments, Rigdon said to Joseph, "When I was preaching in Philadelphia after I had finished my discourse, a man stepped up to me and desired me to explain something in John's revelation [mentioning at the same time what it was]. Well, I could not do it, how is it Joseph?" Joseph cited him at once right offhand to a passage in Ezekiel and something in some other book of the Old Testament, saying that they explained all about it. I thought to myself, that don't look much like Joseph's being a cat's paw. [^8]

334Howard was there when several other men of renown visited the Prophet:

334Stephen A. Douglas called to see him and ask him some questions. One thing he desired to know was how he managed to govern a people so diverse, coming from so many different countries with their peculiar manners and customs. "Well," he said, "I simply teach them the truth and they govern themselves," was his ready answer. [^9] Among other great men who called to see him was Cyrus Walker, a lawyer of much note; he tried to sound the Prophet, and see how deep he was. Well, it was with Walker as it had been with all the others. He soon got enough, found Joseph too deep for his lead and line, and gave up the enterprise. Thus it was in every instance that came under my observation: How could we expect it to be otherwise, for any man who has never peered into heaven and seen heavenly things, be a match for one who had had a half a score or more heavenly messengers for teachers. [^10]

334On another occasion, Howard had a deep spiritual experience as he watched the Prophet Joseph at work:

One morning, I went as usual into the office to go to work. I found Joseph sitting on one side of a table and Robert B. Thompson on the opposite side, and the understanding I got was that they were examining or hunting in the manuscript of the new translation of the Bible for something on priesthood, which Joseph wished to present or have read to the people the next conference. Well, they could not find what they wanted and Joseph said to Thompson, "Put the manuscript to one side and take some paper and I will tell you what to write." Brother Thompson took some foolscap paper that p.335] was at his elbow and made himself ready for the business. I was seated probably six or eight feet on Joseph's left side, so that I could look almost squarely into Joseph's left eye, I mean the side of his eye. Well, the spirit of God descended upon him and a measure of it upon me, insomuch that I could fully realize that God, or the Holy Ghost, was talking through him. I never, neither before or since, have felt as I did on that occasion. I felt so small and humble I could have freely kissed his hand. [^11]

335The incident most widely known in the Coray family, from the time Howard lived with the Smiths, was the time the Prophet broke Howard's leg while wrestling with him. While Howard was working as a clerk for the Prophet, Joseph said he wished Howard were a larger man so they could wrestle. Howard said they could do it regardless of his size and, as they wrestled in the street in front of the office, Joseph broke Howard's leg. Howard said it was foolhardy to wrestle with the Prophet, who weighed over 200 pounds while he scarcely weighed 130. One other thing no one seems to have mentioned, and certainly Howard never did, though Hyrum Smith alludes to it in the Patriarchal Blessing he gave Howard in 1840, is the fact that Howard had only one hand. [^12] Even though he was of small stature, having only a right hand never slowed Howard down a bit as he did manual labor almost all of his life.

335After the Prophet had carried Howard into the house and placed a splint on the broken leg, Howard noted that Jacob received a blessing from the angel that injured him in their wrestle, and asked the Prophet to bless him. Joseph said his father, Joseph Smith, Sr., was the patriarch and he would ask him to bless him. Howard wrote of this incident: "In nine days after my leg was broken, I was able to get up and hobble about the house, by the aid of a cane, and in two weeks thereafter, I was about recovered-nearly as well as ever-so much so that I went to meeting on foot, a distance of a mile." [^13] He considered that experience a miraculous healing since he had expected to be laid up for at least three months.

Howard was also asked to work at compiling a history of the Church. "The Prophet was to furnish all the materials; and our business, was not only to combine and arrange in chronological order, but to spread out or amplify not a little, in as good historical style as maybe." [^14] ( When he and his assistants (first Edwin D. Woolley and then a Dr. Miller) had "used up all the historical matter furnished us by the Prophet," and circumstances were such that Joseph could not give any more attention to the project at that time, Howard turned to teaching school and did not clerk for the Prophet p.336] again. His journal notes, "I next engaged in school teaching; which was my main avocation, for livelihood, while I resided in Nauvoo." [^15]

336We are now at the point in time when Howard met Martha Jane Knowlton (see Fig. 2). But before we discuss that meeting, let us see how Martha happened to be in Nauvoo at that time in her life.

336Martha Jane Knowlton was born in Covington, Kenton County, Kentucky, on 3 June 1822, the fourth of ten children born to Sidney Algernon and Harriet Burnham Knowlton. She was an eighth-generation American. Her sixth great-grandfather, John Knowlton, left his birthplace of Canterbury, Kent County, England, in the 1630s and immigrated to Ipswich, Essex County, Massachusetts, where the Knowlton family stayed until the 1750s, when William Knowlton, Martha's great-great-grandfather, moved to Ashford, Windham County, Connecticut. Sidney Algernon Knowlton, Martha's father, was born in Ashford on 24 May 1793. In 1816, Sidney moved to Dunbarton, Merrimack County, New Hampshire, where he married Harriet Burnham on 30 June of that year. They lived in Dunbarton until after their first child was born (Rumamah B. Knowlton, 6 Sept 1817). By the time their second child, Harriet Virginia, was born (30 March 1820), Sidney and Harriet were living in Susquehanna County, Pennsylvania. They then moved for a short time to Boone County and then to Covington, Kenton County, Kentucky, where Martha and her sister Julia Ann were born (3 June 1822 and 17 Aug 1824). By 1827 the Knowltons had moved north across the Ohio River to the Cincinnati suburb of Cumminsville, where they remained until 1835 and had three more children (Ephraim Knowlton, 22 Mar 1827; Mary Ann Knowlton, 11 Sept 1829; and George Washington Knowlton, 4 July 1832). In 1835, they moved to Bear Creek, Hancock County, Illinois, where Sidney Knowlton became well known as a "scientific farmer" and stockman. Three more children were born there (John Quincy Knowlton, 9 July 1835; Benjamin Franklin Knowlton, 30 Jan 1838; and Marcia Eliza Knowlton, 19 Dec 1841). [^16]

It was in the winter of 1838-39 that the Knowltons came in contact with the Church. They aided many destitute Latter-day Saints as they came into Illinois fleeing the persecutions in Missouri. Then they met other Latter-day Saints, including Elders George A. Smith and John E. Page, who were instrumental in bringing the Knowlton family into the Church. [^17]

338"From the Elders" section of the February 1840 Times and Seasons reads:

338We learn virbally [sic] from Elder John E. Page, that within a few weeks past, he has baptized nine in the lower part of this county, about 8 miles south west of Carthage, and twenty from this place. Among those who embraced the gospel is Mr. Sidney Knowlton and family, who have for several years been zealous members of the Campbellite society, and are personally acquainted with the leaders of that sectÂ�. They are of the opinion that if Messers. Campbell and Scott, and others, had been attentive hearers to the lectures which had been delivered in their place, they would have become Mormon also. [^18]

338Family oral tradition and printed obituaries have it that they were baptized through a hole cut in the ice of the Mississippi River in January or February 1840 and that Martha was the first to be baptized. Whatever the facts, 18-year-old Martha Jane Knowlton was almost a new convert to the Church when she first met the 23-year-old new convert Howard Coray after a Church meeting in Nauvoo in the summer of 1840. Howard later wrote that in their first conversation he found Martha "was ready, off hand, and inclined to be witty; also that her mind took a wider range than was common for young ladies of her age. This interview, though short, was indeed very enjoyable." [^19] When we add to those amicable feelings of that first meeting the fact that the Prophet Joseph played the role of Cupid, telling Howard, "Bro. Coray, you will soon find a companion, one that will be suited to your condition, and whom you will be satisfied with. She will cling to you, like the cords of death; and you will have a good many children," [^20] and telling Martha that Howard Coray was just the man for her, we have favorable circumstances in Nauvoo in 1840.

338In the coming months, Howard and Martha exchanged letters and visits and were attracted to each other. Though we have not found any of Howard's letters, we do have an undated letter from Martha to Howard that obviously belongs to the period of their courtship that gives interesting insights into her concerns about their relationship. Martha told Howard not to misinterpret her favorable reaction to being called "Mrs. Coray" by Esq. Rigdon as her being too forward about their courtship, because it was her natural disposition to tease and turn a laugh on herself in such situations and that that was all there was to it. Then she wrote,

339I may as well drop a hint with regard to your course of conduct. You have indeed expressed yourself [in] terms calculated to be understood which some would consider hasty when short acquaintance but I have no objections to your manner of expression as it is in accordance with the liberty I gave you-you say I evaded answering your question I can not answer your question consistently you know. I can't Brother Coray but I shall always stick to my text until I forget it I have not said I am in love do not take it so if did come suddenly into the neighborhood C C & C but excuse my humor and if you get tired of my folly and see others who carry themselves with more dignity more steadiness etc do not consider yourself under any obligation-but remember that every one has a right to decide according to the evidence given if you turn your attention to a more worthy object you will not be blamed provided it is done frankly and above-board This is not said through any ill will but I want you to know my feelings You know it would be better if you changed your mind that I should know it and I was disappointed at your fancying a temperament so different from your own & thought you might not be aware of my real claims upon your esteem. Adieu for the present / Martha. [^21]

339Written in parentheses on the signature line is ("Consider of it and let me know your answer").

339In other activities of this period Martha received her Patriarchal Blessing under the hands of Joseph Smith, Sr., in August 1840. Howard was ordained an elder by the Prophet Joseph Smith on 3 September 1840. In October, after he had run out of materials for the history of the Church, Howard accepted the Prophet's offer to use his office as a classroom and began to teach school there. On 20 October 1840, he received his Patriarchal Blessing under the hands of Hyrum Smith, father Smith having died in September. [^22]

339With their courtship being aided and abetted by the Prophet Joseph, who told them theirs would be a marriage made in heaven, Howard proposed and Martha Jane accepted. They were married in Martha's family home in Bear Creek on 6 February 1841 by Robert B. Thompson. The young couple moved the next day into Howard's room at the Robert B. Thompson's house at the corner of Sidney and Granger Streets, where he had taken up boarding when he left the Smith household. [^23] From the beginning of their married life, Howard and Martha worked together as a matched team. It did not matter which one needed assistance, the other would pitch in and help. Each found happiness in the other's success. Immediately after they were married, Martha assisted Howard in teaching school.

Later in the spring of 1841, Brother Thompson added a large room to his house and rented it to the Corays for their schoolroom. p.340] They taught school there until the fall of 1841, when they moved to Augusta, Lee County, Iowa, "a small town on the Skunk riverÂ�not far from Burlington, for the purpose of selling goods, or exchanging them for grain for the Nauvoo House." [^24]

340After they had been back in Nauvoo teaching school for about two quarters, Howard was called on a mission to the Lamanites. His father-in-law, Sidney A. Knowlton, was called at the same time and they were assigned to serve together. Martha and her mother stayed home to take care of their families as the men went east to the Allegheny Mountains in Pennsylvania, where they served as missionaries from November 1842 through April 1843. Howard noted that their missionary work was not very successful since they were out proselyting during the time that John C. Bennett apostatized and published his exposÂ of the Church. The rest of 1843 saw them teaching school again in Nauvoo. On 14 June 1843, Howard was ordained a high priest by Edwin D. Woolley. [^25]

340Early in July 1843, Martha had a peculiar dream which she could not fully understand, and she asked Howard to go with her to ask Hyrum Smith to interpret it for her. When they met with Hyrum on 22 July 1843, he took them in his buggy southeast of town far enough for them to converse without anyone observing or overhearing them. Hyrum explained

340the revelation on Celestial marriageÂ�. The dream was in harmony with the Revelation, and was calculated to prepare her mind for its reception: She never doubted the divinity of it, nor rebelled against it. & While still in the buggy, Bro. Hyrum asked my wife if she was willing to be sealed to me; after a moment's thought she answered, Yes. He then asked me, if I wished to be sealed. I replied in the affirmative, & After telling us that he knew by the Spirit of the Lord that it was His will for us to be sealed, he performed the ceremony then and there. [^26]

Martha gave birth to their second child, Martha Jane Knowlton Coray, on 19 February 1844 in Nauvoo and still managed to assist Howard in his teaching. We have no record of their thoughts or feelings at the time of the martyrdom of Joseph and Hyrum Smith. Howard's journal is silent, and we have not yet found any of p.341] Martha's pre-1855 journals. We can only surmise they thought that the events of 27 June 1844 were too tragic to write about since they had associated so closely with both Joseph and Hyrum and had unshakable testimonies of their callings. Howard's next journal entry notes that in the fall of 1844 he rented the Music Hall, which held almost 150 students and that he succeeded in nearly filling it with his school. He also recorded that he and Martha had a Brother John M. Woolley assist them. [^27]

341There were two types of schools in Nauvoo-subscription and tax-subsidized schools. In the first type, the parents paid the teacher directly, either with money or goods, for the children enrolled. There were several such schools advertised in the Nauvoo Neighbor, the weekly newspaper. The 5 July 1843 issue carried an ad "Nauvoo Select School" offered by "J. A. Banister-Just in from New York." His school met at Broadway and Pratt streets and offered instruction in "Reading, Writing, Spelling, Orthography, Geography Arithmetic, Grammer [sic], Composition, Oratory, and Philosophy." The cost was $2.00 for a twelve-week quarter for reading through arithmetic and $2.50 for grammar through philosophy.

341The school that advertised most frequently in the Neighbor was the Nauvoo Seminary. The following ad ran almost regularly, week-in and week-out:

NAUVOO SEMINARY

341Mr. Joseph N. and Miss Adelia Cole, would respectfully inform the citizens of Nauvoo, that they have opened a school in the large and convenient room, in the second story of President Joseph Smith's store, on the corner of Water and Granger streets, on Tuesday the 11 inst., (July) for the instruction of male and female. Having been long engaged as teachers of seminaries in the east on the most approved systems, they feel confident that they will be enabled to give complete satisfaction to those who may favor them with their patronage.

341Their most ernest and individual attention will be paid to those put under their charge. The strictest attention will be given to the improvement of the morals of all, and especially to those of the females.

TERMS OF TUITION

Reading, writing, and spelling $2.00 English Grammar & Geography 2.50 Chemistry and Natural Philosophy 3.00 Astronomy 4.00

342A quarter will consist of twelve weeks or sixty-five days, and no allowance will be made for absences unless prevented from attendance by sickness or by special agreement.

342In the 21 August 1844 issue of the Neighbor, the Coles thanked the parents of Nauvoo for supporting their school and announced that they were leaving and that Eli Kelsey would take over. He used the latter half of the Coles' ad until his last one appeared in the 23 April 1845 issue.

342In the second type of school, the teachers were paid by the community. Consequently, all teachers were required to pass a certification examination and to submit an attendance roll showing the number of student days taught to determine how much they would be paid. The school records for Nauvoo show that many different teachers participated in this type of school. It appears that Howard taught in both types of schools with Martha assisting him. Their early schools were subscription schools which they must have filled by word of mouth referrals since there is no record of their ever advertising in the Nauvoo Neighbor for students. Also, the Prophet Joseph Smith and others supported the Corays by sending their children to their school. And that was a vote of confidence for the Corays since they were not the only school teachers in Nauvoo. Historians James B. Allen and Glen M. Leonard state in The Story of the Latter-day Saints that "at least eighty different men and women served as teachers in Nauvoo." [^28] Although some of these teachers may have stayed for just a few weeks since some advertisements appeared only once or twice in the Neighbor, teaching school in Nauvoo was a very important activity, and Howard and Martha Coray were involved in teaching most of their years there.

Two reminiscences of attending the Corays' school might be appropriate to quote here. The first is a recollection by Joseph Smith III about attending Brother Coray's school. When he and a classmate were to be punished for something they had done in school, Brother Coray had them go out and get switches with which to be punished. The classmate chose a tiny stick while Joseph III chose a much larger one. Howard "surprised them both by switching switches, making the punishment fit their respective crimes" and justice prevailed. [^29] In the other reference, Emily Dow Partridge p.343] Smith Young remembered that she had "the privilege of attending school that summer, taught by Brother and Sister Howard Coray." [^30]

343Some time during their stay in Nauvoo, the Corays built a log home (see Fig. 3) which traditionally is placed at the northwest corner of the intersection of Young and Barnett Streets, just two blocks east and one block north of the temple. But some think that was the site of their school and that the log cabin was built on Block 70, Lot 3, the area where the Garden to Women now is. Wherever it was, the log home is the home they tried to sell when they left the city and were able to sell only the windows and doors before they moved west with the main body of the Saints in May 1846.

343It had been Martha's habit to carry a pencil and paper to church meetings and record what was said. In the fall of 1844, Mother Smith, having heard about Martha's stenographic ability, asked her to help her "write the history of Joseph; to act in the matter, only as her, Mother Smith's, amanuensis." [^31] After much concern and discussion about leaving her work at the school, Martha took the assignment. Before long, Howard was also asked to leave teaching school to assist Martha with the project of writing Joseph's history. At first he hesitated because he felt a keen responsibility to his students, but when President Brigham Young encouraged him to help with the history, Howard turned the school over to their assistant, Brother Woolley, and spent his full time assisting his wife in her work as amanuensis in preparing the history of the Prophet Joseph Smith by his mother. Martha and Howard worked together on the project from the fall of 1844 until near the end of 1845. Mother Smith would talk about the things she remembered about her prophet-son, and the Corays would take notes and transcribe them at night to create the manuscript.

343When they had completed the manuscript, Martha made a second handwritten copy of it. She gave one copy to Mother Smith and retained the other, which she gave to President Brigham Young after they got to Utah. Mother Smith's copy of the manuscript found its way into the hands of Orson Pratt, who took the manuscript to England and published it in Liverpool in 1853. There were several errors in the book due to Mother Smith's faulty memory and Elder Pratt's insufficient knowledge concerning the compilation of the manuscript.

President Brigham Young was aware of some of these errors and was quite displeased when Elder Pratt published the book.

345Then, when he saw other church leaders reading the 1853 book, he recommended destroying it because it perpetuated errors concerning the Prophet Joseph, which he accused "Mrs. Corey" [sic] of introducing as she tried to make the book into a kind of novel when she wrote the manuscript. [^32]

345Mother Smith was at the time she gave her history, in a very low state of health, at times suffering great from the rheumatism, and often much suffocated with an affliction of the chest. The baleful influence of her evil-minded son, William, was another great cause of confusion. Hyrum and Joseph were dead, and thus without their aid, she attempted to prosecute the work, relying chiefly upon her memory, having little recourse to authentic statements whose corresponding dates might have assisted her. The book, when finished by me, required a thorough revision by those who possessed better facilities for correcting it than were accessible to me.

345There were two manuscripts prepared, one copy was given to Mother Smith, and the other retained in the Church. The first copy fell into the hands of Mr. Arthur Milikin [Millikin], Mother Smith's son-in-law [married Lucy Smith on 4 June 1840 in Nauvoo], and went from him, I hear, to A. W. Babbit [Almon W. Babbitt, president of the Kirtland Stake, 1841-43], Esq. and afterwards came into the possession of an editor named Sheen [Isaac Sheen was later an RLDS General Authority] and was sold by him to Elder Orson Pratt, who took it to England and published it in its crude state.

345The preface, printed by Elder Orson Pratt, claims that the work was mostly written under the supervision of Joseph Smith. This was not true, as it was not commenced until sometime after his death.

345Mother Smith was a kind, warm-hearted, noble and good woman. She rejoiced in her family, and had a natural desire to perpetuate their memory, and trusting too much to recollection, she was in some things mistaken. But who has never been mistaken? I wrote the book and my statements were faithful and true as far as I could learn at that time, but upon further information, I am convinced that it contains many unavoidable errors, and should not have been printed in the present form, but being printed should be suppressed. I respected that it would pass through the hands of the Church Historian, but as it did not, its publication seems to have been thoroughly improper. [^33]

346Thanks to David J. Whittaker, BYU archivist, we have found the record of the payment given the Corays for compiling and transcribing the manuscript. Dated 14 January 1846, Bishop Newel K. Whitney authorized paying Howard Coray $200 "For compiling the history of Lucy Smith, mother of the Prophet Joseph," and $35 for transcribing it. "Fifty dollars of the above bill to [be] paid in Store goods." [^34]

346All the time Howard and Martha were working on the manuscripts, the situation with the non-Mormons around Nauvoo continued to deteriorate, and during the winter of 1845-46 the Corays prepared to move west. And yet everything that was happening in Nauvoo was not bad. On 21 January 1846, Howard and Martha received their "Indowments [sic] in the Nauvoo Temple: and were re-sealed over the Altar by Prest. Young-had Howard and Martha, the only children we had at that time, adopted to us." [^35] The Corays left Nauvoo in a big, horse-drawn wagon in May 1846 "with the main body of the Saints, for a new location, somewhere west; but how far, I had not the remotest idea; neither had the Saints generally; and I doubt if there were any that knew very much about the matter." [^36] They got as far as the Missouri River where her parents were, and Martha gave birth to their third child, Harriet Virginia Knowlton Coray, on 9 August 1846. Howard spent the winter helping his father-in-law, Sidney A. Knowlton, take care of his stock. He raised corn in the summer of 1847 and secured a contract to haul 26,000 bushel of corn for the U.S. Quartermaster at Ft. Kearny [^37] on the Missouri River through the winter of 1847-48, but Howard was swindled out of his pay by a dishonest quartermaster. [^38]

346In the spring of 1848, the Corays moved a few miles south into Atchison County, Missouri, to the Nishanebotany River (now Nishnabotna), where Martha took care of her family, tended the ferry, and delivered their fourth child, Mary Knowlton Coray on 22 April 1848. Howard broke several yoke of oxen, plowed prairie with them, and raised a crop of corn. In 1849 they moved up the Missouri River to Kanesville, Iowa (now Council Bluffs), where they "put in a fine garden and 8 acres of corn." [^39] After the harvest, Howard sold his plot to Orson Hyde and moved his family to spend the winter of 1849-50 in the new Ft. Kearny near Grand Island, Nebraska, on the Platte River. Their fifth child, Ephrina Serepa Coray, was born there on 4 February 1850. Later that year, they reached Salt Lake City, traveling with the John Sharp company.

347The Corays lived in Salt Lake City for about four years, and Howard found work as a clerk in the General Tithing office for which he was paid $1,000 a year. The family resided in the old Salt Lake Thirteenth Ward. Martha cared for their children, gave birth to their six and seventh children, a fifth daughter, Helena (Nellie) Knowlton Coray, on 1 February 1852, and a second son, William Henry Coray, on 3 November 1853, and in 1854 served as the secretary of the Thirteenth Ward Female Indian Relief Society. [^40]40 In 1855 the Corays sold their Salt Lake City property for $1,000 and moved to E. T. City in Tooele County, Utah, to farm (Ezra Taft City is now Lakepoint). Martha gave birth there to their eighth child, Sidney Algernon Coray, on 9 July 1855. There is a note in The Utah Knowltons of the birth of another son in E.T. City named Wilford Coray on 26 July 1856. [^41]

347We do know that the Corays were not very successful in farming the alkali soil in E. T. City, since they moved almost destitute to Provo in 1857. They lived in Provo until 1871, about 14 years. Howard's journal entry for the time in Provo reads, "doing various kinds of work-farming, clerking, school teaching, building a saw mill and sawing lumber, running a molasses factory, and hauling lumber to Fairfield, etc, etc. I was getting on pretty well till I undertook the saw mill, which resulted in embarrassing me, not a little." [^42] The Corays had their four last children, all sons, born to them in Provo: George Quincy Coray, 26 November 1857; Francis De LaVan Coray, 17 January 1860; Louis Laville Coray, 9 March 1862; and Don Silas Rathbone Coray, 20 September 1864.

347Retaining their home in Provo, the Corays homesteaded a 160-acre quarter section near Mona in Juab County. They built a fine house, planted trees, and were much involved in farm and community life (see Fig. 4). Both Howard and Martha were involved in the Mona Irrigation Company, Incorporated. Howard was the secretary, and he recorded in company minutes under the date of 27 March 1875 that Martha was "appointed agent for the Co-to collect evidence and employ counsel and transact such business as may be necessary to prevent or meet a suit of law which the Goshen Irrigation District has given notice shall be planted against usÂ�." [^43]

349Martha kept a daily journal during the years 1855-1858 and 1873-1878. The earlier journals are so light they are barely legible, but the later ones are clearly legible. In them Martha often referred to the weather, especially noting storminess and cold. Those journals show her involved in ranch matters, running the house, preserving fruit, keeping up with correspondence with their married children and sons who were on missions, and working on the Brigham Young Academy Board of Trustees after 1875. They also show she knew herbs and grew and harvested them for sale.

It was while the Corays were working their homestead property in Mona, Utah, that President Brigham Young acted on his proposal to start an academy in Provo. When he was assigning people to the Board of Trustees, he determined there should be a female member "to represent women's interests on the Board" and assigned Martha Jane Coray to that position. [^44] Howard supported her in this assignment, often taking her the 45 miles to Provo to meet with the Board. Martha signed the Deed of Trust of the Brigham Young Academy when it was drawn up on 16 October 1875, [^45] and made the motion that Warren N. Dusenberry be assigned the first principal. [^46] [ He served only temporarily because of conflicting outside commitments, and Karl G. Maeser was appointed the first full-time principal a few months later. Martha's journals for those years are interlaced with entries concerning activities and matters on the ranch and the Academy. They often state she went to Provo to attend "Ex committee meeting[s]." The entry for the signing of the deed has already been noted. For Friday, 26 November 1875, Martha recorded, "Trustees Met made rules examined Hall and so forth Rained hard." Under the date of "Sat. 1 Jan 1876," she wrote, "Willie and Frank [sons William Henry and Francis De LaVan] worked on Academy fence. I went to Dedication dinner. Bp Smoot master in Chancery." For Monday, 3 January, the day set for opening the Academy, she noted, "Pa and I opened school in 1st Ward Academy also opened with 4 of the Board of Trustees present-Bp Smoot-W H Duzenberry [sic] [^47] H. Cluff and Mrs J. Coray-our school is horrid noisy." Tuesday she noted, "School is a little better," and Wednesday it was "still improving." Thursday she wrote, "School improving but I am nearly speechless with so much talk." And Friday's entry says, "I am much better but school was outrageous in afternoon." In a 10 April letter to President Brigham Young, Martha noted that for her the "principle of p.350] [ education has been God's laws of religion first, Man's laws of honor and morality second, Science of every attainable kind [next]. Does not the Deed [of Brigham Young Academy] require the sacred book[s] mentioned [the Bible, Book of Mormon, and Doctrine and Covenants] to be taken up as a study in the same way as the sciences mentioned?" [^48]

350Under the date of Saturday, 15 April, 1876, she wrote, "Attended A. B. Meeting-put in Brother Maeser." On Monday, 24 April, she wrote, "Pa and I went to see Bro. Maeser open Academy-I attended Ex-committee meeting. Bro. Maeser came. We fixed tuition at $4.00 per scholar." Her entry for Saturday, 13 May 1876, reads, "Executive meeting I attended-no one else came." Her journals show that she was also involved in discussions of ideas and often wrote articles for the Territorial Enquirer (the Provo newspaper). She was also an occasional contributor to The Woman's Exponent. [^49]

350On 1 March 1880, President George Q. Cannon, in his capacity as editor of The Juvenile Instructor, wrote an editorial in support of the Brigham Young Academy and quoted the following from a letter which Martha had written to solicit support for the school:

350The great key to the past success of this institution, has been largely attributable, not only to the uncommon scope in learning of the principal, but his strong and unflinching trust in God. The spirit of confidence and zeal in every department of this institution, shows conclusively that the energy of the presiding mind is imparted in a degree to each and all of the teachers. Scholars fully feel the moving power of the same spirit, and bend to their work with a will, deeming themselves inexcusably deficient, if in the least they fail to accomplish any task assigned to them.

350Elders, fathers and mothers in Israel, would do well to consider and give close attention to the aims of this place, and more particularly to the actual results arising from even a short experience, as a student in the Academy; and how close the principles of faith, honor and a deep desire for general intelligence cling to the scholar-even after his departure [from] school they do not leave him. [^50]

It was also in 1880 that Howard moved a sick Martha from their ranch in Juab County back to the house in Provo to stay. She had apparently had a cough for several years that worsened that year. But even the medical help available in Provo could not help Martha, and she died 14 December 1881, in her sixtieth year. Her obituaries were very laudatory. The Salt Lake Herald noted in its 16 December 1881 issue that Mrs. Coray "was a remarkable woman p.351] and her superior qualities impressed themselves upon those who approached her even for a brief period. She was possessed of indomitable energy, and [she was] widely read and cultured." Another, from the 21 December 1881 issue of the Deseret News, noted that she was "a woman among ten thousandÂ�. Almost by her own exertion she reared a family of twelve childrenÂ�. She possessed a great independence of character, marked natural intelligence and considerable culture. The nobler traits of womankind have been exhibited in her life to a degree that is seldom excelled" (21 Dec 1881). Her funeral was held in the Provo Tabernacle and the speakers included President Joseph F. Smith, Second Counselor in the First Presidency, and Wilford Woodruff, President of the Quorum of the Twelve Apostles who, with others, had taken a special train down from Salt Lake City. [^51]

351As complimentary as her obituaries are, the most beautiful tribute is the private one Howard wrote in his journal:

351A more intelligent, self sacrificing, and devoted wife, and mother, few men have been blessed with. She became the mother of 12 children-7 sons & 5 daughters, and lived to see them all grown to man and womanhood; educated, intelligent, virtuous and religious. In this great work, she acted well her part. In February 1840, she embraced the Gospel and soon became well acquainted with the Prophet; and as such greatly venerated him. I have frequently heard her say, that he himself was the greatest miracle to her, she had ever seen; and that she valued her acquaintance with him above everything else. She lived as a consistent Latter-day Saint up to the time of her demise; which event occurred on the 14th day of December 1881. Her age, when she passed away, was 59 years, 6 months and 11 days.

351Now, that she has gone, the fondest hope I have is to again strike hands with the wife of my youth, in a more genial clime, where sorrowing and sighing there is none, and parting shall be no more. [^52]

351In 1902, her daughter, Martha Jane Knowlton Coray Lewis, wrote the following of her mother:

351Mrs. Coray was a many-sided woman. Though, in a measure, self-taught; she was a rapid and lucid writer, a brilliant conversationalist, and a fine speaker on a wide range of subjects. She had a fair knowledge of law, philosophy, history, poetry, chemistry and geology-the latter two being her favorites studies. She could and did assay minerals, and distil herbs, write eloquent lectures, and cook dinners that would tempt the appetite of an epicure. [^53]

On 16 October 1986, Brigham Young University recognized Martha's contribution to the the early development of the university, as a member of its original Board of Trustees, by naming the p.352] assembly room in the newly remodelled Karl G. Maeser Building the Martha Jane Knowlton Coray Lecture Hall. The Maeser Building houses General and Honors Education.

352After Martha's death, Howard was called by his stake president, Abraham O. Smoot, on a home mission "to travel and preach in the Utah Stake of Zion." [^54] He carried the following letter of commendation with him:

352To the Bishops and Brethren of the Utah Stake

352Dear Brethren: I take pleasure in recommending to your kind consideration Elder Howard Coray whom I have appointed to travel and preach in the Wards in our Stake, hoping you will extend to him your aid in fulfilling the duties to which he has been appointed, also wishing that his labors may prove instructive and beneficial to all whom he may address. [^55]

352He spoke in all the wards in the stake.

352In May 1882, President John Taylor called Howard to serve a mission in the Southern States Mission. He was set apart on 13 June 1882 and travelled to Virginia with several other missionaries. His journal reads as follows:

352I reached Smith Co. Virginia on the 20th June 1882-the place to which I had been sent to labor in the ministry. Here I spent all my time travelling about the County-preaching in a private way-that is in stores, Post Office-where I stayed over night, and wherever an opportunity might offer etc, etc. [^56]

352He was released and returned home in time to attend Conference in April 1883.

352That entry concludes Howard's Journal. One work notes that he spent the rest of his life "in rather uneventful retirement at the homes of his sons and daughters located in Southern Colorado, or in Utah and Salt Lake Counties in Utah." [^57] However, he did not just sit and do nothing for those years. A certificate of ordination in the Coray Collection in the BYU archives shows that Howard was ordained a patriarch on 5 May 1897 by Franklin D. Richards and Charles W. Penrose. [^58]

352Howard Coray lived for 27 years after Martha died before he died in Salt Lake City at about 10:00 pm on Thursday, 16 January 1908. His funeral was also held in the Tabernacle in Provo, and the main speaker was Joseph F. Smith, President of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, who had made the special train trip down from Salt Lake City. Howard was buried next to Martha in the Provo City Cemetery.

353Even in death many of the Howard and Martha Jane Knowlton Coray family have stayed near each other. Their family plot (at about 2nd South and 1st West within the Provo City Cemetery) is the burial place of Howard and Martha, four of their children and their spouses, and several grandchildren.
