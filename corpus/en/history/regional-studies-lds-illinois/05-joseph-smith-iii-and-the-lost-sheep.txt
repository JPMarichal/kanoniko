Joseph Smith III and the "Lost Sheep"

Susan Easton Black

Religious confusion permeated the infrastructure of the Church following the martyrdom of the Prophet Joseph Smith. The question of whose right it was to succeed Joseph was asked by saints, sinners, and fence-sitters. Honest men and women dramatically conflicted over the proper course to follow. Designing men with self-aggrandized agendas took advantage of the confused extremes "to lift themselves into positions of authority." [^1]

55The "Lo, here!" and "Lo, there!" of the 1840s was reminiscent of an earlier day in Palmyra when young Joseph had wondered, "What is to be done? Who of all these parties are right; or, are they all wrong together?" ( JS-H 1:5 ). Similarly, his devoted followers questioned, "If any one of them be right, which is it, and how shall I know it?" (1:10). The struggle to discover light and truth among the discordant cries left many disappointed and disillusioned.

The Scattering Begins

Many Latter-day Saints maintain that the unanimous vote supporting the Quorum of the Twelve Apostles on 8 August 1844 in Nauvoo, Illinois, provided the resolution to the religious controversy of succession. However, not all of the early Saints would agree. Sidney Rigdon, William Smith, Lyman Wight, and James Strang each broke from the tenuous accord by declaring himself to be in opposition to the Twelve. By April 1845 Rigdon was president of a splinter faction in Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania. Although his organization lacked cohesive elements, it stood defiantly against unanimity. William Smith's assertion that "young Joseph is the only legal successor to the presidency of this church" and his feigned right to guard the seed of Joseph sparked further splintering. [^2] Lyman Wight's stubborn determination to fulfill a mission to Texas split off yet another faction.

56 James Strang's purported correspondence with the Prophet Joseph and his contrived angelic ordination deceived many, including the elect. His prophetic edict that seven members of the Twelve be "delivered over to the buffetings of Satan in the flesh" was cheered by his admirers. [^3] Charles Thompson's claim to personal revelations gave him pretended prophetic powers. His gullible followers gave their earthly possessions, even clothing and services, to him, their "Father Ephraim." James Brewster assured his disciples that Joseph Smith had ordained and blessed him to be a prophet, seer, revelator, and translator.

56And so the list of would-be usurpers of God's Church touted their wares, adorned with deceiving glitter, and attracted disciples from among the Saints that had known the Prophet Joseph Smith. As their doctrinal insights soon failed to create illuminating lustre, the factions splintered again and again under the guise of new, divinely called leaders. William Bickerton feigned that his rebellion against Rigdon began with a heavenly "vision, and in the vision I was on the highest mountain on the earth." [^4] Alpheus Cutler humbly conceded that his call came when he was ordained to be God's prophet.

56The once tranquil society of beautiful Nauvoo was in turmoil as Saints were quickly scattered by winds of doctrine in 1845. Hundreds of disillusioned residents left Nauvoo, struggling with religious discord, hoping for utopian harmony. As former citizens fled to Wisconsin, Kentucky, Pennsylvania, and Texas, the strength once known in cohesive worship dwindled. Misguided, independent thinkers seeking truth found deception on every side.

57The Reorganized Church of Jesus Christ of Latter Day Saints' published view of the scattering swayed toward mercy:

57Certain it is that there were many good, noble, and honorable men with each faction; and we deeply regret the unfortunate tendency of the time to seek to cover those of opposite views with shame and disgrace, and to follow them with cursing instead of sympathy and blessing. [^5]

57The written view of the Quorum of the Twelve Apostles on 23 December 1847 from Winter Quarters, Nebraska, is a strong repudiation of the leaders of the factions: "Since the murder of President Joseph Smith, many false prophets and false teachers have arisen, and tried to deceive many." [^6] My own view tips toward justice; yet, I recognize the prevailing religious confusion and the compelling power of charismatic and Napoleonic leaders. My sympathetic reflections extend to the deceived seekers of truth who were often disappointed in one faction and embraced another and yet another. Their shifting religious loyalties carved for themselves the unsavory reputation of "fickleness, changeability, and instability." [^7]

57Listed among those caught in the web of multiple deceptions were Jason W. Briggs, Zenos Gurley, James Blakeslee, and William Marks. Of these, Zenos Gurley and Jason Briggs are credited with being the fathers of the Reorganization movement. They planted the hope of an organizational rebirth reminiscent of the days of the Prophet Joseph. By 1851 they were advocating that the leadership of the church belonged to Joseph's oldest son, although he was only a boy of eleven at the death of his father. Their teaching and preaching found fertile soil among the disaffected who longed for the unity that had once prevailed in Mormondom. Individuals and even organized branches in Wisconsin began to listen and to whisper aloud the hope that Joseph Smith III would continue his father's work. Former Latter-day Saints gave emotional, dramatic, stirring testimonies, by speaking and singing in tongues of their visions and dreams that were centered on the theme of Joseph Smith III being the Lord's appointed prophet.

The first official pronouncement of his calling as prophet was declared at a Strangite branch in Wisconsin. After the branch renounced James J. Strang as prophet, they acknowledged the seed of Joseph Smith "to come forth in the own due time of the Lord." [^8] This scene was repeated at another Strangite branch in Beloit, Wisconsin, in June 1852. It was not the intention of these pronouncements to organize a new church, for those voting viewed themselves as acting as members and officers of the original Church. They believed they were merely setting in order the affairs of the original Church.

58Historian Roger Launius writes that their pronouncements and resolutions created a Reorganization "born as a dissenting movement." [^9] [ These dissenters waited patiently for the future promises which had been emotionally expressed in spirited testimonies. Launius concludes, "A diversity of congregations, personalities, and ideas all came together in only the loosest of organizations,Â�proud of their diversity, jealous of their independence, [they] gloried in its internal divisions." [^10]

58Waiting was not an inactive position. On 6 April 1853, at a conference in Zarahemla, Wisconsin, seven were ordained to the apostleship, a church recorder was sustained, and the Zarahemla Stake was established. Publishing of pamphlets, newspapers, and even hymns was given high priority. Missionaries labored to gather the "lost sheep" from among the dissenting factions and to welcome them into the fold with love and mercy. One missionary, W. W. Blair, proselytized in the late 1850s in Iowa and Pennsylvania among the early Saints who had once known the Prophet Joseph Smith. He ignited the hope of Joseph Smith's son inheriting his father's prophetic call among the followers of Alpheus Cutler, Granville Hedrick, Charles Thompson, Sidney Rigdon, James Strang, and Lyman Wight. His conviction and untiring labor throughout his life led to 435 baptisms, confirmations, and ordinations of record. Other zealous missionaries were like the woman who "swept the[houseÂ�seek[ing] diligently" the one piece of silver, and "when she hath found it, she calleth her friends and her neighbours together, saying, Rejoice with me; for I have found the piece which I had lost" ( Luke 15:9 ). However, baptismal records and original ordinations prove that the harvest of the scattered was small in the 1850s.

Of those who did unite with the Reorganization by baptism or acknowledgement of their original baptism, thirty-three had joined with James Strang, nine with Sidney Rigdon, and six with William Smith. The following had leadership positions under Strang: Aaron Smith was a counselor; William Marks served as a high councilor, bishop, and counselor; and William Smith, James Blakeslee, Ebenezer Page, Jehiel Savage, H. P. Brown, and James M. Adams were Strang's apostles. Ebenezer Robinson served as a counselor to Sidney Rigdon, and James Blakeslee and Josiah Ells were members of his Quorum of the Twelve. Andrew Ballantyne, Spencer Smith, Edwin Cadwell, Alva Smith, and Jason W. Briggs were apostles for William Smith.

60The meager harvest of the scattered in the 1850s may be attributed to splinter factions that quickly formed within the Reorganization. It may also correlate with members of the RLDS Twelve being excommunicated for apostasy and presumption of authority in 1854. Regardless of these speculations, it is important to know that by 1858 the Reorganization was strongly rooted in small branches in Wisconsin. By 1859 the "priesthood" structure appeared strengthened, but without the leadership of Joseph Smith III it was "like a wave of the sea driven with the wind and tossed" ( James 1:6 ).

Joseph Smith III: President by Lineal Descent

Joseph Smith III wrote a detailed account of the events leading to his presidency of the Reorganization. In the summer of 1853 he had his "first serious impressions concerning my connection with the work of my fatherÂ�. I believed the gospel so far as I comprehend[ed] it." [^11]

I saw stretched out before me towns, cities, busy marts, courthouses, courts, and assemblies of men, all busy and all marked by those characteristics that are found in the world, where men win place and renown. This stayed before my vision till I had noted clearly that choice of preferment here was offered to him who would enter inÂ�. In the subtle transition of a dream I was gazing over a wide expanse of country in a prairie landÂ�. [F]arm and farmhouse, pleasant cot and homelike place, everywhere betokening thrift, industry, and the pursuits of a happy peace were open to the view. I remarked to him standing by me, but whose presence I had not before noticed, "This must be the country of a happy people." To this he replied, "Which would you prefer, life, success, and renown among the busy scenes that you first saw, or a place among these people, without honors or renown? Think of it well, for the choice will be offered to you sooner or later, and you must be prepared to decide. Your decision once made you cannot recall it, and must abide the result. [^12]

61Purposeful meditation led Joseph Smith III to declare he was "at length prepared to answer when the opportunity for the choice should be given." [^13] The question often presented was: "Why not go to Utah?" Another vision and an audible voice declaring, "Because the light in which you stand is greater than theirs," became his answer. [^14]

61Confident of his religious stance, Joseph welcomed to his home Samuel H. Gurley and Edmund C. Briggs from the former Strangite Branch in Zarahemla, Wisconsin. They delivered to him "the word of the Lord," that read:

61A Zerubbabel in Israel art thou. As a nail fastened in a sure place, so are the promises unto thee to make thee a restorer in Zion-to set in order the house of GodÂ�. And like Ezra of old with his brethren, by the direction of the Holy Spirit have we sent faithful messengers to bear this our message to you, trusting that you will by their hands notify us of your readiness to occupy that seat, and answer to the name and duties of that seed. For this have our prayers been offered up without ceasing for the last five years. [^15]

61Joseph Smith III claimed, "I read the message that they brought, but could not accept it as they had hoped." Jason Briggs reacted vehemently to the discouraging news and "urged the matter upon meÂ�in tones of thunder, and almost dictatorially directed me to accept the message, and do as directed therein; or reject it at my peril." [^16]

61I have always avoided talking to my children about having anything to do in the church, for I have suffered so much I have dreaded to have them take any part in itÂ�. But I have always believed that if God wanted them to do anything in the church, the One who called their father would make it known to them. [^17]

61By the fall of 1859 Joseph Smith III had recanted from his earlier reluctance by announcing his intention to lead the[Church"-[Mother] approved my determination." [^18] A letter to William Marks announcing his position read:

61Mr. William Marks; Sir:-I am soon going to take my father's place at the head of the Mormon Church, and I wish that you, and some others, those you may consider the most trustworthy, the nearest to you, to come and see me; that is, if you can and will. [^19]

62William Marks, Israel Rogers, and William Blair soon came to Nauvoo to discuss with Smith his prophetic call. Marks stated, "If God has called you, we want to know it. If he has, the church is ready to sustain you; if not, we want nothing to do with you." [^20] [ Rumors of this meeting and Joseph Smith III's desire "to take [his] father's place" were heralded throughout the small branches of ardent believers in Wisconsin, Illinois, and Iowa. Attendance at the general conference held on 6 April 1860 in Amboy, Illinois, reflected the joyful anticipation of his leadership. When Joseph entered the conference with his mother by his side, "a strange thrill pervaded the air." [^21] After being introduced he declared:

62I came not here of myself, but by the influence of the Spirit. For some time past I have received manifestations pointing to the position which I am about to assume. I wish to say that I have come here not to be dictated by any men or set of men. I have come in obedience to a power not my own, and shall be dictated by the power that sent me.

62Should you take me as a leader, I propose that all should be dealt by in mercy, open as to Gentile or JewsÂ�. I pledge myself to promulgate no doctrine that shall not be approved by you or the code of good morals. [^22]

62On a motion by Isaac Sheen it was "resolved, that Brother Joseph Smith be chosen Prophet, Seer, and Revelator of the Church of Jesus Christ, and the successor of his father." [^23] The unanimous vote was followed by a second vote of agreement that Emma Bidamon, the fifty-six-year-old widow of the Prophet Joseph Smith, be received into fellowship. Immediately following, Joseph Smith III was ordained president of the high priesthood of the church by Zenos Gurley, Samuel Powers, William W. Blair, and William Marks.

62Even though the Reorganized church was eight years old in 1860 when Joseph Smith III became president, according to Roger Launius, the quorums were in a "shamblesÂ�[with] no First Presidency; Bishop Israel L. Rogers acted alone; and, more important, there were only five members of the apostolic quorum remaining." [^24] With a president who would guide the church for fifty-four years, the organizational structure began to solidify. Emma's appointment on 9 June 1860 to select hymns gave further stability to the fledgling church. [^25]

During the early years of Joseph Smith III's presidency, he penned his title as "President of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints." This title confused followers and through their persuasion was changed to "President of the Reorganized Church of Jesus Christ of Latter Day Saints." This early confusion, along with Smith's refusal to designate a gathering site, led disgruntled Saints to wish that his younger, more sensitive brother claim the office of president. David Hyrum Smith poetically silenced the disruptive tide:

63Joseph is the Chosen Prophet; Well ordained in God's clear sight, Should he loose it by transgression, Alexander has the right. Joseph, Alexander, David, Three remaining pillars still; Like the three remaining columns, Of the Temple on the hill. Joseph's star is full and shining; Alexander's more than mine; Mine is just below the mountain; Bide its time and it will shine. [^26]

63The united Smith contingency of Emma, Joseph, Alexander, and David proved a great strength to the Reorganization during the formative years. The family bond held together fragmented branches throughout the midwest when decentralized gathering was preached. The most prominent branch in the 1860s, known as the Olive Branch of Nauvoo, met at the rented premises of Benjamin Austin before congregating in Joseph Smith's little brick store. By 1865 the membership numbered seventy-five and included Emma and her sons, Joseph Smith III, Alexander, and David. Emma's correspondence reflected her regular attendance even after Joseph's removal to Plano, Illinois: "Our meetings are rather poorly attendedÂ�. The outsiders have left off attending. I think some of them are a little like myself, They miss my boys." [^27]

The decentralized gathering was "strange to many, and a severe trial to some who for years had esteemed the gathering to be the paramount feature of the work." [^28] Yet, President Smith's counsel proved thoughtful: "Before any gathering could take place there are many obstacles to be met by us, which are to be overcome, not the least of which isÂ�prejudice." [^29] In order to remove the prejudice and unleashed bias toward the Reorganization, missionaries were called to reclaim the scattered Saints. Calls to find the "lost sheep" in the "American Midwest with lesser activity among groups in the East" were readily accepted. [^30] Willing disciples flocked to the Reorganization in Wales, England, and "in all places where the old saints were." [^31] In 1863 the rue Latter Day Saints' Herald reported that the First Presidency had received more than five-hundred letters from the Great Basin "urging young Joseph to send missionaries there." [^32]

64On 6 September 1863 Jason Briggs presided over the first RLDS baptismal service in Salt Lake City. Soon a RLDS branch was created in the Salt Lake Valley, despite religious persecution and bigotry:

64We had been seated probably half an hour, when we were saluted by volley after volley of rocks, with occasional pistol shots, from a mob, numbering from thirty to forty, who were yelling and shouting like infuriated demons. [^33]

64RLDS elder M. H. Forscutt reported a paper inserted under his window sill threatened, "If not out of this Territory in one week, you will die the death of a miserable apostate dog." [^34] Undaunted, the missionaries baptized and formed additional branches in Provo, Spanish Fork, American Fork, Ogden, and throughout the Utah Territory.

64Moderate success among the Brighamites prompted Joseph Smith III and his brothers, Alexander and David, to join the missionaries in the Salt Lake Valley. Emma Smith Bidamon penned her uncertainty of Alexander's influence on his "apostate" relatives:

64I think it might be right for him to go and discharge his duty to them and leave them without excuse. I look upon their case as a hard one. I believe that God is able to do all that is for his glory and the good of those that truly serve him. [^35]

64His influence on relatives, like Joseph Fielding Smith, was negligible, but upon the less stalwart in Beaver, San Bernardino, and Ogden, his influence and that of his brothers was great. Articles in the Saints' Herald repeatedly confirmed that, despite persecution, contention, and prejudice, the tireless efforts of the Smiths and other missionaries were disturbing Brigham's hold on Mormondom: "The work is progressing rapidly in various localities." [^36]

The conversion rate among the Brighamites was not as great as the conversion rate in the midwest. By 1890 the main geographic location of the Reorganization was in the midwest, where forty- three percent of the membership had located. The secondary location was the Pacific Slope, where missionaries baptized and formed branches among Mormon dissidents. Early demographic studies show that (by 1890), 22,851 members of the Reorganized church were residents of the United States or Canada. They resided in thirty of the United States, in Canada, the Society Islands, the British Isles, Australia, and throughout Europe. [^37]

Joseph Smith III's Effort to Reclaim the "Lost Sheep"

Richard Howard, former RLDS historian, believes the question of numbers gathering to the Reorganization may never be fully answered. On 24 August 1982 he wrote:

66The records are woefully incomplete, andÂ�so unsystematic and variant in character that it is extremely difficult to synthesize anything on the basis of the haphazardly gathered and recorded data.

66His belief is a reaction to the holdings of the RLDS Library-Archives in Independence, Missouri:

66As an example of the paucity of data available here, RLDS membership records begin with voluntary and sporadic reports to semi-annual conferences sending a representative to said conferences. The report would be in the form of a membership list which simply listed the names of persons currently enrolled in the respective branches. Not all of the branches were represented and many of those who were did not give the same kind of report. Some branches would only report the total number of members without listing names and the total number of persons serving in the various offices of priesthood, again not listing names. Gradually over the years a more systematic membership record keeping system evolvedÂ�. Many of the RLDS membership records were destroyed in the fire at church headquarters in January 1907. This adds a further complexity to any attempt to do statistical or demographic studies on the character of the early reorganization. [^38]

66Despite these barriers to accurate research, in 1982, I requested permission from the Reorganized Church of Jesus Christ of Latter Day Saints' leadership to extract information from available records to answer questions about the "lost sheep."

66I was denied permission because of the restricted access policy of the Deceased Members Cards, also known as the RLDS Deceased Files. From 1982 to 1989 I continued to seek permission, still it was not granted. Later a letter dated 31 May 1989 from Ronald E. Romig, RLDS Church Archivist, gave me my first hope of obtaining permission to research the Deceased Members Cards. He wrote, "I can not answer now, about their availability in the near future, but I will begin exploring that possibilityÂ�. I will let you know what I find out." Through his persistence and the good will of many, the First Presidency of the RLDS Church graciously opened their records in 1989 to my research, under the signature of M. Grant McMurray, then serving as World Church Secretary, currently in the First Presidency.

In July 1992, after three summers in Missouri and untold hours of searching primary source documents, I asked the RLDS Archivist, "What have I missed that would be of importance to my research?" He answered, "You have our most valuable records." With that assurance, I began to analyze my findings. This not only led to significant answers, but to a six-volume compilation of 4,800 pages entitled Early Members of the Reorganized Church of Jesus Christ of Latter Day Saints. [^39]

67A total of 25,350 members of the Reorganization are identified in the compilation. This figure may be somewhat inflated because no attempt was made not to count multiple entries that probably describe the same person. For example, when two different sources indicated that Mary Jones was a member, but no identifying dates confirmed association, both entries appear separately.

67Of this unedited membership list, 20,361 have identifying birth information. Birth dates extend from 1769 to 1848-a span of 79 years. [^40] William N. Alden was the oldest identified member, being born on 27 December 1769 in Lebanon, Windham, Connecticut. He was baptized a member of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints in October 1836 at Clinton, Oneida, New York, by A. M. Wilsey. He later affiliated with the Reorganization. [^41] Levi Gamet lived the longest, dying on 16 October 1949. He also joined The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints before being baptized and confirmed a member of the Reorganization on 1 December 1861. He was ordained a RLDS priest in 1865 and attended the Inman, Nebraska Branch. [^42]

67Of those identified as RLDS early members, records clearly show that only 1,557 members had been baptized into The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints before uniting with the Reorganization. It appears from the available records that only 69 had been baptized by those in the Quorum of the Twelve or First Presidency by June of 1844. [^43]

72Demographic studies from Early Members of the Reorganized Church of Jesus Christ of Latter Day Saints graphically illustrate other historic specifics. For example, the most often mentioned individual in biographical sketches as officiating at baptisms, confirmations, and ordinations was William W. Blair, mentioned 435 times. John Shippy was cited 371 times, while E. C. Briggs had 296 references, J. J. Cornish had 270, Zenos H. Gurley had 249, and E. C. Brand had 205. The sons of the Prophet Joseph Smith were also mentioned as officiating at baptisms, confirmations, and ordinations: Joseph Smith III had 182 references, Alexander Hale Smith had 64, while David Hyrum Smith had 34.

72The most frequent references to baptisms appear in the 1860s. The baptismal years from 1860 to 1869 show a pattern of rising growth until 1864. A decline is noted in 1865 after the Civil War. For the next four years a steady pattern of growth is apparent.

72By 1870 a modest surge in convert baptisms occurs, but a marked decline dominates the decade. The decline may correlate with the age of the once zealous missionaries who were returning to the midwest in the 1870s and being assigned leadership positions in local RLDS branches.

72The decline of the 1870s recurred in the 1880s. The descending trend of baptisms among those born before 1849 may reveal their general lack of acceptance of new religious ideas in later years.

Despite incomplete records, one conclusion is that the number of converts to the RLDS Church baptized during Joseph Smith III's presidency is small in comparison to the exponential baptismal rate of the LDS Church during his father's presidency.

Conclusion

The Reorganized Church of Jesus Christ of Latter Day Saints was created among the disillusioned but hopeful seekers of truth. RLDS missionaries labored to bring into one fold the "lost sheep." Their harvest was small; yet, rather than belittling their gain, let us recall that their gain was our loss. Their converts formed a powerful nucleus that strengthened the fledgling Reorganization. They became RLDS leaders for over thirty years by boldly sharing their testimonies of the Prophet Joseph Smith in small branches throughout the midwest. His name was known-for good-among these RLDS believers and the Book of Mormon was acknowledged by them as the word of God.

73The power of their conviction that Joseph Smith, Jr., was a prophet is a striking example of "truth[abid[ing] forever and ever" ( D&C 1:39 ). Those enlightened by truth during the days of the Prophet Joseph Smith knew they had been "enlightened by the spirit of truth" ( D&C 6:15 ) and, despite retaining only partial truth, desired to share what they knew with others. The desire kindled in Joseph Smith III led to his gathering a few of his father's friends and associates about him. His merciful remembrance of those who testified of his father's prophetic call is an example of a son, although misguided, who sought to give further honor to his father's name.
