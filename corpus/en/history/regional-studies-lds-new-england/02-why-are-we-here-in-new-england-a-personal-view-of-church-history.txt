Various authors, Regional Studies in LDS History: New England ( 1988)

Why Are We Here in New England: A Personal View of Church History

Keith W. Perkins

Last fall we decided, in the Department of Church History and Doctrine, to adopt a new direction, one that would give our faculty an opportunity to become more professional.

Our plan is to retrace Church history by visiting all the major sites associated therewith, and to have formal papers presented in each area. Following each symposium we intend to publish the proceedings in a monographic series through the Department of Church History and Doctrine. This symposium, which we expect will be the first of many, is a result of that decision.

In determining where we would go for our first annual Church History and Doctrine Symposium, New England seemed the logical place. This was the birthplace of the Church, since it is the birthplace of the Prophet Joseph Smith.

Our plan is to be in Arizona next year to visit Latter-day Saint and other western history sites, and in the future to retrace Church history to other major historical areas.

Several questions ought to be answered in determining the direction we will take in these symposia. The basic question, which I have asked myself many times, is, "How do you write and teach Church History?" Many historians, and some General Authorities, have addressed this question.

Several approaches can be taken to establish a philosophy of history. Leonard J. Arrington has said:

The challenge of writing religious history is an old one .... Was the primary purpose of such history to be faith-promoting? Should it ignore or leave out items that did not fit the purpose? Should the less-than-admirable activities of religious leaders be mentioned?...Are historians well advised to abandon that which they can get hold of only in part and with the greatest difficulty, namely, the spiritual and supernatural, in order to deal with mundane topics like changing administrations, the construction of chapels, and the establishment of new congregations? [^1]

Another writer, William Mulder, advocated more than one way to look at Church history:

I think I have been saying that there is room in Mormon historical writing for several angles of historical vision, certainly for at least a dual interpretation of "faithful history." The historian as believer must be faithful to his religious assumptions, his vision of man' s life as a spiritual quest; the historian as a skeptic must be faithful to his secular assumption, his view of man's life as a striving not always so illuminated.... Perhaps choosing one's angle of historical vision is, ultimately, as mysterious as choosing a wife: logic has little to do with it. The hope is that, once the choice has been made, we have the temperament to live with it, and in this there is no more guarantee for the faithful historian than for the maverick. [^2]

I would like to offer my personal philosophy of the writing of Church history. When I begin to write I first ask the question, "What has the Lord said on the subject?" The word of the Lord is found in two principal places: the scriptures and the words of the living Prophet.

Frequently we have ignored the scriptures as we have written and taught Church history. One reason I have decided to use the scriptures as my first guide is because of the example of Elder Bruce R. McConkie. He gave us a fresh approach to doctrine. He did not concern himself so much with what was said by others or with the traditional view of a particular subject. Elder McConkie studied the scriptures to see what they said about it. An example of this is his fresh look at the David who would arise in the last days (see Jer. 23:5-6; Ezek. 34:23-24). For years many in the Church taught that there would arise another King David who would reign over the children of Israel. Elder McConkie, examining the scriptures and ignoring traditional teaching, determined that this David was the Lord Jesus Christ (see headings for Jer. 23 and Ezek. 34 for the LDS Edition of the KJV of the Bible).

I believe that the guidelines Elder McConkie used in the area of doctrine can be useful in writing Church history. I decided to employ this method in looking at what is commonly called the Law of Consecration or the Law of Consecration and Stewardship. Using the scripture data base on our computer, I discovered that nowhere in scripture are the words "Law of Consecration" or "Law of Consecration and Stewardship" used, although the Doctrine and Covenants does refer to the "laws of consecration." (D&C 105:29) The only title given in the Doctrine and Covenants for the Lord's economic order is the "United Order." I also examined the words of General Authorities who are considered three of the greatest experts on the Lord's economic order, Harold B. Lee, J. Reuben Clark, and Marion G. Romney. They too referred to this order as the "United Order" and not the "Law of Consecration." [^3]

Another example of a narrow and incomplete view of an event or subject in Church history without examining carefully what the scriptures have said on the subject is what most historians say was the failure of Oliver Cowdery to translate from the gold plates. [^4] D&C 9:3) The Lord explained to Oliver, it was because he "did not continue as [he] commenced, when [he] began to translate, that I have taken away this privilege from [him]." (v. 5, italics added) Oliver's problem, like Peter's, was fear. (v. 11; Matt. 14:30) It is often erroneously stated that Peter failed to walk on water. He did walk (Matt. 14:29). Likewise it has been wrongly stated, I believe, that Oliver Cowdery failed to translate.

Might we not examine anew other aspects of Church history? Have we accepted the traditions of men instead of the statements of the Lord?

I am suggesting an alternative to the way Church history is currently being written by many. Too often, when we write Church history, we rely only on the historical method we were taught at the university. I plead for a return to the way religious history was written during early scriptural times. Just as the Lord said there are two ways to learn, by study and faith (D&C 88:11), so I believe that both study and faith should be used in writing Church history.

Most scripture is history that has been canonized. The Prophet Nephi in the book of Mormon said,

For the fullness of mine intent is that I may persuade men to come unto the God of Abraham, and the Go d of Isaac, and the God of Jacob, and be saved.

Wherefore, the things which are pleasing unto the world I do not write, but the things which are pleasing unto God and unto those who are not of the world. (1 Ne. 6:4-5)

Nephi's stated purpose in writing religious history was (1) to persuade men to come unto God and be saved, and (2) to write those things which are pleasing unto God and to those who are faithful Saints.

Nephi's example certainly demonstrates a different point of view than some histories written about the Church and its leaders today. But, as professors at Brigham Young University, should we do anything else but follow Nephi? If we do other than what the Lord has directed, I am confident He will not be pleased with our teaching and writing. I believe one question that each of us should ask is this: "Will this writing or teaching be pleasing to my Heavenly Father and to those Saints who are not of this word?"

Another important passage from Nephi contains additional things we should consider in our writing and teaching:

And the words which I have written in weakness will be made strong unto them; for it persuadeth them to do good; it maketh known unto them of their fathers; and it speaketh of Jesus, and persuadeth them to believe in him, and to endure to the end, which is life eternal. (2 Ne. 33:4)

As historians of the Church, we should have a much higher purpose than secular historians. Nephi reminds us that our writing should have the three-fold purpose of persuading people to (1) do good, (2) believe in Christ, (3) endure to the end to gain eternal life.

Writing this way does not mean that we don't deal honestly with the weaknesses as well as the strengths of people. Sometimes we can learn as much from the mistakes of the past as from the successes, from the weaknesses of people as well as their strengths. Consider what Nephi said about his family, even his prophet father.

And it came to pass that I, Nephi, did make out of wood a bow, and out of a straight stick, an arrow; wherefore, I did arm myself with a bow and an arrow, with a sling and with stones. And I said unto my father: Whither shall I go to obtain food?

And it came to pass that he did inquire of the Lord, for they had humbled themselves because of my words; for I did say many things unto them in the energy of my soul.

And it came to pass that the voice of the Lord came unto my father; and he was truly chastened because of his murmuring against the Lord, insomuch that he was brought down into the depths of sorrow.

And it came to pass that the voice of the Lord said unto him: Look upon the bail, and behold the things which are written.

And it came to pass that when my father beheld the things which were written upon the bail, he did fear and tremble exceedingly, and also my brethren and the sons of Ishmael and our wives. (1 Ne. 16:23-27)

Another example is King David. David used his faith in God to win the victory over Goliath, but, with Bathsheba, he lost the greatest battle of his life. (1 Sam. 17; 2 Sam. 1112; D&C 132:39)

My concern is that today we seem to have almost an obsession with being critical. This is true in coverage of General Authorities and political leaders. Must we destroy the reputation of every President of the United States, dead or alive?

Writers explain their negative views by saying they have an obligation to tell the truth. Again, the scriptures tell us that it is not enough to tell the truth; what we write must not only be true but righteous. Joseph Smith wrote in his biography, "In this history I shall present the various events in relation to this Church, in truth and righteousness, as they have transpired." (JS-H 1:2; italics added. See also D&C 50:9; 76:5; 107:84; 121:46; Moses 7:62.)

I believe this means that, although some things might be true, it might not be righteous, or appropriate to write about them. Brigham Young University's President Jeffrey R. Holland has counseled us that, as university professors, we must not only teach truth, but virtue as well. President Holland has reminded us that it was precisely because Lucifer used knowledge without virtue that he became Satan. [^5] The scriptures are replete with such counsel:

And this we saw also, and bear record, that an angel of God who was in authority in the presence of God, who rebelled against the Only Begotten Son whom the Father loved and who was in the bosom of the Father, was thrust down from the presence of God and the Son,

And was called Perdition, for the heavens wept over him-he was Lucifer, a son of the morning.

And we beheld, and lo, he is fallen! is fallen, even a son of the morning!

And while we were yet in the Spirit, the Lord commanded us that we should write the vision; for we beheld Satan, that old serpent, even the devil, who rebelled against God, and sought to take the kingdom of our God and his Christ. (D&C 76:25-28: see also 2 Nephi 24: 12-15; Moses 4:1-5)

For several reasons some things that are true still should not be written. Some things are so sacred they cannot be recorded:

And when [Christ] had said these words, he himself also knelt upon the earth; and behold he prayed unto the Father, and the things which he prayed cannot be written, and the multitude did bear record who heard him.

And after this manner do they bear record: The eye hath never seen, neither hath the ear heard, before, so great and marvelous things as we saw and heard Jesus speak unto the Father;

And no tongue can speak, neither can there be written by any man, neither can the hearts of men conceive so great and marvelous things as we both saw and heard Jesus speak; and no one can conceive of the joy which filled our souls at the time we heard him pray for us unto the Father. (3 Ne. 17:15-17)

Other truths are inappropriate because of their evil nature:

And now, my son, I command you that ye retain all their oaths, and their covenants, and their agreements in their secret abominations; yea, and all their signs and their wonders ye shall keep from this people, that they know them not, lest peradventure they should fall into darkness also and be destroyed.

For behold, there is a curse upon all this land, that destruction shall come upon all those workers of darkness, according to the power of God, when they are fully ripe; therefore I desire that this people might not be destroyed.

Therefore ye shall keep these secret plans of their oaths and their covenants from this people, and only their wickedness and their murders and their abominations shall ye make known unto them; and ye shall teach them to abhor such wickedness and abominations and murders; and ye shall also teach them that these people were destroyed on account of their wickedness and abominations and their murders. (Alma 37:27-29; see also Hel. 6:25)

Some things have not been written or taught by certain individuals because that stewardship has been given to another:

But the things which thou shalt see hereafter thou shalt not write; for the Lord God hath ordained the apostle of the Lamb of God that he should write them.

And also others who have been, to them hath he shown all things, and they have written them; and they are sealed up to come forth in their purity, according to the truth which is in the Lamb, in the own due time of the Lord, unto the house of Israel....

And behold, I, Nephi, am forbidden that I should write the remainder of the things which I saw and heard; wherefore the things which I have written sufficeth me; and I have written but a small part of the things which I saw. (1 Ne. 14:25-26, 28)

Illustrating this is the case of Lorenzo Snow, who received a dramatic revelation on the nature of God and man which he formed into the couplet, "As man now is, God once was: As God now is, man may be." [^6] Concerned that he had learned a doctrine that he should not communicate to anyone, he tucked it away in his mind. He later confided his concern to Brigham Young and asked for his counsel. President Young replied: "Brother Snow, that is a new doctrine; if true, it has been revealed to you for your own private information, and will be taught in due time by the Prophet to the Church; till then I advise you to lay it upon the shelf and say no more about it." [^7]-->

Some things should not be written because they would damage the reputation of an individual or individuals. It would be inappropriate to publish minutes of a Church court.

As teachers of Church history we also have an obligation to know how to judge Church history we read. We should ask," Is this something that would benefit our students or ourselves?" Not everything that is written is of equal value. Once again the scriptures speak to an important point. Some ancient writings are certainly more valuable than others. We have our canonized books of scripture; and we also have apocryphal books. The Lord has directed us concerning the value of these apocryphal writings in comparison to the scriptures.

Verily, thus saith the Lord unto you concerning the Apocrypha- There are many things contained therein that are true, and it is mostly translated correctly;

There are many things contained therein that are not true, which are interpolations by the hands of men.

Verily, I say unto you, that it is not needful that the Apocrypha should be translated. (D&C 91:1-3)

In the scriptures we are taught how to judge anything that comes to us. Moroni reminded us,

For behold, my brethren, it is given unto you to judge, that ye may know good from evil; and the way to judge is as plain, that ye may know with a perfect knowledge, as the daylight is from the dark night.

For behold, the Spirit of Christ is given to every man, that he may know good from evil; wherefore, I show unto you the way to judge; for every thing which inviteth to do good, and persuade to believe in Christ, is sent forth by the power and gift of Christ; wherefore ye may know with a perfect knowledge it is of God.

But whatsoever thing persuadeth men to do evil, and believe not in Christ, and deny him, and serve not God, then ye may know with a perfect knowledge it is of the devil; for after this manner doth the devil work, for he persuadeth no man to do good, no, not one; neither do his angels; neither do they who subject themselves unto him. (Moro. 7:15-17)

We are also taught that the Spirit is given to us so that we might judge correctly. The Holy Ghost is given an interesting name in the book of Moses: "the record of heaven." (Moses 6:61, 66) One reason he is given this name is that he bears record of the Father and the Son: "And the Holy Ghost beareth record of the Father and me." (3 Ne. 28:11)

I suggest that when we read or write Church history we should use three standards in order to judge a particular work. (1) We should use the scriptures. That is why they are called the Standard Works of the Church. (2) We must use the Holy Ghost to help us judge the value and correctness of any Church history we read. (3) The Lord has given us the living Prophet so we can judge correctly on matters concerning the Church. Since President Spencer W. Kimball's and President Marion G. Romney's health no longer permits them to speak to us, President Gordon B. Hinckley speaks for the First Presidency. (1985) Has he recently given us any guidance on the writing and reading of Church history?

In a commencement address given at BYU-Hawaii, 18 June 1983, President Hinckley spoke in plain terms on this important subject:

I come to you who are leaving this great institution with a plea that we stop seeking out the storms and enjoy more fully the sunlight .... I am asking that we look a little deeper for the good, that we still our voices of insult and sarcasm, that we more generously compliment virtue and effort. I am not asking that all criticism be silenced. Growth comes of correction. Strength comes of repentance. [^8]-->

He continued by asking that we turn away from the negativism in our society and speak more of a person' s virtues than his faults. He then spoke boldly concerning the recent wave of Church history writing, especially by critics of the Church:

We have those critics who appear to wish to cull out of a vast panorama of information those items that demean and belittle some of the men and women of the past who worked so hard in laying the foundation of this great cause. They find readers of their works who seem to delight in picking up these tidbits, and in chewing them over and relishing them. In so doing they are savoring a pickle, rather than eating a delicious and satisfying dinner of several courses....May I refer to a matter concerning the Church that sponsors this institution. We now seem to have a great host of critics. Some appear intent on trying to destroy us. They mock that which is sacred. They belittle that which we call divine. Some have said that we are trapped by our history, others have worked with great diligence to seek out the flaws in our early leaders. We are accused of being opposed to intellectualism....The humanists who criticize us, the so-called intellectualists who demean us, speak only from ignorance of this manifestation. They have not heard the voice of the Spirit. They have not heard it because they have not sought after it and prepared themselves to be worthy of it. Then supposing that knowledge comes only of reasoning and of the workings of the mind, they deny that which comes by the power of the Holy Ghost....To those who have experienced its workings, the knowledge gained is as real as that which is acquired through the operation of the five senses. [^9]-->

Before historians criticize Church leaders for what they perceive as weaknesses, they should read examples from scripture illustrating how the Lord many times chooses the weak to accomplish His purposes:

For ye see your calling, brethren, how that not many wise men after the flesh, not many mighty, not many noble, are called:

But God hath chosen the foolish things of the world to confound the wise; and God hath chosen the weak things of the world to confound the things which are mighty;

And base things of the world, and things which are despised, hath God chosen, yea, and things which are not, to bring to nought things that are:

That no flesh should glory in his presence. (1 Cor. 1:26-29)

Paul continues his instruction by indicating the importance of the Spirit in our lives:

And my speech and my preaching was not with enticing words of man's wisdom, but in demonstration of the Spirit and of power:

That your faith should not stand in the wisdom of men, but in the power of God. (1 Cor. 2:4-5)

One of the best examples of the Lord choosing the weak to accomplish His purposes is the story of Gideon and his army attacking the Midianites. Gideon started with an army of 32,000. The Lord informed him this was far too many, "lest Israel vaunt themselves against me, saying, Mine own hand hath saved." He told Gideon to have all those who were fearful and afraid go home. This reduced the number to 10,000, still too many for the Lord. He had Gideon further reduce the number of soldiers when he took them to refresh themselves with a drink of water. This brought their number to 300. Now the Lord could demonstrate his power through these few men, and no one could say they had won the battle by their own strength. The Lord told Gideon, "By the three hundred men that lapped will I save you, and deliver the Midianites into thine hand." (Judg. 7:2-7) Thus a great victory was achieved by a few men with the power of God. The Lord demonstrated this same principle of using the weak to confound the strong in the story of David and Goliath. (1 Sam. 17)

Many historians have been critical of the Prophet Joseph Smith because of his youth and weaknesses. "How could such a weak man accomplish all that he supposedly did?" they ask. It was precisely because of his weakness that the Lord called him: "Verily, thus saith the Lord unto you, my servant Joseph Smith, I am well pleased with your offering and acknowledgments, which you have made; for unto this end have I raised you up, that I might show forth my wisdom through the weak things of the earth. (D&C 124:1; italics added.)

The Prophet knew from the beginning of his ministry that negative reports would go forth concerning him. He said that the Lord "called me by name, and said unto me that my name should be both good and evil spoken of among all people." (JS-H 1:33) "For the work which the Lord will perform by your hand shall cause the righteous to rejoice and the wicked to rage: with one it shall be had in honor, and the other in reproach. [^10]-->

Perhaps my personal view of the place of history in the Church is best described through an experience with the Dean of Religious Education, Robert J. Matthews. In 1976 I was helping write the Gospel Doctrine lessons for the Church. My assignment was to write a lesson about the 25th section of the Doctrine and Covenants dealing with Emma Smith. I became convinced that previous Church writings had done a disservice to Emma; that, although we did not fully understand a few things that she did, especially after the death of the Prophet, throughout most of her life she had fulfilled the calling she was given by the Lord in D&C 25. I was concerned that the lesson I had written was too positive, so I took it to Brother Matthews, who then was on the Adult Correlation Review Committee of the Church. In addition, he is a good friend whose judgment I value a great deal. I explained my concern to Bob and asked him to review the lesson. After he read the lesson I asked him whether my approach had been too positive. I will never forget his response! "I would much rather face the Prophet Joseph Smith on the day of judgment having written that lesson on Emma, rather than what some other persons have written unkindly about her."

A scripture from the Book of Mormon concerning record keeping has had a profound impact on my writing. The Savior had made his appearance to the Nephite disciples following his resurrection. He had called the disciples forward, one by one, to receive a personal witness of His resurrection by allowing them to feel the emblems of the atonement in His hands and side. In a later appearance He asked Nephi for an accounting of his record keeping. The Lord actually was giving him a personal priesthood interview on how he wrote history. "Bring forth the record which ye have kept. And when Nephi had brought forth the records, and laid them before him, HE CAST HIS EYES UPON THEM." (3 Ne. 23:7-8; emphasis added.) I sincerely believe that one day we too will be called to account for what we have written and taught. I want to be able to say to the Savior, when He casts His eyes on what I have written, that I endeavored to build the Kingdom and not tear it down. May we all so write!
