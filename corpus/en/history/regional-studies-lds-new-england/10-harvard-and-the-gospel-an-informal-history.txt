Various authors, Regional Studies in LDS History: New England ( 1988)

Harvard and the Gospel: An Informal History

Alan K. Parrish

Six of the finest and fullest years of my life were spent as director of the LDS Institute of Religion at Harvard. I became acquainted with many LDS students and alumni. I heard them tell some of their experiences and those of others they had associated with. It is a history I am fond of though I am not fully qualified to tell it. This account is only a sampling of some unique Church history.

On a broad scale, Mormonism includes the entire latter-day time period and the events that have prepared the world for the restored gospel. This range includes America' s discoverers, the plymouth planters, and the Founding Fathers. In that view we may consider the founding of Harvard an important accomplishment of those settlers and part of the groundwork of Mormonism.

In September of 1630, just ten years after the pilgrims' arrival at Plymouth, the General Court of Massachusetts opened the way for a college. Interested persons donated modest amounts. A popular minister dying of consumption in Charlestown gave half his estate of 779 British pounds and all of his library to the college. There is no known picture of him, and little is known of his successes, but in honor of his contribution the college was named after John Harvard. On 28 October 1636, the Massachusetts General Court passed legislation which led to the official founding of Harvard College.

The mission of Harvard was proclaimed in its charter:" the advancement of all good literature, arts, and sciences... [and] the education of the English and Indian youth of this country in knowledge and godliness." [^1] The early statutes of Harvard (1646) held that "every one shall consider the main end of his life and studies, to know God and Jesus Christ which is Eternal life. John 17:3 .... Seeing the Lord giveth wisdom, every one shall seriously by prayer in secret, seek wisdom of Him. Prov. 2:2, 3." They even required that "if any scholar being in health shall be absent from prayer or lectures, except in case of urgent necessity or by the leave of his tutor, he shall be liable to admonition (or such punishment as the President shall think meet) if he offend above once a week." [^2]--> Numerous statutes read very much like an adapted list of the Ten Commandments. The Harvard Charter of 1650 made it the first and oldest corporation in the United States.

The court followed an English pattern by locating the college in the country, designating "Newtowne" as the site of the college. Because many of those most eager to have the college, including John Harvard, had been at Cambridge University in England, the name of Newtowne was soon changed to Cambridge.

Harvard has long been the standard-bearer of American higher education. Essentially every institution in America, including BYU, is a product of the pioneering legacy of Harvard. While it has its weaknesses, our being here in this symposium witnesses that it also has much appeal. Harvard is not a Puritan or even a Massachusetts institution; rather, it has long been the flagship of American higher education.

Among Harvard's many contributions are the educational, statesmen that have guided it and shaped the destiny of higher learning in our society. Probably the single most influential leader in all American higher education was Charles W. Eliot, who became president of Harvard in 1869. Since then, Harvard has had only five presidents. By comparison, during the same period the Church has had eleven presidents.

Perhaps the most renowned years in Harvard's history, evident from the names of buildings and streets around the Yard, were the mid-1800s which were influenced by such notables as Henry W. Longfellow, Ralph Waldo Emerson, Henry David Thoreau, and Nathaniel Hawthome. Some of their convictions parallel Mormon attitudes. I have come to believe that these men were included in the "winds of the restoration." All of them except Thoreau were born within five years of the Prophet Joseph, and each of them made important and lasting contributions that are encompassed in the teachings of the gospel. Hawthorne's classic, The Scarlet Letter, is perhaps the greatest inspiration for repentance outside the Lord's own teachings. Dr. Pullias, noted professor at the University of Southern California, advocates its annual reading as a reminder of the need to keep our appetites and lives within the bounds of the New Testament. Longfellow, "the children's poet," had the inspiration for "The Family Hour" at an early date, and through it and other family-centered poems he cinched the ties of family ideals. Emerson promoted independent search for truth, recognizing the limitations imposed by the religions of the apostasy. Thoreau strove for the harmony of the soul and nature, and abhorred the hypocrisy of a man-made world with all its superficiality. These men were close friends and regularly met to discuss the important matters of life. Except for Longfellow, they are even buried together on Author' s Ridge of the tranquil Sleepy Hollow Cemetery in their beloved Concord, Massachusetts. Their mutual interests spawned transcendentalism, sometimes by our own people called "early Mormonism."

These strong religious beginnings seem ironic in the face of the unfortunate, albeit popular, image of a "godless Harvard," a title that arose after 1886 when the Governing Boards abolished a 250 year-old rule of compulsory attendance at daily chapel. Addressing this image as recently as November 1984, Peter Comes, Minister to the University, announced in the Harvard Independent, that,

Over the course of the past 15 years, Harvard's religious institutions have prospered. We have witnessed the establishment of a new undergraduate honors concentration in the comparative study of religion; a re-orientation of the professional curriculum in the Divinity school...; the revitalization of student denominational fellowships and associations. To those who have some knowledge of Harvard's 17th-century Christian inheritance, the renewal of religious inquiry in the midst of a community of intellectual vigor and civic purpose should come as no surprise. [^3]-->

More could be said of Harvard's influence on the Restoration and the influence of religion at Harvard. Of more immediate concern, however, are the experiences involving Church members who have elected to attend Harvard and Harvard's direct dealings with Church members and Church-related materials. Time does not permit, nor do I have the information to extensively treat the many members who have come to Harvard and describe its influence in their lives and thereby on the Church. But I would like to share a few experiences.

My favorite is an account of a group of students who had just graduated from the Brigham Young College in Logan, Utah in June 1891. On 13 June, they departed for Cambridge to attend Harvard with Dr. J. M. Tanner as spiritual mentor and landlord. Upon returning to Utah they stimulated the education of the state. This little flock worked out a cooperative arrangement, living together for a total of three years at 421 Cambridge Street, 46 Russell Street, and 222 Prospect Street respectively. Most of them were from Cache Valley and all of them achieved considerable distinction following their training at Harvard.

Most notable among the group was a lad of 19 years named John Andreas Widtsoe. Widtsoe graduated from Harvard in the class of 1894 and returned to Utah to make a considerable contribution to higher education, including presiding over all three of its institutions of higher learning at various times. [^4]--> Some experiences and comments recorded of his Harvard years follow:

Class work had scarcely begun when I was stricken by a terrific attack of homesickness-my first and last attack. The sun was wiped out of the heavens; blackness filled my heart; pain raged through my mind and body. The only apparent remedy was to return home, to live the quiet life with mother and family. It required two months to effect a cure, due no doubt to a growing and enthusiastic interest in the work at hand. But ever since, I have had an understanding sympathy for student or missionary suffering from this real malady. I suspect that the cause of this attack was a hidden fear of my ability to hold my own in this great institution and in competition with students prepared in the famous schools of the land. Such fear, if it existed, vanished after the first examinations. I travelled usually far ahead of most of my classmates in scholastic grades. As the days went on, the doors of knowledge swung open, more and more. Vast vistas of truth appeared. A desire to pursue every known subject possessed me. [^5]-->

Learning was easy for me. Therefore, though I carried a full load of classes, as many as were permitted, I was listener in one or two courses each year. Three of those stand out. One year with the famous and love-able William James convinced me, since the professor had a stomach ulcer that year, that he lived in a dark and uninviting universe. A course with the great Josiah Royce failed to convince me that all things had existence only in the human mind. [^6]-->

At that time I was having my religious battles. Was Mormonism what it pretended to be? Did Joseph Smith tell the truth? I read, listened, compared, thought, prayed. It was a real search for truth. Out of it in time came the certain knowledge that the restored gospel is true and that Joseph Smith was indeed a Prophet, and restorer of the simple true gospel of Jesus Christ. There has never been any doubt about it since that time of deep study and prayer. I must confess that in finding my way to spiritual truth, Dr. Cooke's steady certainty of the pre-eminence of religion was a great help. Remember, in that day materialism was the order of the day,, and permeated every classroom. I owe much to Josiah Parsons Cooke. [^7]-->

Nevertheless there were other sweets in college. English was always a favorite subject. In it I did well. I wrote for our College newspaper, the Harvard Crimson, and our magazine, the Harvard Advocate. One of my stories was selected by the Boston Herald in the special graduation issue, as a representative of the literary efforts of the class. When I graduated, I had the chance to enter the editorial employ of the Youth's Companion, in that day the foremost magazine for youth. [^8]-->

Of course there were times when I met defeat. One day, walking down Cambridge Street, a dwarfish figure wearing a battered straw hat passed me. "What could a figure like that do near the university campus?" I thought. So, having passed the somewhat ungainly dwarf, I turned to get a better look at him. To my surprise he had also turned and stared at me. He probably thought he saw the greenest of Harvard freshmen.

When the autumn term opened I selected among my listing courses the first course in philosophy given by the famous Professor Josiah Royce.

At the appointed hour I was in the classroom, crowded with students eager to see and hear the great man. At last the door opened and in stepped the lecturer. I could not believe my eyes-it was the "dwarf" I had met on Cambridge Street. [^9]-->

I met President Charles W. Eliot three times in his home. I have not with the changing years [58 years later] changed my mind about him. In my generation he was easily the foremost citizen of America. Such men as he have the power to shape the world, and always for good. [^10]-->

So, the last examinations came; then graduation day, June 27, 1894, with its cap and gown parade, its lunches, and dinners, its talks and orations, its lightheartedness, and at last the gathering in Sanders Theatre where President Eliot conferred degrees upon us all. The diplomas were handed to us in a bunch, each man to scramble for his own.

It was difficult to believe, but it was printed in clear type in the program, that I was one of the small handful who graduated summa cum laude, that is, with highest honors. [^11]-->

He completed the work in three years and received the high honor, summa cum laude. This honor may be won in two ways; first, by examination showing excellence and wide acquaintance in one line of study; or second, by uniform excellence in all subjects taken. The highest honors may be won in both ways at the same time. Both of these were won by John A. Widtsoe. [^12]-->

Harvard was good to me. It was literally my alma mater. I have tried in my life to be worthy of that which I received from this great university. [^13]-->

There were with Widtsoe at least sixteen other LDS students at Cambridge. George Thomas, one of them, replaced Widtsoe as president of the University of Utah when Widtsoe was called into the Apostleship. Thomas served for 30 years in this capacity. Following the Widtsoe years, other western Mormons made their way to Harvard. One of note was Levi Edgar Young, who having graduated from the University of Utah on his way to becoming a history scholar, enrolled for graduate study at Harvard, finishing in 1902. Elder G. Homer Durham reported Brother Young's experience:

In 1898 he entered Harvard University, and there followed a glorious season of study under three of the titans of American historical scholarship: Albert Bushnell Hart, Edward Channing, and Ephraim Emerton. Also at Harvard, the young Latter-day Saint scholar came under the stimulating influence of one of the all-time American academic great men, Professor William James. James paid the Utahn the singular honor of inviting him to his seminar, to address the group on Mormonism. The great psychologist introduced him as "a friend" and as a "close relative of Brigham Young." Following a forty-minute presentation, Professor James and the Harvard graduate students kept the young man busy for nearly two hours, answering questions. [^14]-->

Of the same experience, Truman G. Madsen reported that Professor James arose and said, "I thank Mr. Young for his contribution. It is always interesting to get new viewpoints on religion and philosophy. This, Mr. Young has certainly given us. Gentlemen of the class, let us stand and thank Mr. Young." [^15]--> Following this initial meeting, Brother Young visited often with the famed professor. Some have alleged that Brother Young taught James all the Mormonism he ever knew.

Between 1910 and 1914 a remarkable story of Harvard and Mormonism developed. [^16]--> Mrs. Alice Forbes Perkins Hooper, daughter of railroad magnate, Charles Elliot Perkins, was raised on the bluffs above the Mississippi River in Burlington, Iowa. She developed a great love for the West, which continued even though she associated with the elite in Boston. On reading of the Harvard appointment of Professor Frederick Jackson Turner, noted Western historian, she determined to do something as a fitting tribute to the name of her father. A close friend, Edgar H. Wells, secretary of the Alumni Association at Harvard casually mentioned the idea of an annual gift for the purchase of books and manuscripts on Western history in support of Dr. Turner's work. Mrs. Hooper liked the suggestion but would not commit to it without consultation with Turner. Over lunch with the professor, she decided to proceed and soon sent a letter to the president and fellows of Harvard announcing her gift of $1,000 per year.

Efforts to collect materials led to the formation of the Harvard Commission on Western History on 12 February 1912. Its illustrious members had the task of attracting much interest and significant contributions. The stage was set for the commission to purchase its first and largest collection. Before the commission was formed, the Shepard Book Company of Salt Lake City had advertised the sale of a collection of 1,400 volumes on Mormonism at a cost of $4,500. Turner was determined to obtain these volumes and felt sure Mrs. Hooper could be persuaded to give the sum. He accordingly wrote her, including this impressive comment in his appeal:

Mormonism touches not only Utah but the characteristics of a vast area about Utah where Mormons are living, and it touches some pretty important matters of national legislation as well-so such a library has value beyond local history. Perhaps in the long run its greatest value will lie, however, in the field of the history of religion. Here was a native growth under the eyes of the American of the nineteenth century, of a religion that colonized an area equal to that of a great European nation, and built up an industrial empire at the same time. [^17]-->

Mrs. Hooper was not sufficiently impressed, and the matter was allowed to drop. Three years later, the assistant librarian of Harvard heard of a collection of 2,500 titles on Mormonism for sale by the Torch Press Book Shop of Iowa. Newspaper accounts of the time labeled it "the best collection of its kind in the west." [^18]--> Mrs. Hooper's project was now more of a passion with her, although the matter of the Mormon collection had failed to increase her interest to any significant degree. Preliminary discussions began over the new collection. David Heald, of the library staff, raised the possibility that this might be the same collection they had explored in 1911. If they could go right to the seller, they could avoid commissions and acquire the collection at a discount. His hunch turned out to be correct, and Roger Pierce, secretary of the Alumni Association, was sent to Salt Lake City to purchase the collection at the lowest possible price.

The collection had been compiled by Eli H. Peirce (no relation to Roger), an insurance agent of modest means, residing at 161 C Street in Salt Lake City. Roger and Eli reached an agreement and a contract with a thirty-day option was drawn up. Lewis Ball McCornick, Harvard '02, and vice-president of the banking house of McCornick and Company of Salt Lake City, was asked to oversee the details of the purchase, upon Pierce's return to Cambridge. The alumni secretary was expected to raise the money from local alumni, which consisted of a couple of impoverished professors and Mr. McCornick. McComick manifested a willingness to help, but his father did not. Unfortunately, his father controlled the family fortune.

The alumni route having failed, Roger turned next to Harvard connections. William G. Sharp, president of U .S . Smelting, Refining, and Mining Co. of Salt Lake City, had J. J. Storrow, Harvard '85 on his Board, who thus became the chief target. Storrow's best efforts as a loyal Harvard man also failed. Turner turned next to the distinguished former president, Charles W. Eliot, who was only able to help with encouragement. He made this significant comment: "The collection could be preserved against a day when an impartial historian would tell the true story of a church remarkable for its extraordinary merits as a colonizer." [^19] Ray Allen Billington, noted historian of the Huntington Library in California, called Eliot' s letter "an unusual document for a day when bigotry still reigned." [^20]-->

When hopes of finding a donor were exhausted, Mrs. Hooper, "having slept on the Mormons for some time, announced to Turner that she had decided to live up to one of her favorite slogans-do good and throw it into the sea, the fishes won't know, God will-and squander a bit more of her father's patrimony in his memory." [^21]--> She provided the necessary funds for the purchase.

When word leaked out of the purchase, the Salt Lake Herald reported that the Church strongly disapproved of the sale and had agreed to purchase 500 of the most outspoken anti-Mormon works. [^22]--> In addition, the Shepard Book Company contacted Mr. Eli Peirce and demanded full commission. Eli' s neighbors and friends branded him a traitor to both Church and state, assuring him that he could continue in the insurance business in Salt Lake only if he managed to squirm out of the deal. Soon the University of Utah joined in the opposition and even enlisted the governor' s support. A "council of war" was summoned in the Alumni Office in University Hall on Sunday, 9 May, six days before the expiration of the agreement poor Eli now hoped Harvard could not keep. Before the day was over, Turner and friends dispatched Mr. Heald on the train west with instructions to close the deal and see the shipment across state lines and out of the threat of interference from either Church officials or others opposed to the purchase. Eli refused to settle unless the commission issue with Shepard Book Company, who had insisted on payment of $500, was resolved. Heald tried to shift the burden to Eli, who would have happily let the deal fail, but who nonetheless agreed to pay much of the commission.

With the transaction completed, Eli, Shephard, Heald, McCornick's chauffeur, and four other men spent two days until 10:30 p.m. boxing the books for shipment. One more day was required to load the more than 2 1/2 tons of books onto the freight car. Mr. Heald left with McCornick a draft made out to Eli Peirce and his wife. But when Heald arrived to board his train for Boston, Eli was there to meet him. He pled that Heald would tear up the draft and issue a new one without Mrs. Peirce's name. She had refused to endorse it without the guarantee that half the proceeds were hers. Heald could not comply for the banks were closed. But eventually a draft was sent to a Salt Lake Bank against the day when Eli and his wife should settle their marital difficulties.

Turner and the others celebrated in an extravagant manner at the Harvard Club of Boston on the evening of May 18. Turner rejoiced over the rich sources on "how a religion can be originated and how it can grow; how a great migration could create a new colony in a region equal to that of European states; and how that society expressed itself in literature, education, etc." [^23] The Harvard Alumni Bulletin reported the good news, boasting of "fifty-seven volumes of collected pamphlets, the sizeable newspaper and magazine files, the numerous first editions of the Book of Mormon and Doctrine and Covenants, the publications of such dissenting factions as the J. J. Strang group, and the hundreds of books by Utah authors." [^24]-->

Turning from this collection to other Mormons educated at Harvard, we encounter another Widtsoe. Osborne Widtsoe received his M.S. from Harvard in 1905 and later distinguished himself in the English Department of the University of Utah and on the editorial boards of Church magazines.

Because of the number of students in the area, by 1908 the first official branch of the Church was organized in the Cambridge/Boston area. Missionary elders presided over the branch until 1915. Between 1915 and 1938 there were nine branch presidents, most of whom were students. During this time it was reported that "the branch had a few local members. The small student group consisted of several who were married and had children and a number of unmarried students, attending either the Business School or Medical School." [^25]-->

Around 1935, Ruth and George Albert Smith, Jr., a notable young couple from Salt Lake City, arrived in Cambridge. They began to fit into the faculty of the Harvard Business School. Brother Smith descended from one of the most prestigious Mormon families. His father, then in the Quorum of the Twelve, eventually became President of the Church. Albert (as he was called) became very prominent "across the river," and in time it was expected that he would reside in the prestigious office of Dean of the Harvard Business School. The Smiths were a stabilizing influence in the struggling branch, and Albert's ties to the headquarters of the Church hastened approvals for organizing a mission and obtaining a beautiful meetinghouse. For most of his years in Cambridge, Brother Smith taught in the adult Sunday School classes. He was a particularly stimulating teacher to the students, and some have since said that weekday institute classes could never achieve the quality of the religious instruction of Brother Smith.

My father had known George Albert, Jr., while serving a mission in the Boston area in the late 1930s and often spoke highly of him. In 1968 my bride and I spent half a year in Boston. I was compelled to make his acquaintance. Accordingly, I spent a very pleasant afternoon in his office. I was impressed with the many autographed pictures of Church leaders hanging about the room, the most prominent among them being that of President David O. McKay. I was equally alarmed by some telltale signs that indicated he was not abiding in the orthodoxy his church experience would suggest. I asked him why such distance had grown between him and the faithful legacy he had inherited. I wanted to remind him of the vision his father had, warning him, the third to carry the name, to carefully protect its faithfulness. His response was, "My doctor told me I had to give something up. I chose the Church. Oh, I'm there most of the time, and I still believe it, but I don't give it much of my energy anymore." No doubt the career pressures of Harvard, especially the lure of the deanship, took their heavy toll. Some have thought it was the frustrated expectation of being called to follow in the ecclesiastical footsteps of his fathers. Many prominent men of the Church today have known Albert Smith both as a teacher in the Church and as a professor in the "B" school, among them, Henry B. Eyring, Robert D. Hales, Steven R. Covey, and Rodney H. Brady.

The 1950s marked an upsurge of Mormon attendance at Harvard. In the undergraduate college there were about five new freshmen each year in addition to a sizable contingent of graduate and professional school students. Besides those already mentioned who knew Brother Smith, also included in this era were such notables as Richard L. Anderson, Truman G. Madsen, and Chase Peterson. Among these men were a Church Commissioner of Education and two of the four university presidents of the state of Utah. The other two university presidents graduated from Yale and Stanford, a fitting distribution, all things considered.

In my six years in the Yard (Harvard Yard), I was frequently asked about Dr. Chase Peterson, a very visible Mormon at Harvard. His is another interesting story. Perhaps it began with his father, Dr. E.G. Peterson, president of Utah State Agricultural College for 29 years. E.G. carried on a long-time correspondence with President Eliot, who in one letter, as reported by Sister Peterson,

told of the time he was in Utah and attended a very large Mormon gathering where a "vigorous young man with barrel-sized voice" sang "Hard times, hard times, come again no more." We figured out it must have been Melvin J. Ballard. President Eliot said he would like to know more about the Mormon religion, commenting further that what the world needs is a religion that meets people in their simple needs and is lofty enough for other minds to reach for. [^26]

Chase Peterson was born and raised in the president's home on the Logan campus and was probably fed Harvard at every meal. When the time came, he attended both Harvard College and Medical School. Following a few years of private medical practice in Salt Lake City, he was asked by Harvard to return as dean of admissions. Having loved his time in Cambridge, he was unable to turn down the invitation. For many years he and his wife Grethe and their children lived in the house formerly occupied by William James. Eventually Peterson became vice-president of the university, from which he resigned to take a similar post at the University of Utah in 1978. Harvard runs deep in the blood of the Peter-son family. I have had the privilege of having two of his three children and several nieces and nephews in my classes while at Harvard. Erika, his eldest, was selected amid rigid competition to be a speaker at her commencement. Stewart, the second child, was admitted to Harvard following his mission, and Edward, their third child, just completed his freshman year and is taking time off to serve a mission.

Richard Bushman has a similar story. Following a year or two at Harvard, he was called to serve in the New England States Mission. Most of his time was spent in the mission office located in the Cambridge chapel, just two blocks from Harvard Yard. Following his release he continued at Harvard and met his wife Claudia, a student at Wellesley. After trying life in other places, including Utah Valley, they elected to settle near Cambridge. I have known three of the Bushman children in my years at Harvard. Brother Bushman served as bishop and stake president in the Boston Stake, and Brother Chase Peterson served under President Bushman as bishop of the University Ward.

Imagine being in Sunday School class with Richard Anderson, Truman Madsen, Henry Eyring, Steven Covey, Rodney Brady, Carlfred Broderick, Robert D. Hales, and perhaps a dozen more like them, many with equally outstanding wives. Now imagine George Albert Smith, Jr., as the teacher, and each person's life filled with the invigoration of tough academic work under the tutelage of renowned professors. Such company has made Church activity popular among Harvard Saints.

Some time ago I asked Truman Madsen to reminisce about his Cambridge days. He and Ann made a list of some 40 student friends. They have had subsequent experience with most of them. When I asked of the Church experience and activity of these students, Truman replied that to his knowledge, about 80 percent had been either a stake or mission president, or both.

I once toured Cambridge with Brother Henry Eyring. We first stopped at the Cambridge chapel, where he enthusiastically told me of meeting his bride and the assurance he had had of their future. "Sit right here," he said. He then went to the stand and sat in one of the padded seats. "I was right here and she sat right where you are. I nudged President Cox (who had made Henry's singleness a matter of considerable personal interest). 'See that girl sitting right there?' He nodded. 'She is the girl I am going to marry."' She had come from Menlo Park, in part because of a friendship with Brent Lambert, another Harvard student and now president of the Massachusetts Hingham Stake. Brother Eyring kept directing me to stop at the several houses where he had lived. At each one he pulled out his sketch pad and sketched for his journal a picture of each domicile of his five years at the "B" School.

I was not surprised when Bishop Robert D. Hales chose Brother Eyring as his counselor in the Presiding Bishopric. In his remarks, Bishop Hales mentioned the experiences the two of them had shared as counselors to President Cox in Boston. "Bishop Eyring and I have known each other since boyhood. He is a man of God. Sitting in this audience today is Wilbur Cox. Both Bishop Eyring and I served as counselors to him in a stake presidency." [^27]-->

Similar stories could be told of almost all who have attended Harvard. Let me turn to current times. I came to Cambridge in 1978. In my experience each year we had an average of seven Mormon freshmen at Harvard, five at MIT, and several others from schools scattered across the northeast from New Hampshire to Rhode Island. Despite missions or other interruptions, almost all of them completed their degrees. Along with 20-30 undergraduates, we typically had eighteen in the business school, seven or eight in the law school, three or four in the medical school, a dozen or so spread across many graduate programs, and a handful of interns or residents.

I wish to share some experiences of my students here. From 1979 to my last year at Harvard, all but one of the men have gone on missions and most of the women have gone or are planning to. Two who were active on entering the university became inactive before graduation; one has since returned to activity. Many who described themselves upon entrance as inactive or marginal or headed in the wrong direction with the wrong company have filled missions and continued their activity. The most influential element in their Church experience at Harvard has been the sense of group that has existed among them. It has been sweet to watch them care for each other and share their uniqueness in very positive experiences. I have not found them to be elitist or insensitive to others, nor from prominent, wealthy families. When asked, "What is the difference between Harvard students and LDS students at other institutions?" I have consistently answered with one word: "Confidence."

Perhaps the best way to report the experiences of current Harvard Mormons is simply to use their own descriptions from interviews or articles appearing in The Harvard Crimson, The Harvard Independent, Ensign, and This People.

Karl Bushman, a junior, wrote of his experiences for the Harvard Independent, 24 November 1984.

"Are you a junior or a senior? .... I'm a second-semester junior." "You took a semester off, then? .... Three semesters, actually." "What did you do on your time off?. .... I was a missionary working with the Spanish-speaking people in Utah."

Just about every time I meet someone new I have this conversation within the first three minutes. I don't mind repeating it because my mission is such an important part of me. My impression is that many of my acquaintances think of me as the guy who was a Mormon missionary. That isn't the only thing I am but I suppose that it is a pretty unusual experience around here, even though all Mormons are encouraged to be missionaries.

Mormons arrive at Harvard College at the rate of four to eight each year. Thus we form a small and unified community. Twice-a-week classes at the LDS Institute provide contact with other Mormon students outside the context of Church on Sunday. This contact helped me a great deal during my freshman year when I and six other confused freshmen often met simply to share our experiences, good and bad, of trying to be a Mormon at Harvard.

But Harvard is not a place where one can hide in his other ethnic community, particularly with a ratio of seven to 1600. Although some of my closest friends are Church people, I move not only in Church circles but with people from choir, from the literature concentration, from my House, and from other social groups. In 1982 1 was the only Mormon to go on the Glee Club's Asian tour, but I'm certainly not the only Church member with varied interests, as my friends are involved in activities ranging from crew and basketball to journalism and drama.

One of my freshman roommates and I used to talk deep into the night about the nature of God and man, and how that should affect our actions. We asked whether life was a test, a pre-destined course, or something that happened to us by accident. We also discussed whether everyone could find their own way to God, or whether a church was necessary, and if so, which church. Neither of us converted the other-he was a Lutheran-but we strengthened our beliefs by defending them.

But the most important thing for me about being a Mormon is the understanding of gospel principles that it has brought me. My life becomes richer as I contemplate my relationship to my Father in Heaven, and I try to act as He would dictate. Little things like riding my bicycle into the morning sun and singing a sacred anthem mean much more to me than they otherwise would. Religious contemplation and action help me to see my many faults, and Rive me motivation and strength in trying to overcome my weaknesses. [^28]-->

The July 1984 Ensign magazine carried an article written by one of the Harvard students about the experiences shared by many of her classmates.

"I used to call home every week or so and logically explain that Harvard was a dangerous and heretical institution, that I was not ready, able or willing to attend it, and that a plane ticket to Utah would actually cost less than the phone bill I intended to rack up. My parents would simply tell me that I could make it." She smiles. "The worst of it was, they were fight."....

Another student remembers an evening, three years before, when he went as a home teacher to visit another freshman.

"It was the night before my last final, and I hadn't slept much for three days," he recalls. The press of study seemed urgent. "But this guy had been inactive for years, and that evening he really seemed to want me to come. He even called to make sure I'd be there. There must have been twenty people in his dorm room when I arrived. He had me sit down on the bed. He said, 'All these people want to ask you about Mormonism.' Then he gave me the kind of smile a thirteen-year-old uses when he's about to set fire to a cat.

"They questioned me for about half an hour-if you can call them questions. They were more like verbal smirks...

"So what did you do?" someone asks.

"The only thing I could. I told him that the LDS Church is different from all other American religions for the simple reason that God appeared to Joseph Smith and directed him to organize it. I told him that Joseph really was a prophet, that the Book of Mormon is true-actually, I don't remember everything I said. I was exhausted, I was confused, and I was angry."

He looks into the fireplace, thinking. "As I talked, though, a real change came over the room. All those smirks sort of wilted away, until everyone was watching me in total silence. The divinity student looked stunned. I don't think he'd ever considered that I might actually believe my religion was the truth. I remember someone telling me later that they'd never felt anything like that in a campus dorm."

"What really amazes me is how consistently LDS students perform well in academics, without spending Sundays in the library. I think that getting away from school, being with friends, and getting an eternal perspective all keep me refreshed and ready to study harder at other times. Church helps me stay sane. [^29]-->

This People Magazine also interviewed several LDS students in various Ivy League schools. Some observations, mostly from Harvard students, were reported in the October 1984 issue as follows:

"One of my freshman-year roommates owns elephants," a student says. "He's from India, and I think he met every Latter-day Saint in the university through me; some of them became his close friends. I have a feeling that someday his exposure to the Church will bear important fruit in India. I'm glad that he got a positive introduction to the Church."

A graduate student... recounts a conversation with his financial aid counselor:

"He said, 'You Mormons are such anomalies. I've adjusted several scholarships for people on missions. Then we've had some get married-as undergraduates! One student actually became a father this year, and we had to adjust his aid for that. You're a very unusual group.'"

"I'm stronger now," he says. "I've come to appreciate my education, my associates, myself, and most of all, the gospel. I feel that my experiences here at college are helping me do my part to build up the kingdom of God on earth."

With few exceptions these bright, confident LDS Ivy Leaguers admit they were academically intimidated as freshmen, and describe themselves after one full semester as feeling such things as panic, terror, and abject humility. Russ Hancock remembers, "Frankly, it was terrifying. It was the most humbling experience I've had in my whole life. My first midterm exam had a big F on it. I think they fail you on the first midterm just to humble you."

Joyce remembers her first semester. "When I took my first hourly exams [midterms] I was at the bottom, I mean the bottom, of the field. By the time I went home for Christmas I was strung out. I slept for about three days. But I studied for finals like crazy and pulled through my exams after about a week of solid study. It was hard. Never in high school had I faced complete failure.

"When I first came here I thought, 'Well, I'll just get Bs.' You can get As here. You just have to study really hard. A lot of times the grades you get here are in direct proportion to how much you study. The people I know who do really, really well here study really, really hard."

"I'm really proud of BYU and the things they do. I'm sad, though, if it keeps people from coming out here. I know people here who've been converted just because they met another Mormon. If all the Mormons stuck together and went to one school, it would be a tragedy. People should branch out."

All things considered, it seems clear that Ivy League schools are more than bastions for preppies and prima donnas. There's much more involved than penny loafers and Oxford shirts, including, for example, such intangibles as self-discipline, confidence, a healthy curiosity about the world, and certainly endurance. But for LDS Ivy Leaguers there's an additional essential ingredient. Perhaps it could be labeled as a sense of direction. These young men and women feel sense of purpose and adventure and challenge, all of which are best met or at least instigated in the rigorous climate of Ivy League schools. The environment is, as Joyce Gallagher summarizes, not for everyone. "But for those who've found their collegiate niche in the East, the experience is without peer." [^30]-->

An article in the Harvard Crimson, the official campus newspaper of 1 October 1982, entitled "Spreading the Faith: Harvard Mormons Take Time Off to Recruit," states:

For Beck, who grew up in Provo, Utah, the decision to undertake a mission was more complex. "Mormonism is a social thing in Utah; I went through the motions in high school, but it wasn't until I got to Harvard that I began to have real faith. I started to see a lot of logic in things that I had been doing without knowing why, like resting on Sundays and not smoking or drinking. In a way, I started proselytizing my roommates before I decided to do a mission."

However, reports from Harvard undergraduates indicate that missions are, in the words of Finlayson, "very positive, strengthening experiences ." Carter agrees, saying that after his mission he felt "more secure" about himself and about Harvard. These four reached highly similar conclusions in their definition of a successful mission. Carter, who fulfilled his mission in Bangkok and Campang, explains, "I would define a mission as successful if the missionary' s understanding and appreciation of other people grew and if he changed some lives for the better."

Before his mission in southern Italy and Sicily, Cameron Carson, '84, thought that success could be measured in numbers of converts; after several months he realized that personal growth was a more likely yardstick. Finlayson concurs: "Even if I hadn't taught anyone who convened, it would have been a success just in terms of the broadening of my own horizons." Reported conversion rates are impressive in any case: They range from 20 (Carmr) to 50 (Beck).

Differences among the returned missionaries' sentiments surfaced primarily in their attitudes toward Harvard. Finlayson, who grew up in a California town in which his was the only Mormon family, reports little difficulty in adjusting to Harvard life as a freshman. Beck, on the other hand, was shocked and disconcerted by his introduction to the college: on his first night in Cambridge he saw a man lying in the street "with blood spurting from both arms," and on his second night he chanced upon two male students kissing on the first floor of Weld. Carson, who grew up in Boise, Idaho, a city with a 20 to 25 percent Mormon population, also reports that he had difficulty learning to live in close proximity with people whose values and attitudes diverged so radically from his own.

As the missionaries differ in their initial reactions to Harvard, so do they report disparate experiences of their return to school after completing their missions. Finlayson found readjustment very difficult: his friends from freshman year had become seniors during his absence, and the need to concentrate on exclusively personal goals clashed with the missionary emphasis on outward-reaching aims. "Sometimes I find myself questioning the value of what I'm doing, here," he admits. Carter agrees: "The problems with getting used to Harvard again were partly not being a missionary anymore." Beck alone reports a smooth transition from missionary to student life. His roommates all took a year off, and he enjoys advanced standing while they do not, so he and his friends will graduate together.

For all the missionaries interviewed, the Harvard Mormon community is an important resource. The Institute of Religion holds twice weekly study sessions, at which religious questions and issues are raised and discussed. Although these sessions are held at 8:00 a.m., they are attended by nearly every Mormon who participates in Church services at least once monthly.

Finlayson chose to attend Harvard in part because its Mormon population is larger than those of the other Ivy League universities to which he applied. Beck reports that "the Harvard Mormons mean more and more to me all the time." His appreciation of the warmth and solace provided by his religion was heightened during this past summer, when he arrived in Tokyo, utterly without personal contacts, and the Japanese Mormon community found him an apartment and helped him to settle in.

Carter praises the Harvard Mormons, claiming that "they have big dreams and they're working to make them into something." Carson is in a unique position among the undergraduates interviewed. After completing his miss ion and then his sophomore year at Harvard, he married a Mormon from his hometown. In addition to his wife, his closest friends are Mormons.

Despite the differences in their attitudes toward Harvard, despite the disparities among their chosen fields of study, despite dissimilarities in their personalities, which are manifest even in interviews, the returned Mormon missionaries display one vital characteristic in common: their faith. John Beck summarized the situation neatly when he stated, "The most important thing, if you're going to do a mission, is to believe in it." And they do. [^31]-->

Mormonism and Harvard are both powerful symbols commanding much respect in our world. In the Book of Mormon Jacob aptly warns of the dangers of man' s wisdom, as represented by Harvard. He also spoke of the goodness of learning when properly interwoven with righteousness. As proponents of higher education, we should have a high appreciation for the history and excellence in academics represented by Harvard. The faculty of Religious Education at BYU strives to instill as high an appreciation for hearkening to the counsels of God. Mormonism at Harvard has been filled primarily by inspiring episodes. There has always been a strong propensity for upholding one's faith. Harvard Mormons have sought balance in the academic and the spiritual. For them Harvard has been a fruitful field for both faith and learning.

The Harvard Crimson, 1 October 1982, pp. 3-4
