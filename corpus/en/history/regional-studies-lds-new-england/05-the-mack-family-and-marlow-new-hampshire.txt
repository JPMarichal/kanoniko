Various authors, Regional Studies in LDS History: New England ( 1988)

The Mack Family and Marlow, New Hampshire

A. Gary Anderson

Although The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints usually looks to Vermont for its historical origins with the birth of Joseph Smith at Sharon, there is an interesting phase of history connected with his ancestors in neighboring New Hampshire. Solomon Mack, Joseph Smith's maternal grandfather and resident of Lyme, Connecticut, migrated to Marlow, New Hampshire, to become the first resident of the area in 1761.

This move was undoubtedly prompted by the excitement created by the granting of township charters in 1761 on both sides of the Connecticut River in what are now the states of New Hampshire and Vermont. Governor Benning Wentworth had granted 68 charters on 7 October 1761, one of which was Marlow, named after an old English town. The charters provided opportunity for inexpensive land. Most of the original grantees sold their rights to those who became actual settlers. How soon the grantees first visited the township is uncertain. Samuel Gustin, one of the grantees, had been in the area at least by the summer of 1764, and built a cabin. The township proprietors held several meetings in Lyme during the latter part of 1764 and decided to meet on 8 October 1765 in Marlow at Samuel Gustin's dwelling. [^1]-->

Samuel Gustin and Joseph Tubbs, another grantee, made an interesting discovery as they journeyed to Gustin's cabin in Marlow to keep the appointment:

On their way to his place these two men were given a great surprise, for just before they reached the Ashuelot, or to be exact, at the comer, of the village cemetery of the present time, they found a cabin. It proved to be that of Solomon Mack-a wanderer-having no home until he came to New Hampshire. In an autobiography he says after his marriage, in about 1761 he went into the back country to clear himself a farm. Being of Lyme he knew of the course of this grant to the men of that town, where better then could he locate? Mr. Hayward in his History of Gilsum, where Mack later lived, dying there in 1820 says, "Solomon Mack and his wife Lydia had five children born to them in Marlow." The earliest date given being 1764, but there was one, at least born before that. In early layouts mention is made of Mack's "old" house beside the path that leads to the Ashuelot. These facts make it evident that Solomon and Lydia were the pioneer settlers of Marlow. Can we of today imagine the hardihood, the courage, the desire for gain, that would prompt a young couple to establish a home alone in the solitude of such a wilderness with the nearest neighbor fifteen miles away? [^2]-->

Solomon Mack had the hearty, adventurous spirit to become the first resident and pioneer of Marlow. His own account reflects the challenge he and his family faced, "When we moved there it was no other than a desolate and dreary wilderness. Only four families resided within forty miles." [^3] Under these primitive circumstances the early training as a school teacher, Solomon's wife, Lydia Gates, had received became very useful. She educated her children in the home in the various branches of ordinary education and called them together night and morning to teach them to pray and urge them to develop other Christian virtues. Her abilities and devotion won the respect of her husband and inspired her children. [^4]-->

Other migrants from Lyme joined Mack in Marlow and organized a town in 1766. These early settlers were a close-knit group, many of whom were related by blood or marriage. The names of these early families were Beebe, Beckwith, Bingham, Canfield, Church, Gee, Gustin, Huntley, Lewis, Mack, Miller, Otis, Royce, Tubbs, and others. [^5] The wife of Solomon Mack's father, Ebinezer, was Hannah Huntley; his sister Hepsibah married Abisha Tubbs. [^6] Another of the pioneer women of Marlow was Lydia Mack Gustin, a cousin. [^7] Apparently Mack was well known by his relatives and became a respected figure among them. He was elected "dear reef' in the community on 12 May 1767. [^8]--> This office was equivalent to that of a game warden.

The Marlow town center originally developed in the Baker Comer and Marlow Hill area, which is about two miles from the present town center. Solomon Mack' s homesites were in close proximity to present-day Marlow. They were on the east and west ends of the cemetery:

That Solomon Mack was the first to build a home here we believe is established (by) the reference to "Mack's old house and the" path that heads to Solomon Mack's house from the town in 1768, confirm this house was located near the N. E. Cor. of the old part of the cemetery. This being his old house the question at once arises where was the new one. Our answer is the so-called Washburn House which stood at the west line of the "new" cemetery at the burial lot of Eben P. Knight where bricks from the chimney may now (be) plainly seen. [^9]-->

Although he seems reasonably certain, there seems to be some question in the mind of Elgin Jones, the compiler of the town history, as to whether Solomon Mack's home was the first home in Marlow. His further comments regarding the first proprietors' meeting were recorded as follows:

The next official act was the town meeting held at the house of Samuel Gustin Oct. 8, 2nd Tuesday 1765. This is interesting because it gives us positive knowledge that one house at least had been built in 1765 or before. More than this is conjecture. We may here ask was this home the first? In the layout of the Hyde Lot in July, 1768 mention is made of the path that goes past Solomon Mack's house, in other layouts mention is made of the path that goes by Mack' s old house. Solomon Mack was an early resident. Hayward in his history of Gilsum says that a daughter was born to Solomon Mack and wife Lydia in Marlow in 1764 also a son prior to this. The fact that Mr. Mack later lived in Gilsum as well as his descendants, all well informed and reliable people, leads us to believe that Solomon Mack was here a settler earlier than any other in 1763-64. An autobiography of Mack' s shows him to have had the wanderlust, but no direct mention is made of being in either of these towns. Incidentally we may note the probable location of this Mack old house was in the N. E. part of the village. These are the facts each may form their own conclusions. [^10]-->

Solomon Mack, according to the records, was deeded a total of 526 acres of land in Marlow. The first deed was recorded 15 May 1767 for 100 acres. [^11]

" After his graduation he returned to Marlow in whose welfare he was interested during his entire life. While in Marlow he engaged in various occupations, but among others, civil engineering which familiarized him with properties in his section of the country. He was employed, as a result of this knowledge, by large owners of real estate for whom he purchased large tracts of land in Marlow, Stoddard and other places. He also became an owner of a considerable acreage in Marlow in his own right.

"As a result of this study he acquired considerable knowledge in regard to the history of Marlow, and of the early settlers of that town. He, therefore, resolved to preserve that information and made intensive investigation into all matters pertaining to the early history of Marlow. It was his purpose to collect and classify this information and eventually to put it into the form of a History of Marlow.

"This publication, therefore, is not intended in any sense as a history but as an effort to preserve the information assembled by Mr. Jones." A second 100 acres was deeded to Mack on 1 October 1770. [^12] Another tract of land, adjacent to his old house consisting of 25 acres, was deeded to him 8 December 1770. [^13] The final plot of land deeded to him while he still resided in Marlow was a fifty acre plot of land on 27 May 1773. [^14]--> This was the same year in which he left Marlow and moved to Gilsum, New Hampshire.

Why Solomon Mack purchased another 250 acres of land in Marlow after he had moved to Gilsum is not apparent from the records, but this transaction occurred on 2 January 1781. [^15] County land records also show the sale of 50 acres of Solomon Mack's land which had apparently been cleared, as the deed indicated the labor was done. This transaction occurred on 24 March 1773, before Mack left Marlow. [^16] The only other land transaction involving Mack was the sale of one-half the original tract of land, owned by Jonathan Alger to Abisha Tubbs, his brother-in-law, for five pounds in February 1894. The exact acreage is not identified in the deed. [^17]-->

The early history of Marlow is replete with references to the Mack name. A Prominent hill in the vicinity of Solomon Mack's property is still referred to as Mack Mountain. The first sawmill was called the Mack Mill, as the site is still known to Marlow residents:

Just the date when the first saw mill was built is uncertain. In 1767, action as taken in a prop' s. meeting for providing a grist mill. In 1768, in the layout of the first road through town from Burkes comer to Stoddard mention is made of a saw mill later known as "Mack Mill." We believe this to have been the site of the first mill in town and that it was built by Elisha Royce. The layout of the lot on which the dam and pond would occupy is not recorded but the lot adjoining it is mentioned as Royce's Saw Mill lot in 1769, though no record of the latter is found. Elisha Royce convey. s to Samuel in 1778. In 1779 Samuel conveys this property to Silas Mack. [^18]-->

This account does not indicate why this mill site came to bear the Mack name, although it seems reasonable to assume that after 1779 it was identified with Silas Mack, who remained in Marlow until his death in 1836.

The Mack Mill was the scene of some notable incidents, a few of a very tragic nature. Fires seemed to be the greatest hazard to the early settlers in this area. The Mack Sawmill was burned the day before the election of 1816. [^19] Two Canfield girls died in a fire in 1789. The local Baptist minister, Elder Beckwith, preached the funeral sermon. His voice at the funeral, strangely enough, was heard a mile away at a home near Mack' s Mill by Aunt Nabby Mack in another unusual happening involving the Mack name. [^20] Another tragedy occurred at the Mack Mill Pond in 1819 when Erastus May drowned while bathing. [^21]-->

Elisha Mack, a brother to Solomon, was also a well-known figure in early Marlow. He had much to do with another early mill located on Gee Brook:

On Gee Brook we find again in layout but no record of its layout nor have we the exact date when this mill was erected on this we are certain a recorded deed dated 1774. Elisha Mack conveys to Stephen Gee 3/4 of the mil lot of 250 acres upon which the corn and saw mill and all ye grist or com mill on said 250 acres lot. In 1791 Nathan Huntley conveys by deed to Stephen Gee 1/4 of 25 acres on which a corn and saw mill stands together with 1/4 of the saw mill. Elisha Mack was a mill wright and bridge builder having later on a principal part in building the first bridge across the Connecticut at Bellows Falls and still later on the dams at Holyoke. The first record of his having been in Marlow permanently is 1770 we have reason for assuming that his mill was erected about the time and possibly by him. The mill is still standing in good condition. The old saw was removed by Byron Nichols about 1815. The grist mill was a separate building standing nearly where present house is, the old house being on the south side of the road. Mr. Gee was a clothier by trade and soon built a fulling mill which cease to use at his death, the grist mill was not used after 1845 or thereabouts. The saw mill as owned and operated successfully by Elisha, Horace and George F. Gee until 1908, 134 years in one family the second in town consecutive ownership. [^22]-->

Two other early residents of Marlow bearing the Mack name are also referred to in the history. They are Ebenezer Mack and his son, Silas. Silas evidently arrived in Marlow before his father had become a permanent resident. He lived in Marlow until his death in 1836 at age 84. He was buried in the old cemetery, where his tombstone can still be seen. His father lived in Marlow for a time and then left, perhaps returning to Lyme, Connecticut. The genealogical chart, See Appendix A) indicates the relationship of these men to Solomon Mack. [^23]-->

There are four Mack names in a list of families from Marlow made on 7 January 1772, viz., Elisha Mack, Solomon Mack, John Mack, and Zopher Mack. [^24]--> The Solomon Mack family moved to Gilsum the following year, where Solomon Mack, Jr., was born 28 January 1773.

A call for men to help in the Revolutionary War at Ticonderoga reached Marlow in May and June of 1777. The Mack family participated in the war. John, Zopher, and Silas Mack were gone but eight days. A more earnest call came in July of the same year as Burgoyne advanced rapidly towards Bennington, Vermont. Silas Mack again responded and fought in General Stark's army in the battle of 16 August 1777. Elisha Mack of Gilsum, formerly of Marlow, was the captain of the company of Gilsum and Marlow men. An interesting account of this battle was related by David Mack, son of Silas Mack, to the author of the Marlow History:

Tradition has it that William Lewis when cleaning his farm on west side of Huntley Mt. plainly heard the cannonading at the battle .... While the battle was in progress the people of Bennington were busily engaged in cooking for the soldiers everybody did this. After the fight as the men returned to town orders were given to go into any house they wished for food, all was free. Buckets of W. I. Rum with tin dippers were provided and passed among the soldiers free as water, the only charge being don't get drunk. [^25]-->

A Revolutionary War statue in the town square in Marlow bears the names of these patriots, including the three Macks.

Silas Mack was chosen a member of the school board in Marlow in 1792. [^26] He was also an active member of the Baptist Church. It was on his land that the first meetinghouse in Marlow was constructed about 1780. [^27]--> Another reference to the Macks in Marlow had to do with the town's ecclesiastical history:

The beginnings of the ecclesiastical history in Marlow was entirely different from that in all adjoining towns where Congregational Churches [prevailed]. "The standing order were first to organize. The reason for this is not difficult to find-the first settlers as we have noted were from Connecticut, and kinship of the first families was close. The Mack being one to which many were allied. The immediate ancestor of the family was Ebenezer Mack junior, a brother of Solomon the first of the name in town. The Ebenezer as a Baptist minister. The Connecticut settlers were also Baptist. Lydia Gustin wife John perhaps the first woman in town was a daughter of this Ebenezer. Tradition has it that the father of Samuel and John Gustin was a Baptist minister with these facts in view it would inadvertently follow naturally that the first church organization would be of this faith. We may reasonably believe that this Ebenezer was a visitor in town before he become a land owner in 1774. [^28]-->

It was 1777 when the first Baptist meetings were held. Although Caleb Blood was the first Baptist minister in Marlow, there is evidence that Ebenezer Mack officiated in ministerial duties in the Baptist Church. He may have done so during an interim when they were looking for a new minister. [^29] An Elder Beckwith succeeded Blood as the Baptist minister and remained there for many years. A humorous incident involving one of the Macks was recorded on one of the old manuscripts. "Elder Beckwith told Lucy Mack by name in his meeting if she could not stop laughing she had better go home. (She left; afterward she married the Elder's son.)" [^30]-->

The Mack name was prominent not only in the beginnings of the Baptist Church in Marlow, but also in the Christian Church. [James O. Kelley is purported to be the founder of the denomination. He had been associated with Bishop Ash-bury in the Methodist movement since 1774, but separated himself from this group when he disagreed doctrinally with Ashbury in 1792. Kelley then led in the organization of the Christian Church, although Abner Jones established the first Church organization in New England using the name Christian. It was Abner Jones who established many churches of this denomination in Vermont and New Hampshire. [^31]-->

The Christian Church was organized in Marlow on 1 June 1827 at the home of Silas Mack. [^32] William and Betsy Mack are on the original list of 32 members in 1827. Silas Mack is not listed as an original member, although the meeting was held in his home. There is no further mention of his involvement with this denomination in the history. William Mack was chosen to serve as church clerk and also as bishop. He served for nine years until 1836, when it was said of him, "Mr. Mack was a faithful man scarcely ever failing to be at the conference meetings, a record of each being in his handwriting." [^33]-->

In summation, it is evident from the early records of Marlow that the Macks were well-known, respected, and reliable citizens. They were involved as pioneers, in industry, in the military, and in town government, and were active church members. The Mack name in Marlow enhanced the image of Joseph Smith' s ancestral heritage and left a permanent impact upon the community. The story of the Macks in New Hampshire constitutes a significant and little-known chapter in the history of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints in New England.
