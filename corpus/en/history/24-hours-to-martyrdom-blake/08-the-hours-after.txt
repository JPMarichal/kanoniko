Reed H. Blake, Twenty-four Hours to Martyrdom ( 1973)

The Hours After

From Carthage, Richards and Taylor sent the first message of the martyrdom to Nauvoo. It read:

145"Carthage jail, 8:05 o'clock p.m., June 27th, 1844.

145"Joseph and Hyrum are dead. Taylor wounded, not very badly. I am well. Our guard was forced, as we believe, by a band of Missourians from 100 to 200. The job was done in an instant, and the party fled towards Nauvoo instantly. This is as I believe it. The citizens here are afraid of the Mormons attacking them. I promise them no!

"W. Richards. John Taylor

145"N.B.-The citizens promise us protection. Alarm guns have been fired."

145The letter was addressed to Governor Ford, General Dunham, Colonel Markham, and Emma Smith, at Nauvoo. It was at Taylor's request that Richards wrote, "not very badly." Despite the fact that for several days it was not known whether he would live or die, Taylor did not want to alarm his family, and when given the note to sign, did so "as quickly as I could, lest the tremor of my hand be noticed."

145The note was given to two mobocrats, William and John Barnes; but they were afraid to go to Nauvoo and instead carried it to Arza Adams at his home two-and-a-half miles north of Carthage. He was afraid to go on the main road, and after two more hours Benjamin Leyland consented to pilot him by a blind road. Starting about midnight, they arrived in Nauvoo at sunrise, only to find that the news had preceded them.

146A few miles out on the prairie from Nauvoo the forces of Governor Ford met the Mormon George D. Grant and the constable David Bettisworth hurrying to the Mormons with the report. The Governor, fearing immediate retaliation, took the two into custody. In Carthage, Ford stopped only long enough to denounce the people and gather the county records-then he hastened on to Augusta that night and Quincy the next day.

146As soon as released, Grant rode again to Nauvoo. He now carried a second message dispatched, "12 o'clock at night, 27th of June, Carthage, Hamilton's Tavern," and addressed to "Mrs. Emma Smith and Major-General Dunham, etc." It advised the people to keep the peace. It was signed by Richards, Taylor, Samuel Smith (who had gained the town sometime between six and seven that evening), and by Governor Ford and General Miner R. Deming.

146Grant was the first to reach Nauvoo with official news.

146Of the following day, Thomas Gregg, the anti-Mormon editor of the Warsaw Message, wrote:

146"The sun rose on as strange a scene as the broad Hancock prairie had ever witnessed. At the three corners of a triangle eighteen miles asunder, stood a smitten city (Nauvoo) and two almost deserted villages (Warsaw and Carthage), and here and there a group of questioning men, anxious to hear the news of the night. Toward the two villages the more courageous ones were returning to find their several abodes unsacked and untouched. The wet and heavy roads leading to the county seat from the South and East were being traversed by the refugees of the night, now returning and wondering that they had homes to return to. All knew that a great crime had been committed, by whom they knew not; and they knew not how, upon whom, where, or in what manner retribution might fall."

147Wrote the Mormon historian B. H. Roberts:

147"The marvel of that morning was-as it has been the marvel ever since, and will be in all generations to come-that the 'Mormons' at Nauvoo did not wreak vengeance upon the inhabitants of Carthage and WarsawÂ�for the murder of their Prophet and Patriarch. The means of vengeance at their command was ample. There was in Nauvoo a body of the best trained militia in the state of Illinois or in the United StatesÂ�who could have swept Hancock County as with a besom of destruction. This, to their everlasting credit, they did not do; but were true to their pledged word to Governor Ford, and to the instructions of their Prophet."

147In the city of Quincy, the ringing of bells at daylight announced a public meeting. On hand was a committee from Warsaw who informed the assemblage that a party of Mormons had attempted to rescue the Smiths out of jail; that a party of Missourians and others had killed them all; that on hearing the news, the Nauvoo Legion had attacked the Governor and his party in the Mormon city; that the Governor was now lodged in a house and under siege by the Legion; that Ford had sent word he could maintain his position for two days.

147By 10 A.M. from two to three hundred men were under arms and on their way by steamboat to Nauvoo.

147Inland on the prairie, two other bodies of armed men were marching north. One was a body of militia from Schuyler County; the second, a force from Brown County. Both had been mustered to duty during the night on orders bearing the forged signature of the Governor.

147In Columbus, also, a Captain Jonas was raising a force of men to march to upper Hancock. His command of one hundred never left the city. They were met by the Governor in his flight south.

147Only the dark-green-clad Quincy Riflemen-dispatched to maintain order in Carthage by the Governor-entered the scene of the trouble, the other militias learning the truth of the matter somewhere along the way and returning home.

148The bodies of Joseph and Hyrum were conveyed to Nauvoo the next day in wagons belonging to Artois Hamilton. Being open wagons, they were covered with bushes to protect the bodies from the sun. Artois drove one rig-the one conveying the body of the Prophet-and his son William R. followed with the body of Hyrum. General Deming detached a mount of eight soldiers to act as guard.

148Dr. Richards and Samuel Smith accompanied the bodies to Nauvoo.

148On arriving, the doctor, as the only member of the Council of the Twelve in Nauvoo, directed the people to keep the peace. He spoke from the platform the Governor had spoken from the evening before. The estimated eight thousand citizens grouped about him pledged themselves to trust to the law for a recourse.

148In the Mansion House the bodies were washed and prepared for viewing.

148Joseph had taken four shots. One in the right side of the chest, another beneath the heart, a third in the lower section of the right bowel, and a fourth in the back part of the right leg.

148Hyrum, too, had been shot four times: one in the face to the left of the nose, one in his back that passed through his body, one in his left leg, and one that grazed his chest and entered his head through the throat.

148After the bodies were prepared, the two wives and the children of the martyred men were admitted to see the bodies as they lay on separate beds.

148Emma screamed and fell back, on first seeing the body of her dead husband, and was caught and supported by Dimick Huntington. Then she fell forward to the Prophet's face, kissing him and calling, "Joseph, Joseph, Joseph," and begged him to speak to her. Later she placed her hand upon the Prophet's cheek and murmured, "Oh, Joseph, Joseph, my husband, my husband, have they taken you from me at last?"

149Hyrum's wife Mary remained composed throughout, after having lost control during the night. She sat on the edge of the bed and pulled her husband to her. Stroking back his hair, she said, "Hyrum, Hyrum. Have they shot you, my dear Hyrum? Are you dead, my dear Hyrum?"

149The children clustered about their fathers in mute bewilderment, tears streaming down their cheeks.

149Later the bodies were put into coffins covered with black velvet and trimmed with brass nails. Over the face of each was hung a lid with brass hinges, under which was placed a plate of glass to protect the faces. The coffins were lined with white cambric and then put into rough pine boxes.

149At eight the following morning the doors were opened to the Mansion House and over ten thousand persons filed through to view the Prophet and the Patriarch.

149After the martyrdom, Samuel Smith was taken to a frame house across the street from the Mansion House where he died on July 30 "of a fever occasioned by over-exertion." In less than a month his widow bore his last child, a girl.

149Emma Smith gave birth to the Prophet's last child, a son, on November 18. He was named David Hyrum.

149Attended by his wife, John Taylor remained in the Hamilton House for five days, when, against the wishes of those looking after him, he demanded to be removed to Nauvoo. He lived to become the third president of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints.
