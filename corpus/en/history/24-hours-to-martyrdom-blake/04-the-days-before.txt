Reed H. Blake, Twenty-four Hours to Martyrdom ( 1973)

The Days Before

Just over a year after the founding of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, the Prophet Joseph Smith designated the area in and around Jackson County, Missouri, as the gathering place for the people where they should erect a temple and establish the kingdom of God.

48The new people were soon resented. The Mormon stock was mainly New England and English, which represented two hated factions: abolitionists and foreigners. (The feeling against Great Britain and her part in the War of 1812 was still strong on the American frontier.) The Mormons were clannish, and the fear generally manifested against any close-knit group soon sprang up. Month by month the Mormons acquired more and more land, and thus would begin in due time to crowd out the old settlers. In time also their number would give them political control of the state. From the pro-slave senate seats in Washington to the governor's office in Jefferson City to the homes of its citizens, Missouri was dedicated to the cause of slavery. As a body representing free-staters, the Mormons should be driven out, said the Missourians, as were all other unwanted elements.

48The movement against the Saints began, and in time brought about the expulsion from Jackson County and from Ray County, the Haun's Mill massacre, and finally the siege at Far West in 1838-when the remaining Saints were driven from their homes across the frozen earth to the Mississippi River, where on the other side the city of Quincy, Illinois, opened her arms to the destitute people.

48Joseph in the meantime, with other Church leaders, had been jailed.

49When first taken, they were paraded from county to county and exhibited as a trophy of war; poorly treated and cared for, and threatened with death at every turn.

49It was of this time that Parley P. Pratt wrote:

49"In one of those tedious nights we had lain as if in sleep till the hour of midnight had passed, and our ears and hearts had been pained, while we had listened for hours to the obscene jests, the horrid oaths, the dreadful blasphemies and filthy language of our guards, Colonel Price at their head, as they recounted to each other their deeds of rapine, murder, robbery, etc., which they had committed among the Mormons while at Far West and vicinity. They even boasted of defiling by force wives, daughters, and virgins, and of shooting or dashing out the brains of men, women, and children.

49"I had listened till I became so disgusted, shocked, horrified, and so filled with the spirit of indignant justice that I could scarcely refrain from rising upon my feet and rebuking the guards; but had said nothing to Joseph, or any one else, although I lay next to him and knew he was awake. On a sudden he rose to his feet, and spoke in a voice of thunder, or as the roaring lion, uttering, as nearly as I can recollect, the following words:

49"'Silence, you fiends of the infernal pit! In the name of Jesus Christ I rebuke you, and command you to be still. I will not live another minute and hear such language. Cease such talk, or you or I die this instant!"

49"He ceased to speak. He stood erect in terrible majesty. Chained, and without a weapon; calm, unruffled and dignified as an angel, he looked upon the quailing guards, whose weapons were lowered or dropped to the ground; whose knees smote together, and who, shrinking into a corner or crouching at his feet, begged his pardon, and remained quiet till a change of guards.

49"I have seen the ministers of justice, clothed in magisterial robes, and criminals arraigned before them while life was suspended on a breath, in the courts of England; I have witnessed a Congress in solemn session to give laws to nations; I have tried to conceive of kings, or royal courts, of thrones and crowns, and of emperors assembled to decide the fate of kingdoms; but dignity and majesty have I seen but once, as it stood in chains, at midnight in a dungeon, in an obscure village in Missouri."

50It was late in November of 1838 when five of the prisoners were taken to Liberty jail-the others being released or kept in other jails for a while.

50After five and a half months they were allowed to leave, when no charge could be sustained against them.

50When Joseph finally found his way to the banks of the Mississippi River in upper Hancock County, Illinois, in the spring of 1839, he found his people starving, dejected, and ill-clothed, living in makeshift homes, in tents, in wagons. Many of the people were sick, suffering from cholera and the effects of spending so many weeks on the prairie without adequate food, clothing, or shelter.

50In their weakened condition, the people readily fell prey that summer to the malaria that infected Nauvoo and its vicinity. On July 22 Joseph felt inspired to go forth healing the sick. Wilford Woodruff recorded what took place. Joseph began at his own log cabin, healing the sick about him, then walked up the river bank "Â�and he healed all the sick that lay in his path. Among the number was Henry G. Sherwood, who was nigh unto death. Joseph stood in the mouth of his tent and commanded him in the name of Jesus Christ to arise and come out of his tent, and he obeyed him and was healed. Brother Benjamin Brown and his family also lay sick, the former appearing to be in a dying condition. Joseph healed them in the name of the Lord.

50"After healing all that lay sick upon the bank of the river as far as the stone house, he called upon Elder Kimball and some others to accompany him across the river to visit the sick at Montrose. Many of the Saints were living at the old military barracks. Among the number were several of the Twelve. On his arrival, the first house he visited was that occupied by Elder Brigham Young, the President of the Quorum of the Twelve, who lay sick. Joseph healed him, when he arose and accompanied the Prophet on his visit to others who were in the same condition. They visited Elder W. Woodruff, also Elders Orson Pratt and John Taylor, all of whom were living in Montrose. They also accompanied him.

51"The next place they visited was the home of Elijah Fordham, who was supposed to be about breathing his last. When the company entered the room the Prophet of God walked up to the dying man, and took hold of his right hand and spoke to him; but Brother Fordham was unable to speak, his eyes were set in his head like glass, and he seemed entirely unconscious of all around him. Joseph held his hand and looked into his eyes in silence for a length of time. A change in the countenance of Brother Fordham was soon perceptible to all present. His sight returned, and upon Joseph asking him if he knew him, he, in a slow whisper, answered, 'I fear it is too late; if you had come sooner I think I could have been healed.' The Prophet said, 'Do you believe in Jesus Christ?' He answered in a feeble voice, 'I do.'

51"Joseph then stood erect, still holding his hand in silence several moments, then he spoke in a very loud voice, saying, 'Brother Fordham, I command you in the name of Jesus Christ to arise from this bed and be made whole.' His voice was like the voice of God, and not of man. It seemed as though the house shook on its very foundation. Brother Fordham arose from his bed and was made whole. His feet were bound in poultices, which he kicked off, then putting on his clothes he ate a bowl of bread and milk and followed the Prophet into the street."

51The group continued to travel and heal the sick.

51Thus began the Nauvoo period.

51Nauvoo was a city visited daily by authors and journalists-from the East and abroad-who came to view the swiftly blooming metropolis and interview the Prophet; by politicians courting the favor of the Saints, who for several years held the political balance of power in Illinois; by land speculators bent on making a hasty profit; by people of means making the fashionable journey up the Mississippi River by steamboat; and-as with all western cities of the time-by the cunning, the destitute, and the wanted.

52A city of eleven to twelve thousand people, the largest in Illinois, it boasted the first chartered university in the state, the University of the City of Nauvoo, this at a time when Chicago was a fledgling city of five thousand on the southwest shore of Lake Michigan, and Springfield, the new state capital, a "muddy little town recently planted on the prairie."

52The city, with its broad streets intersecting one another at right angles, was hailed even at that time as it was in the years to come as a new concept in community planning and the first truly American contribution to city dwelling. Each family had a plot within the city limits large enough for home and garden. The barns and farms were beyond the city limits and travelled to each day. Under this plan everyone could enjoy the conditions of both city and country life.

52Yet between the appearance of the Saints in Commerce, as Nauvoo was first called, and their hurried exodus, only seven years elapsed.

52Commerce was originally a large Indian village known as Quashquema, and later, in the early 1820s, it was settled as an Indian trading post. The land eventually came into the hands of one Dr. Isaac Galland, an Illinois and Iowa landholder, and a farmer named Hugh White. On May 1, 1839, a committee representing the Mormon Church purchased two large farms from Galland for $14,000 in the vicinity of Commerce. Liberal terms of credit were arranged and shortly thereafter other purchases in the area were made, the Church buying some land in Keokuk, Iowa, and nearly all the land between Nashville, Iowa, six miles above Keokuk,

53 City of Nauvoo

53and Montrose. So it was that the Mormons dominated the lands on both sides of the Mississippi River.

54Of the early development, Joseph recorded the following:

54"When I made the purchase of White and Galland there were one stone house, three frame houses, and two block houses, which constituted the whole city of Commerce. Between Commerce and Mr. Davidson Hibbard's, there was one stone house and three log houses, including the one that I live in, and these were all the houses in this vicinity, and the place was literally a wilderness. The land was mostly covered with trees and bushes, and much of it so wet that it was with the utmost difficulty a footman could get through, and totally impossible for teams. Commerce was so unhealthful, very few could live there; but believing that it might become a healthful place by the blessing of heaven to the Saints, and no more eligible place presenting itself, I considered it wisdom to make an attempt to build up a city."

54The city-in the horseshoe bend of the Mississippi River-stood on two levels, the Hill and the Flat.

54The bank of the river, some haft-dozen feet above the high water mark, immediately leveled off for seven blocks and then the ground began a gradual ascent of sixty to seventy feet where it leveled off again into the prairie, thus forming the city's two levels.

54Between the great crescent of the river and the homes on the Flat was wedged a belt of wooded ground.

54It was from the bluff, in Nauvoo's new Hill section, that one went to view the surrounding country. Here from the lookout platform, a common addition to river town homes, one could see the whole valley of the Mississippi River lying exposed for miles in each direction. Beyond the checkerboard of the city, the wooded belt, and the broad, sweeping curve of the river, the abrupt banks of the Iowa shore leveled off into a low plateau covered by a stand of timber. Here was the Mormon settlement of Montrose.

54Looking south one could see the tortuous course of the Des Moines River forming the boundary between Missouri and Iowa Territory as it made its confluence with the Mississippi from a northwesterly direction.

55Upstream one could see as far north as Fort Madison and downstream as far south as Keokuk, both on the Iowa side.

55Dividing the river between Nauvoo and Montrose was a low-lying island some fifty to two hundred yards wide and three-fourths of a mile long.

55Stretching eastward from Nauvoo was the beautiful flat country, of prairie grass and intermittent woodlands. The whole country between Nauvoo and Carthage, to the southeast, with but very, little exception, was one continuous plain, the soil rich and productive, the small winding creeks lined with trees.

55In the center of the city, on the brow of the bluff, stood the Nauvoo Temple. Already, in its partly completed condition, it could be seen for several miles up and down the river. It measured 128 feet from east to west and 88 feet wide. Construction on the building started soon after the settling in Illinois, the cornerstone being laid April 6, 1841-the eleventh birthday of the Church.

55The temple represented a willing hardship to the Saints. Materials were hard to come by and considerable distance was covered to bring in the wood and some of the stone. During its construction several of those engaged in cutting stone suffered severe illness from the dust.

55When Joseph looked last upon it in June 1844 the walls were completed up to the first story windows; the basement with its baptismal font having already been completed and in use since November of 1841.

55Its style of architecture was said to be a mixture of Romanesque, Egyptian, and Greek.

55Under Brigham Young the temple would be completed, the front spire rising 168 feet into the sky. It would be dedicated privately on April 30 and publicly on May 1 of 1846.

55Still later, on October 9, 1848, it would be destroyed by an incendiary, the burned walls standing until May 27, 1850, when they would be leveled by a heavy wind. At that time it would be planned that the temple should be restored-not by the Mormons, but by a communistic group known as the Icarians who moved to the Nauvoo ground made cheap by the exodus. The group was led by Etienne Cabet, prominent French jurist and attorney-general of Corsica during the Second Republic, who desired to establish a new social order but found Europe unwilling to sanction it.

56Upon seeing the burned-out temple, Cabet determined to reconstruct the building, and despite the heavy debt already handicapping the new social order, paid five hundred dollars for the structure. Eleven workers having been assigned to the building, an Icarian architect, Alfred H. Piquenard, who would later design the Illinois state capitol building at Springfield, went north to buy lumber.

56On that May afternoon at almost the exact hour the martyrdom had taken place six years earlier, a wind swept through Nauvoo and, seeming to single out the temple, toppled the walls with such force that the collapse was heard three miles distant.

56The Icarian reconstruction was abandoned.

56In time the stones would be carted to nearly every state in the Union and to Europe, while many remained in Nauvoo, being used in the construction of other buildings.

56Nauvoo had two steam grist and saw mills and one water mill, a wagon shop, two stone quarries, an iron foundry, one pottery works, and a match and powder factory. Most of the manufactured products, however, were consumed at home; agriculture was the economic basis of the area, the farms extending deep into the country, away from the river.

56Each morning from the temple construction site a bell tolled at seven, signaling the start of another work day, and rang again at twelve noon, one, and six o'clock.

56During the day the air was rent with the whistles of riverboats coming upstream from perhaps St. Louis, Quincy, or Keokuk, or downstream from Fort Madison, Burlington, or Rock Island. Between Nauvoo and Montrose the river was two miles wide, the water making a broad, even flow as it swept around the curve, and the Maid of Iowa plied daily between the two cities when not on a run; and when it came in to dock at the head of Water Street, if someone were atop a lookout platform, he would wave his arm in greeting and Dan Jones, the Maid's Welsh captain, would wave back and sound the whistle.

57Around the homes grew phlox, cannas, larkspur, dahlias, sunflowers, and persicary or heartsease; and often the sidewalks between the homes were lined with hollyhocks. In the gardens the common plants were corn, potatoes, turnips, and tomatoes, referred to as loveapples.

57Day by day oxen plodded through the streets of Nauvoo, pulling behind them large slabs of stone underslung on the heavy timber of a wagon skeleton. As they worked their way up the bluff to the temple site, children, temporarily lost to their parents, could be found trailing along behind or standing at a distance watching the workmen on the temple block; or perhaps, if not engrossed with such things, playing among the cornelians which grew in profusion on the sand banks along the river.

57On the Flat, twelve blocks across town from the temple, was the home of Joseph Smith. Here also, on the comer of Water and Granger Streets, stood Joseph's store. There being no paid ministry in the Church, the Prophet as well as all others worked for a living. His commercial venture, however, was not a financial success, largely because of the liberal credit the Prophet extended to the poor.

57Beside the store on the east side stood what Joseph fondly referred to as the Homestead. It was a log house and stood just back from the river. The Prophet had moved into it from his tent nearby when Commerce was first settled, later adding a frame wing and an addition to the rear.

58Around the corner from the Homestead north on Main Street was Joseph's present home, the Mansion House, a twenty-two-room home of which fifteen rooms were bedrooms, and in which Joseph and his family had lived for the past year. It was a clapboard, two-story house with nine shuttered windows facing the street. It was built to accommodate visitors, since there was only one small hotel in Nauvoo, and to serve as the Prophet's residence. It always had guests and showed a profit.

58Across Water Street to the south, on the bank of the river, in the process of being built, was the Nauvoo House. Begun in 1841, it too was to be a boarding house and home of the Prophet, as the Mansion House was proving too small. The plans called for a building 120 feet by 40 feet on Main Street and an ell of the same dimensions extending at right angles along the river.

58Next to Joseph's Mansion House, on the north side, stood the home of Sidney Rigdon, a one-and-one-half-story house that served as Nauvoo's first post office. Its owner was now in Philadelphia. After many years of hardship, Rigdon was wearying of the persecution against the Saints.

58A block north from the Prophet's home on Main Street was the hotel. It was a two-story frame building of modest quarters that was continually strained to the limits by the constant influx of visitors to the city, and of immigrants-converts to the Church-while they were looking for permanent quarters. On the third block above the hotel facing east on Main Street was the Times and Seasons printing office, next to which was the home of its editor, John Taylor. Two blocks north of the Taylor home on Main was the handsome three-story Masonic Hall.

58The day in Nauvoo began at 5 A.M. and extended for some twelve to sixteen hours in the summer and ten to twelve hours in the winter. In the evenings one would read the Bible, the Book of Mormon, or other Church literature; perhaps a copy of some eastern newspaper arriving on a steamboat that day; or one of the local newspapers, of which the city had two, the Nauvoo Neighbor and the Times and Seasons.

59The Neighbor was an informal news sheet containing mostly local news. The Times and Seasons was a common country newspaper on one hand and a Church journal on the other. A semi-monthly publication, it carried marriage notices (headed hymeneal notices), obituaries, advertisements, orders to the Nauvoo Legion, and acts of the legislature, in addition to reports of missionary work, communications from the Prophet, and the expounding of Mormon doctrine.

59Sundays in Nauvoo were devoted to church services and religious instruction. When the basement of the temple was completed the Saints met there, whereas previously, and on some occasions yet, they met in one of the city's three principal groves-one immediately west of the temple, another in the hollow south of Mulholland Street, and a third on Knight Street, in the fourth tier of blocks east of the temple. The last-named was the one in which the Prophet had delivered some of his most powerful sermons and where six weeks hence Brigham Young would first be accepted as the new leader of the Church.

59In the evenings the seventies, elders, or priests quorums might hold a corn-husking party, a pie supper and dance, or an amateur theater night. A favorite with everyone was the dancing on the deck of the Maid of Iowa when it was docked at Nauvoo. The cool breeze off the water that gave relief to the summer heat, the twinkling of lights on the rippling water below, and the swish of full skirts was a thrill long remembered by the young. Of special occasion for the young adults was the gathering of the young ladies to a home for quilting from three until six in the afternoon, where, while they quilted, they discussed the happenings of the day, often referred to by the mothers as gossiping, and then the retiring to a large food-laden table in the kitchen for supper. After the dishes were washed, the kitchen cleaned, and the quilt frames taken down, the young men began arriving following the completion of their evening chores. Once together, the young people would sing several rousing songs and then promenade around the room for a dancing partner. At nine o'clock a second supper was eaten with the young men; after which games followed until midnight, when the guests departed.

60As one would surmise, the social engagements of importance were held at the Mansion House, where the Prophet loved to entertain, or at the Masonic Hall. Cotillion parties and other formal affairs were held at the Mansion House or in other homes large enough for such affairs. Generally the proceeds from such events went to the benefit of widows, missionaries, or the needy.

60Informal affairs were held in many places-a neighbor's new barn or shed, in the cleared areas of the groves, or on the wharf.

60Popular with the Saints were debating and dramatic societies. These were sponsored by the various Church groups, social organizations, and schools, and received much praise from Church officials.

60In the evenings the streets were often filled with the music of singers, the serenading of newly married couples and new parents being a common practice.

60The city had three bands: the Nauvoo Brass Band, the Quadrille Band (when strings were added to the Brass Band), and the Nauvoo Legion Band.

60There were special events in Nauvoo, too, such as a visit from the Mabie and Howe Circus Company, or a pleasure trip for one dollar up the river to the Illinois bluffs on the New Haven as part of the Fourth of July celebration.

60In education the city offered complete grade schooling in 1839, and in 1841 chartered the university, although a campus was never built upon and the classes that were offered were conducted in other public quarters. By 1844 many schools in the city offered "common studies," meaning the three R's, "and on demand the higher mathematics, philosophy, chemistry, Greek, Latin, French, and Spanish."

60Visiting in 1843, a Rev. M. Prior of the Methodist Church reported: "I found all the people engaged in some useful and healthy employment. Their place was alive with business-much more so than any place I have visited since the hard times commenced. I sought in vain for anything that bore the mark of immorality, but was both astonished and highly pleased at my ill success. I could see no loungers about the streets nor any drunkards about the taverns. I did not meet with these distorted features of ruffians, or with the ill-bred and impudent. I heard not an oath in the place, I saw not a gloomy countenance; all were cheerful, polite, and industrious."

61Of special importance to the men, outside of their Church activities, were the activities of the Masonic Lodge and the Nauvoo Legion. Many recreational activities were open to the men in Nauvoo, but parading and drilling with the Legion was the chief activity. Other pastimes included baseball, horse-pulling contests, horseshoe pitching, quill hunting, wrestling matches, pulling sticks, informal horse racing, and sleighing in the winter and rowboating in the summer. If one wanted to swim he had to walk up or down river, as a city ordinance forbade the activity within the city limits because of the "state of nudity" involved.

61At the time, the Legion numbered about five thousand. Lacking funds for a common uniform, the men improvised their own, and visitors often commented on the display of imaginative dress-as well as the diversity-when the Legion paraded.

61The Nauvoo Legion was not an exclusively Mormon military association (Nauvoo was 80 percent Mormon, 20 percent non-Mormon), but a body of citizen soldiers from north Hancock County. In time they became known as the best militia in the state, and on occasion were said to be the best in the country.

61Yet even as the Legion drilled, it brought resentment for the Mormons. Jealousy among the other militias in the surrounding counties arose, and when the Legion drilled, as often it did, a shout went up from the others that it was not drilling to perfect itself for the upholding of state and national laws, as the Nauvoo Legion claimed, but for the spreading of the Mormon religion as, said the critics, did Mohammed of old.

62Joseph realized there was but little difference between the people who lived on the east bank and those who lived on the west bank of the Mississippi River. It followed then that the same habits of Mormonism that had proven offensive on the west bank would prove offensive on the east bank. And so he took steps to fortify the Mormon position.

62First was the securing from the Illinois legislature an unprecedented charter for the city of Nauvoo, which granted the city complete independence educationally, judicially, and militarily. The city council was given power to establish and execute all such ordinances, not repugnant to the state and national constitutions, as they might deem necessary for the peace, benefit, good order, regulation, convenience, and cleanliness of the city. An independent military organization known as the Nauvoo Legion was to be established and was to be at the disposal of not only the governor but also the Nauvoo mayor. The municipal court was to consist of the mayor as chief justice and the alderman as associate justices and was to have power to grant writs of habeas corpus in all cases arising under the ordinances of the city council, and the mayor was to have exclusive jurisdiction in all cases arising under the ordinances of the city.

62The second step was the securing of the lands opposite Nauvoo on the Iowa side of the river. Here grew up the small Mormon community of Montrose.

62In the meantime the Church petitioned Congress for a redress of wrongs suffered in Missouri, to which President Martin Van Buren replied: "Your cause is just, but I can do nothing for you. If I take up for you, I shall lose the votes of Missouri."

62As Nauvoo grew, and the Mormon vote held the balance of power in Illinois, it was to someone's dislike no matter how the Mormons voted. It came to the point, said Joseph, of being damned if you do and damned if you don't.

63The Mormons favored neither political party, but voted for that man who didn't claim to be an anti-Mormon. Through the years the Mormon vote was shared by both parties.

63Meanwhile Joseph set about to remove his people from further persecution. If the American people could not accept a new religion, he would strike a compromise until he could remove his followers to where they could live in peace.

63First, to remove the Mormon vote out of the coming national election, the Prophet himself was nominated for the presidency on a ticket independent of the Whig or Democratic parties; and secondly, he prepared a party to explore the Oregon and California country.

63Of the first move, the Mormons did not seriously think the Prophet could make it to the presidential chair; nevertheless, it was an opportunity to place before the people nationally some interesting views on politics and government. Second, it would enable the people to vote conscientiously in the coming fall elections.

63If the Mormons didn't take the Prophet's bid seriously, many others did, even to the extent that the Prophet once laughingly commented: "When I get hold of the eastern papers and see how popular I am, I am afraid that I shall be elected."

63In the east, many men of wealth and position were offering their means and influence to support the Prophet's views, which included:

63Reduce Congress at least two-thirds. Two senators from a state and two members in the House to a million population "will do more business than the army that now occupy the halls of the national legislature. Pay them two dollars and their board per diem (except Sundays). That is more than the farmer gets, and he lives honestly."

63All prisoners would be required to work on public projects "where the culprit can be taught wisdom and virtue." Only the murderers should be placed in confinement or put to death.

64Abolish slavery by the year 1850, paying every man a reasonable price for his slaves from money received from the sale of public lands.

64Create national banks in every state with a central bank in Washington, the net gain to go to the national and state treasuries, with no bank being allowed to issue more bills than the amount of capital stock in her vaults and the interest.

64And finally-in keeping with his belief that the Constitution of the United States was divinely inspired, that the country was to be a mighty nation, and that the land of America should be a bulwark of freedom where the world's oppressed would find a haven-the Prophet proposed the annexation of Texas and the acquisition of the Oregon country. Thus he struck out in the vision of the Great America, free of colonial concepts and stretching from the shores of the Atlantic to the shores of the Pacific. It was this plank of his platform, later crystallized into "Fifty-four Forty or Fight," that won the presidential chair for James K. Polk.

64The ticket was called the Jeffersonian Reform Party, and to promulgate its views, 350 men were sent to all parts of the nation to stump for the Prophet.

64Of the second move, the Prophet recorded under a date of February' 20, 1844: "I instructed the Twelve Apostles to send out a delegation and investigate the locations of California and Oregon, and hunt out a good location, where we can move after the temple is completedÂ�." A few days later he said of the western expedition, "Let that man go that can raise five hundred dollars, a good horse and mule, a double-barrel gun, one-barrel rifle and the other smooth bore, a saddle and bridle, a pair of revolving pistols, bowie-knife, and a good sabre." He suggested twenty-five men for the job.

64Sang Joseph and his Mormon followers:

64"In Upper California, O that's the land for me- It lies between the mountains and the great Pacific sea. The Saints can be protected there, and enjoy their liberty In Upper California, O that's the land for me."

65In April the Prophet sent Orson Hyde to Washington to get information on the Oregon country and to induce Congress to pass a bill authorizing the Mormons protection along the way.

65With these two plans under way, the Mormon leader could have overcome his troubles in Illinois and Missouri. (There were still those in Missouri who hounded the Saints. Many of them were living on land taken from the Mormons-and thus without legal title. The Mormons talked of going to Texas and to Oregon, and with the mighty Nauvoo Legion leading the way, they could do it-and many Missourians wanted those areas also to be pro-slave, not free state. To dissolve the Mormon movement would solve many problems.)

65Yet it was trouble from still another quarter-Nauvoo-coupled with those already at hand that precipitated the explosion.

65After the death of the Prophet there came to light the existence of a secret order in Nauvoo whose purpose it was to bring about the downfall and/or death of the Prophet. Joseph had been aware of it at the time. The organization included men who had once been high in the Church and who had later broken away in an attempt to organize a church of their own; men who had hoped to use the Church for their own personal gain and had failed; and men who had been excommunicated from the Church for whoredoms, stealing, and the like.

65In time the group began working with those anti-Mormons in Carthage and Warsaw, the two parties in turn communicating with the Prophet's Missouri enemies.

65It was this faction in Nauvoo that established a hostile newspaper in the Prophet's camp, the Nauvoo Expositor. That newspaper having been declared a public nuisance by the city council, by order of that body the printing press and the first issue of the newspaper were destroyed and the type pied.

66Night was upon the city of Nauvoo when Hyrum Smith walked out of the Nauvoo Mansion and extended his hand to Reynolds Cahoon, saying, "A company of men are seeking to kill my brother Joseph, and the Lord has warned him to flee to the Rocky Mountains to save his life. Goodbye, Brother Cahoon, we shall see you again."

66A few minutes later Joseph emerged from the Mansion. He had been with his family. He held a handkerchief to his face as tears streamed down his cheeks. He followed silently behind his brother.

66Between nine and ten o'clock they waited on the bank of the river for a skiff to take them across. There were three of them now, the Prophet, his brother, and Willard Richards.

66Only hours earlier the Prophet had dictated to Richards the last entry to be put into his private diary. Related Joseph:

66I told Stephen Markham that if I and Hyrum were ever taken again we should be massacred, or I was not a prophet of God. I want Hyrum to live to avenge my blood, but he is determined not to leave me.

66Thus the diary ended.

66Earlier in the evening a council of men composed of Joseph and his close associates had heard John Taylor report on his visit to the Governor. The Governor had requested that one or more "well-informed and discreet persons" be sent to him to "lay before me your version of the matter." Taylor and Dr. John M. Bernhisel had gone.

66The whole countryside was in a state of confusion. The Mormons were as much feared for what they would do as they feared what their enemies would do. Rumors bred rumors. Men were armed and drilling everywhere. Agents were sent abroad for arms and ammunition. Day by day men crossed and recrossed the river from Missouri as attacks upon Nauvoo were planned and cancelled. A cannon was brought up from St. Louis to Warsaw, and the city voted one thousand dollars for supplies. Mormons were driven from their outlying homes in the county, and in many of the towns, stripped of their arms.

67At Carthage, the Governor informed Taylor and Bernhisel that Joseph and the city councilmen would have to submit themselves for arrest to the same magistrate whose authority had been heretofore resisted. "Nothing short of this can vindicate the dignity of violated law and allay the just excitement of the people." The Governor, Taylor reported, was surrounded by the Prophet's enemies, who frequently interrupted Taylor and Bernhisel's discourse with "That's a damned lie," and "You lie like hell."

67Having heard Taylor's report and read the Governor's letter, Joseph turned to his brother and said, "There is no mercy-no mercy here."

67"No," Hyrum said, "just as sure as we fall into their hands, we are dead men."

67"Yes," the Prophet returned. "What shall we do, Brother Hyrum?"

67"I don't know."

67Then the Prophet's mood changed, and he said, "The way is open. It is clear to my mind what to do. All they want is Hyrum and myself; then tell everybody to go about their business, and not to collect in groups, but to scatter about. There is no doubt they will come here and search for us. Let them search; they will not harm you in person or property, and not even a hair of your head. We will cross the river tonight, and go away to the West."

67While waiting on the river bank, they sent for William W. Phelps. When he arrived Joseph instructed him to take their families to Cincinnati by the second steamboat arriving at Nauvoo, and that once in the Ohio city he was to begin petitioning the President of the United States and Congress for redress of grievances, and to see if they would grant the Church liberty and equal rights.

68Concluded Joseph: "Go to our wives, and tell them what we have concluded to do, and learn their feelings on the subject; and tell Emma you will be ready to start by the second steamboat, and she has sufficient money wherewith to pay the expenses. If you ascertain by tomorrow morning that there is anything wrong, come over the river to Montrose, to the house of Captain John Killien, and there you will learn where we are."

68Following this the three men called at the lodgings of Orrin Porter Rockwell. It was now Sunday, June 23. At 2 A.M., with Rockwell, they finally crossed the Mississippi after walking up the river bank until they found a boat owned by Aaron Johnson. The boat was leaky, and while Rockwell rowed, the others baled water with their boots and shoes to keep it from sinking.

68At sunrise they arrived at the home of John Killien, but not finding him at home they went to William Jordan's.

68Dr. Bernhisel and Reynolds Cahoon crossed over the river and visited the Prophet at nine in the morning. Cahoon made some explanations concerning the Governor's last letter, and assured the Prophet the Governor meant to give him full protection. When the two returned to Nauvoo, Rockwell went with them. He was to have returned after dark with horses, but at one o'clock in the afternoon he and Cahoon returned at the request of Joseph's wife, Emma.

68Early that morning a posse had arrived in Nauvoo to arrest Joseph, but not finding him they left immediately. The posse told the citizens that if Joseph and Hyrum were not given up the Governor intended to send in his troops until such time as the men did surrender. Emma had then written a letter asking Joseph to return, which she gave to Cahoon; and then she asked both Cahoon and Rockwell to convince her husband he should surrender himself.

68The two found Joseph, Hyrum, and Willard Richards in a room by themselves. Provisions for their journey to the Rocky Mountains were scattered across the floor.

69They delivered Emma's letter and verbal message and told of the people's excitement at the report of what the troops intended to do. Others in the house joined them and the group began discussing the matter.

69Three of those present, Lorenzo D. Wasson, Hiram Kimball, and Reynolds Cahoon, accused the Prophet of cowardice for leaving his people, concluding it was like the fable, when the wolves came the shepherd ran, leaving the sheep to be devoured.

69Joseph replied: "If my life is of no value to my friends it is of none to myself."

69Turning to Rockwell he asked for his opinion on the matter and Rockwell answered: "You are the oldest and ought to know best; and as you make your bed, I will lie with you."

69Hyrum said, "Let us go back and give ourselves up, and see the thing out."

69Joseph stood silent for a few moments, then replied, "If you go back I will go with you, but we shall be butchered."

69"No, no," Hyrum returned, "let us go back and put our trust in God, and we shall not be harmed. The Lord is in it. If we live or have to die, we will be reconciled to our fate."

69After another pause, the Prophet said: "Doctor Richards, will you write a letter to Governor Ford?" Richards prepared his paper and pen. The Prophet worded the letter and it was signed by himself and Hyrum. It read:

"Bank of the River Mississippi Sunday, June 23rd, 1845, 2 p.m.

"His Excellency Governor Ford:

69"Sir.-I wrote you a long communication at 12 last night, expressive of my views of your Excellency's communication of yesterday. I thought your letter rather severe, but one of my friends has just come to me with an explanation from the captain of your posse which softened the subject matter of your communication, and gives us greater assurance of protection, and that your Excellency has succeeded in bringing in subjection the spirits which surround your Excellency to some extent. And I declare again the only objection I ever had or ever made on trial by my country at any time, was what I have made in my last letter-on account of assassins, and the reason I have to fear deathly consequences from their hands.

70"But from the explanation, I now offer to come to you at Carthage on the morrow, as early as shall be convenient for your posse to escort us into headquarters, provided we can have a fair trial, not be abused nor have my witnesses abused, and have all things done in due form of law, without partiality, and you may depend on my honor without the show of a great armed force to produce excitement in the minds of the timid.

70"We will meet your posse, if this letter is satisfactory, (if not, inform me) at or near the Mound, at or about two o'clock tomorrow afternoon, which will be as soon as we can get our witnesses and prepare for trial. We shall expect to take our witnesses with us, and not have to wait a subpoena or part at least, so as not to detain the proceedings, although we may want time for counsel.

70"We remain most respectfully, your Excellency's humble servants,

"Joseph Smith Hyrum Smith"

70After signing the letter, Joseph dictated two other letters, one to Horace T. Huggins engaging him in counsel, and a second to Dr. J. R. Wakefield to appear as a witness.

70At four o'clock the party started back.

70While walking to the river, Joseph and Rockwell fell behind. Joseph expressed his wish to get his people together once more to talk to them. Rockwell returned that if that be his wish, he would get the people together and Joseph could talk to them by starlight.

71At nine o'clock at night, Theodore Turley and Jedediah M. Grant arrived in Carthage with Joseph's letter to the Governor. Upon reading it aloud, the Governor nodded and said he would send a posse to escort General Smith in safety to Carthage.

71Immediately several anti-Mormons in the room, including Wilson Law and Joseph Jackson, began hurling insults against the Mormon leader.

71It was not in the personality of the Governor to stand against popular opinion. He now turned his back on the messengers, and when he turned again to face them, it was to inform them that an escort was an honor not given to any citizen, that Joseph and the others were to be in Carthage at 10 A.M. without any escort from Nauvoo or Carthage, and "if he hadn't given himself up by that time, Nauvoo would be destroyed and all the men, women, and children in it."

71The two men were stunned by the reversal. But before they could offer a rebuttal, the Governor ordered them from Carthage, to be out of the city by ten o'clock (they had made preparation to spend the night). Grant and Turley left immediately, but as their horses were tired from the journey to Carthage, they did not arrive in Nauvoo until four o'clock the next morning.

71Though quieter than usual, the streets of Nauvoo were awake with people. Closing the wicket-latch loudly behind him, Joseph mounted his horse and joined his party of seventeen men for whom a warrant had been sworn.

71They rode up Main Street and then turned right on Mulholland. When they reached the temple, Joseph stopped and looked upon the building for a long time and then down over the city. To the west the Mississippi River lay like a silver ribbon glistening under the morning sun as it arched around the city. The sight drew from Joseph the comment: "This is the loveliest place and the best people under the heavens; little do they know the trials that await them."

72From across the square some of the workmen recognized the Prophet and waved to him. A cloud of dust rose above the stonehouse, while to one side were piled large stacks of cut lumber from the Church's pinery at Black River Falls in the Menimonie, Chippewa, and Winnebago Indian country in the Wisconsin Territory. The pinery had been in operation under the direction of Bishop George Miller since the spring of 1841.

72A mile and a half east of the temple on the road to Carthage the party crossed Casper Creek and a short distance further the city graveyard, now known locally as the "Old Mormon burying ground." They passed groves of oak and hickory and sycamore trees, and other woodlots containing walnut, cherry, cottonwood, and elm. Now and then there would be a little unnamed swale with a rivulet of water running down, or off to the side a limestone quarry. About three miles out from Nauvoo they passed Joseph's farm-Cornelius Lott occupied the house and looked after things-and Joseph paused again for a few moments to look upon it.

72At ten minutes to ten they arrived at Albert G. Fellows' farm and saw coming down the road a company of militia. "Do not be alarmed, brethren," the Prophet said, "for they cannot do more to you than the enemies of truth did to the ancient Saints-they can only kill the body."

72Their fears proved unfounded, for it was Captain Dunn and sixty men of the Augusta militia. The captain presented to the Prophet an order from the Governor demanding the state arms from the Nauvoo Legion and asked if the Prophet would accompany him back to Nauvoo, for he feared the people would be reluctant to give up the arms.

72The Prophet agreed to return with him, and Hyrum sent Abram C. Hodge ahead to Carthage with a message to the Governor as to their delay.

72It was at this time that Joseph made the statement: "I am going like a lamb to the slaughter, but I am as calm as a summer's morning. I have a conscience void of offense toward God and toward all men. If they take my life, I shall die an innocent man, and my blood shall cry from the ground for vengeance, and it shall yet be said of me, 'He was murdered in cold blood!"

73In Nauvoo the arms were given up peacefully, except for one dissenter. It was a justice of the peace, Daniel H. Wells, a non-Mormon, who balked when learning the arms were being taken from the Nauvoo Legion while the militias in Carthage and Warsaw were keeping theirs. To him it was the preparation for another Missouri massacre for the Saints-and perhaps for those around them.

73While the Legion's arms were being gathered, the Prophet spread out a map and said, "Now I will show you the travels of this people." Tracing a course across the Territory of Iowa, he pointed to a spot and said, "Here you will make a place for the winter; and here you will travel west until you come to the Great Salt Lake Valley."

73One asked, "Where will you be at that time?"

73The Prophet returned, "I shall never go there."

73In the streets of Carthage, his message delivered to the Governor, Abram Hodge met the Rev. Mr. Dodge, and the minister advised Hodge to prevail upon Joseph and Hyrum not to come to Carthage. "For as surely as they do," the reverend said, "they will be killed." Leaving the minister, Hodge met Artois Hamilton, the innkeeper, who, pointing to the Carthage Greys, said, "Hodge, there are the boys that will settle you Mormons."

73Hodge replied, "Hamilton, we can take as many men as there are there out of the Nauvoo Legion and they wouldn't be missed."

73The retort angered Hamilton and he turned on his heels. But the boast was a short-lived elation for Hodge, for when he met the Prophet's party on the prairie on his return to Nauvoo, he sought out Hyrum, reported what he had heard in Carthage, and said, "Brother Hyrum, you are now clear, and if it was my duty to counsel you, I would say, do not go to Carthage."

74It was midnight when, in company with Captain Dunn's militia, the Prophet and his retinue arrived in Carthage and went to the Hamilton House. While they were passing the courthouse square the Carthage Greys became alerted to their presence and began shouting: "Where is the damned Prophet? "Let's shoot the damn Prophet!" "- - - - -- - - you, old Joe, we've got you now!" "Stand away, you McDonough boys, and let us shoot the damned Mormons." Those in the rear of the ranks threw their guns over their heads in a curve, so that the bayonets struck the ground with the breech of the guns skyward, and then retrieved them with whooping and cursing.

74The excitement woke the Governor. Going to the window, he put his head out and said: "I know your great anxiety to see Mr. Smith, which is natural enough, but it is quite too late tonight for you to have the opportunity; but I assure you, gentlemen, you shall have that privilege tomorrow morning, as I will cause him to pass before the troops upon the square, and I now wish you, with this assurance, quietly and peaceably to return to your quarters."

74A "Hurrah for Tom Ford" went up from the troops.

74Quartered also at the Hamilton House was a group of apostate Mormons working openly against the Prophet-William and Wilson Law, John A. Hicks, Henry O. Norton, Augustine Spencer, Chauncey L. and Francis M. Higbee, Sylvester Emmons, and Robert D. and Charles A. Foster.

74The next morning-Tuesday, June 25-events moved rapidly. The Mormon party surrendered themselves to Constable Bettisworth. They were released on bail for $7,500 pending the next term of the circuit court of Hancock County. In the meantime Joseph and Hyrum were served with a warrant of arrest for treason. Then the justice of the peace, Captain Robert F. Smith, before the counsel for Joseph and Hyrum could ask for a review of the treason charge, adjourned the court and left town for a few hours.

75Several officers of the different militias in the city paid Joseph and Hyrum a visit at the Hamilton House. Joseph asked them to give him their honest opinion as to anything in his appearance that might indicate he was the desperate character his enemies represented him to be.

75One of the officers replied, "No, sir, your appearance would indicate the very contrary, General Smith; but we cannot see what is in your heart, neither can we tell what are your intentions."

75"Very true, gentlemen," Joseph replied, "you cannot see what is in my heart, and you are therefore unable to judge me or my intentions; but I can see what is in your hearts, and will tell you what I see. I can see that you thirst for blood, and nothing but my blood will satisfy you. It is not for crime of any description that I and my brethren are thus continually persecuted and harassed by our enemies, but there are other motives, and some of them I have expressed, so far as relates to myself; and inasmuch as you and the people thirst for blood, I prophesy, in the name of the Lord, that you shall witness scenes of blood and sorrow to your entire satisfaction. Your souls shall be perfectly satiated with blood, and many of you who are now present shall have an opportunity to face the cannon's mouth from sources you think not of; and those people that desire this great evil upon me and my brethren, shall be filled with regret and sorrow because of the scenes of desolation and distress that await them. They shall seek for peace, and shall not be able to find it. Gentlemen, you will find what I have told you to be true."

75Though possibly none of his non-Mormon listeners understood it, the Prophet was speaking of the coming Civil War-seventeen years hence. He had spoken of it on several occasions. As early as January 1833 he had said, "And now I am prepared to say by the authority of Jesus Christ, that not many years shall pass away before the United States shall present such a scene of bloodshed as has not a parallel in the history of our country." This he had put down in a letter to N. E. Seaton, an editor of a Rochester, New York, paper. Prior to this, on Christmas Day in 1832 in Kirtland, Ohio, the Prophet stated: "Concerning the wars that will shortly come to pass, beginning at the rebellion of South Carolina, which will eventually terminate in the death and misery of many souls;Â�the Southern States shall be divided against the Northern States, and the Southern States will call on other nationsÂ�evenÂ�Great BritainÂ�.Slaves shall rise up against their masters, who shall be marshaled and disciplined for war." Later, on April 2, 1843, at a meeting at Macedonia-often called Ramus by the Mormons, and now known as Webster-he spoke of it again.

76In his political platform the Prophet had included the pledge to set all slaves free by 1850, paying out of the federal fund a fair price to their masters-this being designed, no doubt, to avert the foreseen tragic war over the question of slavery.

76The Governor, in his communication to the Prophet, had stated that to satisfy the law Joseph would have to submit to the same warrant, served by the same constable, and tried by the same judge. All this was now forgotten.

76The new justice was Robert F. Smith, an anti-Mormon. That morning his militia had rioted in the streets of Carthage following the Governor's walking their ranks with the Prophet, as he had pledged the night before. The Greys had been arrested, but when they refused to submit to the law the charge was dropped.

76While waiting for Justice Smith to return so they could have an examination on the treason charge, Joseph, Hyrum, and the counselors returned to their room. It was evening now. They had had supper and were commenting on the day's events when a knock came at the door.

77On their opening it, Constable Bettisworth entered and announced that the Smiths must go to jail. The two counselors, Woods and Reid, explained that a mittimus was needed before such action could take place, and, said they, before a mittimus could be issued, the prisoners had to be brought before a justice of the peace for an examination.

77To their surprise, the constable pulled a mittimus from his pocket.

"STATE OF ILLINOIS,) HANCOCK COUNTY,) ss.

77"The people of the State of Illinois to the keeper of the jail of said County,

"Greeting:

77"Whereas Joseph Smith and Hyrum Smith, of the county aforesaid, have been arrested upon oath of Augustine Spencer and Henry O. Norton, for the crime of treason, and have been brought before me as a justice of the peace in and for the said county, for trial at the seat of justice thereof, which trial has been necessarily postponed by reason of the absence of the material witnesses-to wit, Francis M. Higbee and others. Therefore, I command you, in the name of the people, to receive the said Joseph Smith and Hyrum Smith into your custody in the jail of the county aforesaid, there to remain until discharged by due course of law.

[SEAL] "Given under my hand and seal this 25th day of June, A.D. 1844.

(Signed) R. F. Smith J.P."

77The counselors objected to "such bare-faced, illegal, and tyrannical proceedings," and went down the hall to the Governor. Ford refused to intervene.

77So Joseph and Hyrum went to Carthage jail.

78Following them, intent that Joseph and Hyrum not be left alone, were Willard Richards, John Taylor, John P. Greene, Stephen Markham, Dan Jones, John S. Fullmer, Lorenzo D. Wasson, and a Dr. Southwick, a non-Mormon landowner from Texas and Louisiana who was visiting Nauvoo to persuade the Prophet to buy land in the new Republic of Texas.

78Markham had a large hickory cane which he called "the rascal-beater," and Jones had a smaller walking-stick, and positioning themselves on each side of Joseph and Hyrum, they beat off the drunks and others grabbing at the Mormon leaders as they covered the distance between the hotel and the jail.
