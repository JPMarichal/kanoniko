Reed H. Blake, Twenty-four Hours to Martyrdom ( 1973)

The Last Morning

5:00 a.m.

June 27, 1844. The Thursday morning sun followed a fascia of grey into blue into pink as it came up out of the east. There were more clouds in the sky than for the previous twenty-four hours and their slightly dark edges threatened another shower.

79Leaning against the pole fence that surrounded Carthage jail, Lt. Frank A. Worrell, officer of the guard, regarded the silent windows of the building's second story and pondered his job and the day-the last day of Joseph Smith.

79Before the man could be killed, four steps needed to be taken. The first was to get the leader out of Nauvoo and into Carthage, which step the Governor had performed. The second step was to disarm the Nauvoo Legion. This, also, the Governor had done. The third step was the confining of the Prophet. And that step had been accomplished by his commander, Captain Robert F. Smith, acting in his capacity as a justice of the peace.

79Now there remained only the fourth step to assure their success-the isolation of the prisoners.

79This was Worrell's job, and he moved off the fence to begin his work.

79In the Hamilton House, John P. Greene and William W. Phelps, both of whom had come to Carthage on the Nauvoo Expositor charge, arose to return to Nauvoo. While most of those freed on bail had left for Nauvoo two nights earlier, these two had stayed over as a precautionary' measure. Despite the lateness of the hour at which those in the Hamilton House had retired, the two noticed many were up and about.

80Before leaving Carthage, they called at the jail and found the Prophet and those with him just arising. It was 5: 30 A.M.

80After Greene and Phelps had left, Joseph asked Dan Jones if he would go below and inquire about the shooting of the night before. As yet, Jailer Stigall was not up, but Frank Worrell was talking to several of the guards and Jones approached him on the matter.

80For the lieutenant, the hour was too early to be agreeable and he ignored the query. Instead, he turned on Jones and said, "We have had too much trouble to bring old Joe here to let him ever escape alive, and unless you want to die with him you had better leave before sundown. You are not a damned bit better than him for taking his part, and you'll see that I can prophesy better than old Joe, for neither he nor his brother, nor anyone who will remain with them, will see the sun set today."

80The men upstairs were washing themselves and cleaning the room when Jones returned. The mattress had been rolled up and carried to the cell next door. His walking stick, along with Markham's "rascal-beater," had been put in the corner below the bed. As he tried to shut the door behind him, he remembered it wouldn't latch.

80Stepping over to the Prophet, Jones related the sharp comments of the lieutenant, and Joseph asked him if he would seek out the Governor and inform him of the officer's remarks. As he left the building to do the Prophet's bidding, he could hear the Stigall family rising.

6:00 a.m.

Slowly the rabble began filtering over to Carthage jail. Some did not stay long; others remained for hours. They joked and cursed and spat on the ground. And they told stories about another time and another day, and sometimes even they talked about their lives today, but generally it was about how tomorrow would be.

81Lt. Worrell would stay all day.

81Occasionally he would leave the grounds, but never for long. He stopped in at Hamilton's. He had his dinner at noon on the public square with the troops. He went down to the bivouac area for the four o'clock guard mount.

81The rabble walked around and talked to the guards and sat on their haunches against the pole fence, the morning sun warming their backs.

81As were other persons of notoriety on the frontier, the Governor was the subject of a long doggerel, and among the people it was popular for over a decade. Now, one along the fence struck up the tune of "Old Dan Tucker," to which the words were put, and began to sing the rhymes.

81"There's Governor Ford with mind so small He hasn't room for a soul at all; He can't be either damned or blest, If heaven or hell should do their best."

81The song fitted their mood, and among three or four of the rabble they sang all the verses.

81If one could have traced the history of those on the grounds, even at this early hour, he would have discovered most sections of the young nation represented.

81There was the twang and drawl, the abolitionist and the reactionary, the farmer and the merchant. Some were "pukes," and others "hawkeyes," and others southerners. Most were Illinois "suckers." They had crossed the Mississippi and the Des Moines, they had crossed the Ohio and the Tennessee, they had crossed the Wabash and the Missouri.

81For his own reason, each had come to Carthage.

81On the jail house grounds, as in the streets and on the square, the arms were many. Of the men not in the militias, a few carried the Model 1803, the first departure from the long barrel and small caliber in that it was a short weapon with a half-stock and caliber .54. It had proven itself well on the Meriwether Lewis and William Clark trek from St. Louis to the Pacific Coast and back.

82Other muzzle-loading arms were the "common rifle," three inches longer than the 1803; the 1841 percussion model; and the Hall breech-loading rifle. Except for the Hall, all were nearly alike. They were short, they fired a round ball, and they had seven-groove rifling to reduce the tendency of the ball to wobble and drift in flight.

82The most common weapon, the one carried by the regulars, was the U.S. flintlock musket Model 1816-sometimes called the Model 1822. Of both bright and brown finish, it was made and distributed to the frontier militias from 1817 to 1844. The caliber was .69 as in previous flintlocks, and the barrel was 42 inches long. As with other government muskets, the stock was of tough black walnut or maple, "straight grain, well seasoned, and free from sap and worm holes."

82Complete with 16-inch bayonet, the musket weighed about 10 1/2 pounds. It was the last of America's flintlocks. Only the Model 1840 followed (manufactured between 1840 and 1848), and this had such minor changes that it is unlikely any sharp distinction was made between the two. The Model 1840 ended a series of some 150,000 muskets begun half a century before.

82By modern standards, the flintlocks were a highly inaccurate weapon.

82To load one, the carrier placed the hammer at half cock, opened the pan, took a cartridge from the case at his side and, with his teeth, tore the paper. From the broken cartridge, the printing charge was poured into the open pan, and then the steel was pulled back to shut the pan. While the butt of the musket was dropped to the ground, the remaining powder was poured from the cartridge into the muzzle, followed by the ball and the wadded cartridge paper. The ramrod was drawn from beneath the barrel and used to tamp the load to the muzzle's end. The ramrod returned to its place, the musket was ready for cocking and firing.

83In this maneuver, the user was obliged to stand erect.

83Not more than four or five rounds could be fired in two minutes, and the user must have sufficient room in which to load and fire. It was this requirement of time and space in the use of the flintlock that would work in the favor of those with the Prophet.

83By military order, the flintlocks were referred to by the militiamen as "firelocks."

83Outside of the military, for the frontier man in a fight the knife was the most trusted weapon. And until the Civil War period brought a new refinement in weapons, it would remain the king of battle. In one's hand the knife was dependable. The blade never missed fire. It never blew up. It never missed its mark of its own accord. And-most important-it never was empty.

83It wasn't until the sixties and seventies, when the first fairly reliable metal-cased pistol cartridge appeared, that the day of the gun fighter dawned. Until the Civil War, even in polite society, a man was seldom without a knife. And after the 1830s it was almost certain to be a bowie knife. This knife varied in length as a personal preference, the front blade honed to a razor edge, the point curved concavely and sharpened back about two inches for ripping, the reverse edge cast in soft brass to catch and hold an opponent's knife.

7:00 a.m.

On the jailer's call, five of the men-Joseph, Hyrum, Richards, Stephen Markham, and John Fullmer-went down to the dining room for breakfast. They informed the jailer that Dan Jones was at the Hamilton House and John Taylor would not be down. By his son, Stigall sent food up to Taylor.

84Breakfasting with them was a Mr. Crane, who asked the Prophet if it were true that he had fainted three times while the Governor was exhibiting him to the troops on Tuesday morning. The Prophet smiled, and one of the others said it was a false report. "The town is full of rumors," Stigall said.

84This brought back to the jailer's mind the mobbers' plan to attack Nauvoo two weeks earlier, and he recounted what he had told the prisoners about this the previous morning. "They sent runners into all the surrounding counties and across the river to Missouri. They calculated on about nine thousand showing, but only about two hundred came." He got up from the table; and not seeming to talk to anyone other than himself, he said, "It's a mean time."

84The men finished their meal and, as with each meal, paid the jailer. Then they returned to the upstairs room. It was nearing the hour of eight.

84His brother and his secretary noticed that Joseph was unusually silent.

84Abruptly the Governor looked tired. For nearly an hour now the meeting had been going. If at the beginning one had assumed this to be just another in a series of meetings that had come and gone since the Chief Executive's arrival in Carthage, he would be mistaken.

84To begin with, to be sure, it had seemed like just another meeting-the Governor announcing he wanted to once again take under consideration the matter of marching to Nauvoo with a great show of strength. At once the officers, as on other occasions when wanting a desired course of action from Ford, began putting pressure on him. And, as in the past, without much resistance, the Governor had sustained the resolution to march.

84Of his thinking on the matter, Ford later wrote:

84"Two or three days' preparations had been made for this expedition. I observed that some of the people became more and more excited and inflammatory the further the preparations were advanced. Occasional threats came to my ears of destroying the city [Nauvoo] and murdering or expelling the inhabitants.

85"I had no objection [to going]Â�if I could have been assured of my command against mutiny and insubordination. But I gradually learned, to my entire satisfaction, that there was a plan to get the troops into Nauvoo, and there to begin the war, probably by some of our own party, or some of the seceding Mormons,Â�and then lay it on the Mormons. I was satisfied that there were those amongst us fully capable of such an act, hoping that in the alarm, bustle, and confusion of a militia camp, the truth could not be discovered, and that it might lead to the desired collision."

85Reviewing this thinking with those in the room, the Governor had concluded, "Â�that such wanton and unprovoked barbarity on their part would turn the sympathy of the people in the surrounding counties in favor of the Mormons, and therefore it would be impossible to raise a volunteer militia force to protect such a people against them."

85A vote was taken, and the Governor had lost. The officers from the Schuyler and McDonough counties militias had voted with the Governor, and the officers from the militias gathered from Hancock County had voted against him.

85The talk had then centered itself on the Prophet's political career. His views on government, it was reported, were widely circulated and "took like wildfire." If the Prophet did not get into the presidential chair this election, they said, he would be sure to do so the next time. If they banded together to kill the Mormon leader, they would not be brought to justice.

85And so it went, back and forth across the room.

85Then the Governor had stood. Notwithstanding a majority of the officers had voted for a military display in Nauvoo, he was against it, he had said. But as a concession to their wishes, he would lead a force to Nauvoo for the purpose of searching for the "counterfeit plant," and, as the officers insisted, "more particularly, to terrify the Mormons from attempting any open or secret measure of vengeance against the citizens of the county who had taken part against them and their leaders." However, he said, the force would not be a large one-only Captain Dunn's company of Union Dragoons from Augusta, the small township in the extreme southeast of Hancock County. Of the other militias-with the exception of the two companies in Carthage, the Greys and the Riflemen, they to guard the jail-all other companies were to be disbanded immediately by their several commanders.

86There was instant commotion in the room. Captain Smith of the Greys had jumped to his feet, but he had been unheard.

86Now the Governor sank back into his chair. Of a sudden he looked tired. From his sitting position, he asked for their cooperation, and his voice seemed to plead. There in the room with its shafts of morning light half hiding him from their view, it seemed he wished the council over.

86To the officers who wanted to march to Nauvoo, the order was more of the "Ford reasoning."

86And then, as if it were magnetically communicated to the retinue about him, the import of the Governor's orders became apparent.

86The troops were to be disbanded in Carthage!

86Not marched home, as was the standard procedure, and then disbanded. But disbanded in Carthage, where, once the level-headed and the responsible returned home, the residue would become a lawless mob.

86The embroilment ended.

86As the officers filed past him to leave, Ford watched closely their faces to see how they took the news. Satisfied, he stood.

86In the far end of the room another man stood also and walked to the entrance. It struck the Governor he had not noticed the man before. The face was familiar, yet he could not place it. But he had seen a lot of faces in Carthage. Once alone in his room, the Governor checked his watch. It was a few minutes past eight. He went to his basin, poured water into it, and covered his flushed face. The water felt good; he cupped his hands again. Then he buried his face in a towel, and as he did so, it came to him.

87The man was the doctor from Texas.

8:00 a.m.

If Dan Jones had known the Governor of Illinois better, perhaps he would have made a request for a pass first and reserved the giving of his opinions until such pass was given him, but he didn't. He spoke first and requested second, and his request was denied.

87On the square, while waiting to see the Governor, Jones had been attracted by a large crowd of men grouped about a speaker. On his approaching, someone said the speaker had just returned from a council in the hotel, and the Governor had changed his plans again. Jones pushed his way forward.

87"Our troops will be discharged this morning in obedience to orders, and for a sham we will leave the town," the speaker shouted. "But when the Governor and the McDonough troops have left, we will return and kill those men, if we have to tear the jail down!"

87A shout went up from the crowd. Jones went into the hotel.

87In the Governor's chambers, he told of what the officer of the guard had said earlier and of what he had heard in the street only minutes before, and charged the Governor to avert the danger.

87Ford replied, "You are unnecessarily alarmed for the safety of your friends, sir, the people are not that cruel."

87The reply irritated Jones. The guards at the jail were professed assassins, he returned, and should be replaced in spite of the Governor's thinking. "The Messrs. Smith are American citizens, and have surrendered themselves to your Excellency upon your pledging your honor for their safety; they are also Master Masons, and as such I demand of you protection of their lives."

88The Governor's face turned pale, and Jones continued: "If you do not do this, I have but one more desire, and that is if you leave their lives in the hands of those men to be sacrificed-"

88"What is that, sir?" Ford asked, catching it up.

88"It is," said the Welshman, "that the Almighty will preserve my life to a proper time and place, that I may testify that you have been timely warned of their danger."

88The meeting over, the Governor turned his back, and Jones left the hotel.

88He returned to the jail, but the guard would not let him enter.

88Joseph's love for his family was deep. And now with Mills and Wheelock soon to depart for Nauvoo, he felt the need to write another letter home. He brought Richards to his side to prepare the letter to Emma, who was now four months heavy with child.

"Carthage Jail, June 27th, 1844 20 minutes past eight A.M.

88"Dear Emma.-The Governor continues his courtesies, and permits us to see our friends. We hear this morning that the Governor will not go down with his troops to Nauvoo, as we anticipated last evening; but if he does come down with his troops you will be protected; and I want you to tell Brother Dunham [acting commander, Nauvoo Legion] to instruct the people to stay at home and attend to their own business, and let there be no groups or gathering together, unless by permission of the Governor, they are called together to receive communications from the Governor, which would please our people, but let the Governor direct.

89"Brother Dunham of course will obey the orders of the government officers, and render them the assistance they require. There is no danger of any extermination order. Should there be a mutiny among the troops (which we do not anticipate, excitement is abating) a part will remain loyal and stand for the defense of the state and our rights.

89"There is one principle which is eternal; it is the duty of all men to protect their lives and the lives of the household, whenever necessity requires, and no power has a right to forbid it, should the last extreme arrive, but I anticipate no such extreme, but caution is the parent of safety.

"Joseph Smith.

89"P.S.-Dear Emma, I am very much resigned to my lot, knowing I am justified, and have done the best that could be done. Give my love to the children and all my friends, Mr. Brewer, and all who inquire after me; and as for treason, I know that I have not committed any, and they cannot prove anything of the kind, so you need not have any fears that anything can happen to us on that account. May God bless you all. Amen."

89When the secretary finished, Joseph took the letter and read it over. Then he forced a smile at Richards and walked to the window. Hyrum joined him, and the two watched for a while the rabble in the yard below.

9:00 a.m.

Dismounting, John P. Greene wiped the sweat from his horse's shoulder where it had come up in a foam. He didn't unsaddle. In his coat pocket he carried subpoenas for witnesses for the trial on Saturday. When he entered the city there was a stillness in Nauvoo he had never before known. He nodded to the few men standing on the steps of the Masonic Hall and went inside to find General Dunham of the Legion.

90Prior to his leaving Carthage he had told the Governor there was a conspiracy to take the Prophet's life, if the Governor should go to Nauvoo. Ford returned, "Marshal Greene, you are too enthusiastic."

90He hoped the Governor was right.

90 Dan Jones sat upon the pole fence on the east side of Carthage jail. Occasionally he could see the men in the second story room as they moved about. He watched also the guards on the grounds. The mount was in an ugly mood. At times even they threatened among themselves. Surveying the situation, the Welshman knew it best not to antagonize the Greys for entrance to the prisoners' quarters. He would again try to enter when the guard was changed. Perhaps the new mount would be less severe.

90He waited.

90The sun had come up bright orange over the town, and as he studied the sky, he decided it would not rain today. If it didn't, it would make for the area three days without heavy rain. With the sun so strong the earth would dry fast. It could be another good year for the farmer yet, he concluded.

90Presently Jones saw the Prophet approach the window and open it completely. Sitting on the wide sill, Joseph beckoned to him, and Jones left the fence and stood beneath the window.

90It was deemed best, the Prophet said, that since the trial had been postponed, those with him and Hyrum should have passes for moving to and from the jail without undue restraint. Particularly did he desire a pass for his secretary, Richards. Would Jones petition the Governor for such passes?

90Jones concurred and left the yard.

91In front of the Hamilton House the Augusta militia had formed for the expedition to Nauvoo. The horses were pawing the ground and swatting flies with their tails.

91To the front he could see the Governor and General Deming by the picket fence. The Governor saw him too, as he approached, but did not smile.

91At the outset, the Mormon could see Ford was still displeased with their earlier conversation. Nevertheless, he was displeased with the Governor, so the scale was balanced.

91According to Ford's promise to the prisoners, Jones reported, he was requesting passes in and out of the jail for his friends, particularly Dr. Richards and himself; and as he had feared, the Governor refused. The young riverboat captain again repeated his mission, and again the Governor refused. Then to Jones' surprise, Ford turned to the general and instructed the latter to issue a pass to "one Dr. Willard Richards, Joseph Smith's private secretary."

91From the steps of the Hamilton House, Chauncey L. Higbee, one of the excommunicated Mormons staying at the hotel, and an avowed enemy of the Prophet, had watched Jones as he came up North Main from the town square to seek out the Governor. It bothered him to have the Prophet's confidant engaged in conversation with the Chief Executive. If he had known of the breach between Ford and the Prophet's lieutenant, perhaps he would not have been so concerned, but he didn't, and now he moved off the steps to where the three men stood.

91When Higbee reached the trio he discovered he need not attempt to recover any ground lost to the Mormon. And so he waited until the general had completed the pass; for if the pass meant that Jones could come and go as he pleased, it would hinder their efforts.

91General Deming completed the pass and handed it to the Governor who quickly read it, put his signature to it, and passed it on to Jones.

91"Permit Dr. Richards, the private secretary of Joseph Smith, to be with him, if he desires it, and to pass and repass the guard.

"June 27th, 1844 Thomas Ford, Commander-in-Chief"

92As the Welshman reached the square on his return to the jail, Higbee drew alongside. "We are determined to kill Joe and Hyrum," he said, "and you had better go away to save yourself."

92Jones did not tell Higbee the pass was for the doctor.

92It was 9:40 A.M. when the two counselors, Woods and Reid, arrived at the jail and informed Joseph that after another consultation with his officers the Governor was going to Nauvoo. Shortly thereafter the doctor from Texas arrived, said he had attended the meeting, and told the group what had taken place.

92He said it disturbed him that the troops were being disbanded in Carthage.

92Joseph and the others seemed little interested in the details of the meeting, but the doctor found a listener in Stephen Markham, to whom he gave a complete account.

92After learning that the Governor was, after all, going to Nauvoo that day, Joseph added, by the hand of his secretary, a second postscript to his letter:

92"P.S.-20 minutes to 10.-I just learned that the Governor is about to disband his troops, all but a guard to protect us and the peace, and come himself to Nauvoo and deliver a speech to the people. This is right as I suppose."

92When Richards concluded, the Prophet took the letter and added a few lines with his own hand which were not copied.

10:00 a.m.

It had been a few minutes past the hour of eight when Cyrus H. Wheelock, at the Prophet's request, applied to the Governor and obtained the following passes:

93"Suffer Mr. C. H. Wheelock to pass in to visit General Joseph Smith and friends in Carthage jail unmolested.

"June 27th, 1844 Thomas Ford, Governor and Commander-in-Chief"

93"Protect Mr. C. H. Wheelock in passing to and from Carthage and Nauvoo.

"June 27th, 1844 Thomas Ford, Governor and Commander-in-Chief"

93In this respect Wheelock was more adroit than his companion Dan Jones. It was not until after he had secured the passes that he said: "Sir, you must be aware by this time that the prisoners have no fears in relation to any lawful demands made against them, but you have heard sufficient to justify you in the belief that their enemies would destroy them if they had them in their power; and now, sir, I am about to leave for Nauvoo, and I fear for those men; they are safe as regards the law, but they are not safe from the hands of traitors and midnight assassins who thirst for their blood and have determined to spill it; and under these circumstances I leave with a heavy heart."

93Ford expelled a heavy breath of air and replied: "I was never in such a dilemma in my life; but your friends shall be protected and have a fair trial by the law; in this pledge I am not alone; I have obtained the pledge of the whole of the army to sustain me."

93The Governor's assurances somewhat relieved Wheelock, and he had gone to his room in the Hamilton House to prepare for his return to Nauvoo. As the morning threatened rain, he elected to wear his overcoat, and as he put it on he felt the weight of a six-shooter he had been carrying in the side pocket.

94It did not occur to him that even with a pass he would be subject to search once at the jail, and so he left the revolver in the pocket rather than transferring it to his saddle roll.

94Leaving the hotel, he rode down North Main to the northwest corner of the city square, then north one block to Walnut Street, and hence west to the jail.

94He dismounted briskly, giving his horse over to the jailer's son, and, without stopping to distinguish between the two, handed the outside guard both his passes. The guard was momentarily baffled by the two papers, and he read them a second time before he realized one was for passing to and from the jail and the second for passing to and from Nauvoo. Embarrassed at his delay, the guard handed the papers back to their owner and opened the door. It was not until the man had ascended the stairs that the guard remembered he had failed to search the visitor. But he shrugged it off. It was just so much formality anyway.

94Now, about to take his leave from the jail and return to Nauvoo, Wheelock remembered the revolver, and taking it from his coat pocket he handed it to the Prophet. Joseph examined it closely for a moment, and then raising his eyes to Wheelock, asked if he did not think he had better retain it for his own use. The other returned that he didn't feel the necessity-he would be in Nauvoo in a few hours and could get another firearm there if the need arose.

94Earlier in the day, John S. Fullmer had left a single-barrel pistol with the Prophet before leaving the jail. He had brought the pistol in with him when the men had been committed to jail two nights previously, the men at the time not being searched.

94It was this single-shot pistol that Joseph handed to Hyrum saying, "You may have need for this."

95His brother was reluctant to take it, but did so, returning, "I hate to use such things or to see them used."

95"So do I," replied Joseph softly, "but we may have to, to defend ourselves." Joseph then placed the six-shooter in his pantaloons pocket.

95Actually, the revolver given Joseph belonged to John Taylor. Taylor had given it to Wheelock a few days before when the two were preparing for a trip. Reported Taylor: "The pistol was a six-shooting revolver, of Allen's patent; it belonged to me, and was one I furnished to Brother Wheelock when he talked of going with me to the east, previous to our coming to Carthage."

95The revolver looked like a bent pipe. It had six barrels. The barrel in the early models was revolved by hand, but now in the double-action, or self-cocking, gun this was accomplished by a single pull on the trigger. New to the frontier-it was patented in 1837 it was called a pepperbox and was the fastest firing revolver of its day. Despite its popularity, it had serious defects. Worst of all was its inaccuracy. A second defect was its tendency to fire several barrels at once as the flash from one charge spread over and ignited the others. As with all multi-barreled arms of that day, it was clumsy and muzzle-heavy, and once the six shots were expended, it took considerable time and effort to reload. The barrel of the pepperbox, which ended at the trigger guard, measured three and three-fourths inches. It was a caliber .30 percussion model.

95After giving the single-shot to Hyrum, the Prophet turned his attention once again to Wheelock. He instructed Wheelock to have the commanders of the Nauvoo Legion avoid all military display or other movement that might produce excitement during the Governor's visit. Moreover, said Joseph, he especially charged him to use all the influence he possessed to have the brethren and friends in Nauvoo remain perfectly calm and quiet inasmuch as they respected the feelings and well-being of both him and Hyrum.

96"Our lives have already become jeopardized by revealing the wicked and bloodthirsty purposes of our enemies; and for the future we must cease to do so. All we have said about them is truth, but it is not always wise to relate all the truth. Even Jesus, the Son of God, had to refrain from doing so, and had to restrain His feelings many times for the safety of himself and his followers, and had to conceal the righteous purposes of his heart in relation to many things pertaining to his Father's kingdom.

96"When still a boy he had all the intelligence necessary to enable him to rule and govern the kingdom of the Jews, and could reason with the wisest and most profound doctors of law and divinity, and make their theories and practice to appear like folly compared with the wisdom he possessed; but he was a boy only, and lacked physical strength even to defend his own person, and was subject to cold, to hunger, and to death.

96"So it is with The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints; we have the revelation of Jesus, and the knowledge within us is sufficient to organize a righteous government upon the earth, and to give universal peace to all mankind, if they would receive it, but we lack the physical strength, as did our Savior when a child, to defend our principles, and we have of necessity to be afflicted, persecuted and smitten, and to bear it patiently until Jacob is of age, then he will take care of himself."

96When the Prophet concluded, they drew up a list of names for witnesses for the Saturday trial. On reading it over, Hyrum struck the names of a number of men, among which were Alpheus Cutler and Reynolds Cahoon, saying he deemed it unnecessary, for them to attend.

96"And why should they not come?" questioned Joseph.

96"They may be very good men, but they don't know enough to answer a question properly," returned his brother.

96"That is sufficient reason," Joseph said.

97By Wheelock, those in the room sent many verbal messages to their families in Nauvoo. In time the messages became so numerous that Dr. Richards proposed writing them down, lest Wheelock forget one, but Hyrum, fastening his eyes upon Wheelock, and with a look of penetration, said, "Brother Wheelock will remember all that we tell him, and he will never forget the occurrences of this day."

97When the men finished with their messages, the Prophet spoke of a dream he had had during the night; at its conclusion, however, he did not elaborate.

97"I was back in Kirtland, Ohio, and thought I would take a walk out by myself, and view my old farm, which I found grown up with weeds and brambles, and altogether bearing evidence of neglect and want of culture. I went into the barn, which I found without floor or doors, with the weather-boarding off, and was altogether in keeping with the farm.

97"While I viewed the desolation around me, and was contemplating how it might be recovered from the curse upon it, there came rushing into the barn a company of furious men, who commenced to pick a quarrel with me.

97"The leader of the party ordered me to leave the barn and farm, stating it was none of mine, and that I must give up all hope of ever possessing it.

97"I told him the farm was given me by the Church, and although I had not had any use for it for some time back, still I had not sold it, and according to righteous principles it belonged to me or the Church.

97"He then grew furious and began to rail upon me, and threaten me, and said it never did belong to me nor to the Church.

97"I then told him that I did not think it worth contending about, that I had no desire to live upon it in its present state, and if he thought he had a better right I would not quarrel with him about it but leave; but my assurance that I would not trouble him at present did not seem to satisfy him, as he seemed determined to quarrel with me, and threatened me with the destruction of my body.

98"While he was thus engaged, pouring out his bitter words upon me, a rabble rushed in and nearly filled the barn, drew out their knives, and began to quarrel among themselves for the premises, and for a moment forgot me, at which time I took the opportunity to walk out of the barn about up to my ankles in mud.

98"When I was a little distance from the barn, I heard them screeching and screaming in a very distressed manner, as it appeared they had engaged in a general fight with their knives. While they were thus engaged, the dream or vision ended."

98Following his relation of the dream, Joseph bore his testimony-as did his brother Hyrum-to the latter-day work and the coming forth of the Book of Mormon and prophesied of the triumph of the gospel over all the earth, and exhorted those present to faithfulness and persevering diligence in proclaiming the gospel, building up the temple, and performing all the duties connected with their holy religion.

98Thomas Ford, the Governor of Illinois, rode northwest across the prairie; behind him trailed Captain Dunn's dragoons from Augusta and a baggage wagon. It had been a difficult time for him since leaving Springfield. More than at any time in his life he felt the pressure of popular opinion. Because of it, he at times felt more like a pawn in this chess game of life and death than the Governor of the state.

98Presently he muttered to Colonel Buckmaster, the quartermaster-general, "Upon the whole, if one-half of these reports are true the Mormon community must be the most intolerable collection of rogues ever assembled; or, if one-half of them false, the most maligned and abused."

98Given this opening, the colonel relayed to the Governor his uneasiness at leaving the Smiths behind with the Carthage Greys. He had been with the dragoons in Nauvoo when Captain Dunn had gathered the Mormon arms; he had watched the mob spirit in Carthage. He was fearful of the situation.

99His apprehension was enough to cause the Governor to halt the troops, now four miles out on the prairie, and send a message back to Captain Smith "to guard the jail strictly, and at the peril of his life, until I return."

99The message was indicative of the Governor's instability as he wavered from one course to another on the Mormon problem. There was a plan afoot to kill the Smiths in a mock escape try, a plan to which the Governor never fully committed himself, yet neither did he demand its abolition. Earlier the Mormon leader had disclosed to him the Saints' intention of moving to the Rocky Mountains, the entire body to be out of the state within two years.

99He had presented this proposed movement to the council during one of the meetings in the Hamilton House, saying he favored this plan. Some had expressed their doubt as to the actuality of the plan-why did they continue to build an expensive temple if they didn't plan to stay?-and said the proposal was only a delaying tactic on the part of Smith. Others said two years was too long-rid the state of them now! Still others advocated the destruction of the Mormon Church. "Kill the Smiths and you kill the Church."

99After the fervor died, he had again pushed the idea, but to no avail. Ridding the state of the Saints, he now realized, was not the entire goal. The "antis" wanted the Mormon spoils. An orderly exodus would have meant the disposal of Mormon homes, land, and businesses by sale. But to drive the Mormons out meant that the "antis" would get their property for the price of a wad of shot.

99With the message to Captain Smith on its way to Carthage, the Governor signalled the troops to continue their march.

99In Nauvoo, while the Governor's party was halted on the prairie, Captain Singleton issued to the Nauvoo Legion orders for that portion of the militia within the city limits to prepare for a review at noon.

Samuel H. Smith was one of the eight witnesses whose signed testimony is found in the preface to the Book of Mormon that he viewed and handled the gold plates from which the book was translated. Living in Plymouth, in the southeast section of Hancock County, thirty-four miles from Nauvoo and sixteen miles from Carthage, he did not hear of his brothers' imprisonment until the morning of the 27th when, with a fourteen-year-old boy who was working for him at the time, he immediately set out for Carthage by team and wagon.

100In the state of New York where the LDS Church had been organized, the law called for six members for incorporation, and Samuel Harrison Smith was one of those six original members of the infant Church. At once he left home to preach the new gospel and thus became the Church's first missionary. Samuel was thirty-six and the fourth son in the Smith family. His first wife Mary, by whom he had four children, died in January 1841, and later in that year he married Levira Clark, by whom he had two children, and who several weeks after the martyrdom would bear him another child he would not live to see.

100Beginning to notice the day's heat as the morning wore on, Samuel was just turning to remark about it to the boy when he was motioned to stop by a picket line of disbanded militiamen, now a part of the Carthage mobbers. Taken by surprise by the sudden action, Samuel at first talked freely to those about him and when on questioning the mobbers learned he was the Prophet's brother, they grabbed the team and ordered him back.

100"The less Mormons in town the better," said one, and another added, "Three Smiths would be more than any town could stomach." The men laughed. Following that, in an effort to antagonize, the first said, "We'll let the boy through, but not you." And then as if in answer to Samuel's unasked question, concluded, "It'll keep you from getting killed."

101To their surprise, Samuel took them up on the proposition and gave the boy instruction to proceed into the city, feeling, perhaps, that the sight of his wagon might bring inquiries by those aligned with the Mormons and thus news that he was coming. Once in Carthage the boy was to go directly to the Hamilton House and wait for him there.

101Outwardly shaken, the boy asked, "You'll be back?"

101 Samuel assured him he would.

101"You'll not get through to Carthage, Smith," the first guard broke in. "We'll kill you first."

101The boy passed on, and Samuel began to dog-trot some thirteen miles back to Plymouth.

101As Samuel jogged along, the seriousness of the situation in Carthage began to reveal itself, and it wasn't until he began to cough that he realized he was running full strength. His legs felt heavy, and a burn had come up in his chest-which told him he should rest-but his anxiety was such that he could not bring himself to stop.

101Meanwhile the boy pulled up in front of the Hamilton House. The hordes of people milling in the streets, and the shouting and curses that accompanied them, dismayed the boy, and he hunkered down in the wagon to await his employer.

101On the square was the makeshift bivouac of the Carthage Greys; men in and out of uniform clustered in groups of twos and threes, tens and twenties, drinking, scuffing, swearing. In the square about them were the horses and wagons of the usual camp-followers, the hangers-on, the curious, and in many wagons the wives, and sometimes the families, of the men pressed into service. To the south and east were the camps of the McDonough, Schuyler, and Augusta militias, from which a steady stream of men and women, boys and dogs passed in and out of the square.

102Another ant line extended to the Hamilton House, where many of the officers and other interested civilians were quartered, adding themselves to those who walked about, their business important or unimportant as it could only be judged by themselves. Some hollered, "Hey, you Warsaw boys," and others hollered, "Pass the whiskey."

102 Samuel Smith's young farm hand felt strangely alone. He had been to Nauvoo on occasion and knew many of the people there by face, if not by name. And now he began looking for a citizen of Nauvoo.

102But the town of Carthage seemed denuded of Mormons.

102The Governor's party was now eight miles out on the prairie. Since sending the courier back to Carthage, the Governor had been silent, lost in his thoughts. As he retraced the events of the last few days-the several meetings held, the disposition of the people-perhaps he concluded that he also was to be a part of the coming murder plans. On this point, writing later of his reactions soon after the assassination, he wrote:

103"As for myself I was well convinced that those, whoever they were, who assassinated the Smiths, meditated in turn my assassination by the Mormons. The very circumstances of the case fully corroborated the information which I afterwards received, that upon consultation of the assassins it was agreed amongst them that the murder must be committed whilst the Governor was at Nauvoo; that the Mormons would naturally suppose that he had planned it; and that in the first outpouring of their indignation they would assassinate him by way of retaliation; and that thus they would get clear of the Smiths and the Governor all at once. They also supposed, that if they could so contrive the matter as to have the Governor of the state assassinated by the Mormons, the public excitement would be greatly increased against that people, and would result in their expulsion from the state at least."

103Whatever his real reasons, Ford called a second halt to the march. The baggage wagons were ordered to remain where they were until evening, then return to Carthage. In the meantime, the Governor would hasten to Nauvoo with the dragoons, address the citizens, and return to Carthage that night. Cancelled were the plans calling for the search for counterfeit money and an examination of the other charges against the Mormons.

103William M. Daniels was from Augusta. He had arrived in Warsaw a few days earlier on his way to Quincy, and finding that river city in a state of excitement, he stayed. On the twenty-seventh he had joined the rifle company under the command of Captain Jacob Davis, and now, with the Warsaw militia, he was marching to Nauvoo.

103Behind him were the streets of Warsaw, full of people. When he had assembled, Thomas Sharp, astraddle his big bay horse, was at the front of the force shouting orders to his company as the men mustered in the streets, the other captains behind him assembling their commands: Captain William N. Grover, a lawyer who would later, after leaving Illinois, become a United States attorney for Missouri; Captain Jacob C. Davis, a lawyer and at the time a member of the state senate, and who later would become a member of Congress; and Captain Mark Aldrich, a Warsaw civic leader who would later make the rush to the California gold fields and still later drift into Arizona and serve two terms in the state legislature.

104The four Warsaw companies were under the command of Colonel Levi Williams of Green Plains, a week-day farmer and a Sunday minister-as were most of the early frontier preachers.

104Outside of the officers in command, Daniels knew few of his fellows-in-arms. When one of the men made a speech calling for the death of the Smiths and twenty volunteers to go to Carthage to do the job, Daniels knew only the speech-maker, W. W. Chittenden, as one of the Chittenden brothers, of which there were four. And when the twenty men left the main body, bearing right toward Carthage, he knew only that the group contained Houck, a tailor, and Stephens, a cooper. The rest of the men, as was the main body of the Warsaw force numbering between four and five hundred, were strangers to him.

104Golden's Point, the place of rendezvous with the Governor's militia, was a few scattered stone houses off the Mississippi River eight miles south of Nauvoo where a point of timber projected into the prairie. As the men marched toward it their spirits were high. The flat land of the prairie lay broad before them, and above it the summer sun came down strong.

104The intermittent patches of prairie mud sucked heavy on the boots of William Daniels. In time he noticed that the excitement in the ranks had given way to the task of covering the miles that lay before them. It was nearly noon when he sighted some shanties among some scattered trees ahead. The sun was warmer now and the moistness of the earth before the sun's force seemed to crowd in around him, causing his clothes to cling to his body. Pulling a handkerchief from his pocket, he patted his brow and ran it along his neck beneath his collar.

105Off to the right he could see a speck that looked like a horseman approaching.

105Why the guards let Almon W. Babbitt pass into Carthage jail is not known. Perhaps it was that he was not known to be a Mormon, or that Babbitt convinced the guards he did not intend to stay long in Carthage. At any rate, at 11:30 he had passed up the stairs and entered the second-floor bedroom.

105Why Babbitt was in Carthage is only conjecture, but one might surmise that it was to determine the Prophet's feelings on his refusal to defend the Mormon leader in court. Indeed, his reply that he was already engaged on the other side, when contacted the day before by John Smith, was only his flippant way of evading a matter in which he did not want to become involved. Babbitt had had positions in both Church and Hancock County affairs, and financially was one of the more prosperous citizens. Yet in time he would turn his back on Hancock County and join with those Saints making the trek to the Great Basin.
