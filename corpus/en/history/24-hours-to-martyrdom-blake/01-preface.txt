Reed H. Blake, Twenty-four Hours to Martyrdom ( 1973)

Preface

In looking back on it, this was an enjoyable book to do. It deals with an interesting time and with interesting people. And as a result of the experience, I came to love the "American Prophet" and his wife Emma. Most of the material reviewed, quite naturally, did not find a place in the manuscript, for the story itself is concerned with only a twenty-four-hour period. Nevertheless, considerable material was reviewed, the conclusion from which is that Joseph Smith is a great man by any standard.

ixThis is not the full story concerning the last day of Joseph Smith, but it is a story based on what facts are available. A word about those facts: the first thing that struck this researcher is that for such an important and dramatic an event as the martyrdom there is really a scarcity of information. (Most of what is written and published is simply a rehearsal of someone else's writing. Original documents are hard to come by.) The second thing is that the information that is available is all too often biased in some direction or another.

ixUnder these limitations, I tried to thread my way through the records to arrive at what really happened. No doubt in doing this I have made errors. Hopefully, these errors are on the side of caution and not on the side of sensationalism. In working over the material, I asked three questions of it: Is it supported by other material? Does it "fit" the events surrounding it? What are the biases which it represents?

ixAs a result of these questions, there are some popular versions of incidents in this twenty-four-hour period that are missing. Perhaps the most obvious will be that story concerning the events immediately after the Prophet's death while he is in the jailyard surrounded by mobbers. This version includes a flash of heavenly light and some mobbers rendered immobile. It was felt necessary to omit this from the manuscript because all who recorded such a happening drew on the testimony of one man. Of the people on the jailyard grounds at the time, no other person was discovered attesting to these phenomena. Further, while this testimony did come from a non-Mormon, his recounting of the events of that day displayed a pro-Mormon bias.

xOther examples could be cited.

xPart of the methodology also included a large chart with the hours of the day running in one direction across the top and the names of the participants-significant and insignificant-running a second direction down the side. Then, as the research progressed, each person's actions were logged according to time and place. In time this chart became a central clearinghouse, so to speak, for sorting out and reconciling conflicting information. The end result was a firm feeling on the part of the author that the time sequencing here shown for each person is very close to what really happened-though some widely published reports do indeed differ with this account by as much as an hour or more-and that what this book records as having taken place, did, in fact, occur.

xThis book is more properly a historical novel than it is a history book. As a historical novel, however, it falls far short-for it lacks the depth and interpretation which an author is permitted (expected) to bring to that kind of writing. This lack of interpretation was a deliberate stance on my part.

xThe intent of this book is to provide the reader with an accurate historical chronicle of the events of this dramatic twenty-four-hour period in a journalistic style.

xIt does not pretend to interpret the events or the characters involved in these events. It did, however, seem appropriate to provide a degree of low-grade interpretation. Accordingly, the author has supplied facial expressions, hand movements, and the like, in some instances. In still fewer instances, direct-quote dialogue has been supplied to help carry the narrative along. But as has been indicated, this is infrequent, and in no case was it felt to be central to the machinations of this period. In sum, I view this book as a small history.

xiThis history begins with the six o'clock hour on June 26 and ends at the same time on June 27. Because of this narrow time span it was felt advisable to include a chapter ("The Days Before") which would give the reader some feel for the time, place, and circumstances in which the martyrdom took place. Similarly, a postscript was added in the form of a chapter entitled "The Hours After" to provide a degree of closure on the many loose ends left dangling at the end of the twenty-fourth hour.

xiIn doing these two chapters, as well as the others, it was the scarcity of information that made the research engrossing. When there are not many facts at hand, the discovery of some small detail in an old county history, for instance, can brighten a day as few things can. Several examples come to mind; permit me to cite two here. In reading Littlefield's The Martyrs, I found a piece of information to the effect that Thomas Sharp had a big bay horse ("Thomas C. Sharp mounted his 'big bay horse,' and made an inflammatory speech to the companies,Â�"). So this almost esoteric piece of news found itself in the manuscript-undoubtedly bringing more joy to the man who put it there than to any reader of the passages in which it appears. The second example is similar to the first, but of greater consequence. It was late at night and I was going over Gregg's History of Hancock County, published thirty-six years after the martyrdom. The author was dealing with some anecdotal material, and had inserted this comment:

xi"At Fountain Green, at the store of C. C. Tyler, we were shown the journal belonging to the firm of Tyler & McClaughey (Stephen H. Tyler and Matthew McClaughey) doing business as merchants in 1844, in that place both now deceased. In it is a memorandum in the hand-writing of C. C. Tyler's father. then the bookkeeper, in the following words:

xii"'This night, at five o'clock p.m., Joseph Smith and Hyrum Smith, his brother, were mobbed and shot at Carthage, Ill'" Dated June 27, 1844."

xiiI knew then-while still in the middle of the research-that I had the book's ending, that I had the last paragraph for the twenty-fourth hour. As I recall, that bit of information did not just brighten what remained of that day, it brightened the whole week.

xiiI say "recall," because I wrote this book ten years ago. Or more properly, I concluded it ten years ago. Shortly after its completion, however, another book about this time and place appeared in print-and as anyone knows, there is a book market just as there are other markets. Thus The Carthage Tragedy, as my manuscript was then titled, was tucked away. And little thought of, as new interests and pursuits came about.

xiiMany stories have sequels, and this is one of them. When reviewing the manuscript, Marvin Wallin, the publisher of Bookcraft, Inc., indicated his desire to publish the manuscript at a later date. While I gave little consideration to this at the time, Marvin Wallin did not. Hence, in the first days of 1973, I received a letter from Bookcraft. It said in effect, "Let's publish."

xiiNow that's the kind of publisher an author likes to work with.

xiiOne final note. When one does a piece of writing, he should leave it for a period of time and then return to it, for the return brings with it a new perspective. Certainly, ten years is more than adequate in this regard. But the interesting thing here-to me, at least-is that after ten years I did not change the manuscript; it went to press as it was written a decade ago.

xiiMy thanks in this endeavor go to many. To the staffs of the following libraries: The Historical Department of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, the Salt Lake City Public Library, the University of Utah Library, the Brigham Young University Library, the Utah State Historical Library, the Washington County (Utah) Library, and the editorial libraries of The Instructor and The Improvement Era magazines. Also to my wife Katie Dean Hall Blake for her assistance in the manuscript's preparation. Finally, to the editor at Bookcraft, George Bickerstaff, for his able assistance, including the book's new-and better-title, and to the manager of Bookcraft, Marvin Wallin.
