Reed H. Blake, Twenty-four Hours to Martyrdom ( 1973)

Introduction

The cities of Nauvoo, Warsaw, and Carthage, in Hancock County, Illinois, form a triangle, each city located eighteen miles from the other. At the vertex is Nauvoo, snuggled into a horseshoe bend on the east bank of the Mississippi River; down river is Warsaw, the oldest of the three cities; and inland on the prairie is Carthage, the Hancock County seat.

1 In 1844 these three cities on the west Illinois border became the focal point of bitter religious persecution. Not that religious persecution was new to the world, or to the United States of America, or to the people of this area.

1 For hundreds of years the Old World had felt the strains of religious conflict. In America religious differences erupted early in the young colonies, and at the same period as this book deals with, July 1844, fourteen people were killed and fifty wounded in Philadelphia when a mob gathered to burn St. Philip's Church as a result of differences between the Protestants and Irish Catholics; the riot lasting two days and the Governor calling out three thousand of the state militia. Up to this time, too, many of the people of this area, in establishing a new religion on the American frontier, had given their homes and their lives in support of their cause.

1 What made the triangle on the west border of Illinois important was that for the first time in America the founder of a religion was mobbed and killed.
