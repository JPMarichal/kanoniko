Reed H. Blake, Twenty-four Hours to Martyrdom ( 1973)

The Twenty-Fourth Hour

5:00 p.m.

A few miles outside Carthage, Colonel Levi Williams had led the band of Warsaw militiamen in a wide arch to the left, leaving the Warsaw road to come into Carthage on the Nauvoo road. Behind them the sun was dropping toward the Mississippi River and the few scattered clouds cast a polka-dot pattern on the knee-high grass of the prairie. At a bank of black mud they stopped, and with powder and mud they blackened their faces as a precaution against recognition by someone not in sympathy with their cause.

124"Where is the Prophet Joseph?" they sang, "Where is the Prophet Joseph? / Where is the Prophet Joseph? / Safe in Carthage jail."

124The Warsaw boys completed their long detour and now the town of Carthage loomed on the horizon. They avoided the jimson and mayweed-lined road that led to the jail and instead cut through a woodlot and pasture. Their earlier morning spirits had returned, and the closer they came, the quicker became their step. There was no need for orders now. No need for urging, no chance to organize. Ahead through the trees was the Carthage jail, its yellow stone picking up the flood of the dropping sun, and the men broke into a run.

124On the street, Artois Hamilton had found the "quack doctor" Thomas L. Barnes, another man by the name of "Doc" Morrison, and an "old-citizen"-as the faction labels went-John Wilson. Hamilton asked the three men to go out on the Nauvoo road for a distance of three miles to "see what was going on," for there was the constant danger of help from Nauvoo. Shortly after positioning themselves, the trio saw a large group of men-just off the Warsaw road-hurrying toward Carthage. A little later they saw a group on the road going back to Warsaw. It was at this point they decided to return to Carthage and report what they had seen.

125Meanwhile, the lookout stationed atop the courthouse shouted to the Greys below, "They're coming down the fence to the jail!"

125The men formed and started for the scene.

125On the road between Napoleon and Jacksonburg, Jackson County, Michigan, George A. Smith, one of the ten members of the Council of the Twelve who were in the east preaching-but now at the summons of Joseph Smith, like the rest of the Twelve, on his way back to Nauvoo-got off his horse and walked a few yards distant to a grove of oak where he dropped to his knees in prayer. He was travelling in company with Crandall Dunn and had for the past hour felt unusually downcast. They were not far from the farm of Noah Willis Bartholemew, to which they were travelling, and Dunn, noticing the time, said he would ride on and make preparations for their stay.

125George A. Smith asked the Lord to break the spell of horror that had taken hold of his mind. He remained on his knees "for a good length of time," but could find no relief. At the Bartholemew farm he spent a sleepless night in a series of "miserable thoughts and reflections." Once he thought someone whispered in his ear, "Joseph and Hyrum are dead; ain't you glad of it?"

125In Boston, three other members of the Council, Brigham Young, Wilford Woodruff, and Orson Hyde, were in the Saints' meeting hall with a small party of Church members examining maps. Presently Orson Hyde said he had a heavy heart and knew not why. Then he went to the other end of the hall and walked the floor, tears running down his face. A few minutes later, Brigham Young and Wilford Woodruff left the hall to catch a train. While waiting at the depot they commented to one another how very depressed they felt.

126On a canal boat between Utica and Buffalo in upstate New York, Parley P. Pratt of the Council bade welcome to his brother William as he came aboard during a stop. Apostle Pratt asked William if he had any books or tracts containing the gospel of Christ, and if so, to put them under lock and key. "The people are not worthy of them," he told his brother. "I feel that the spirit of murder is in the hearts of the people through the land."

126Two other members were travelling together from Philadelphia to New York. Heber C. Kimball and Lyman Wight had spent the day travelling by railway and steamboat. Nearing the end of their journey, Kimball remarked to Wight that he felt very mournful and knew not the cause. "It is as though I had lost some dear friend," he said.

126From the landing there was a polite knock at the door, and at the prisoners' bidding the jailer's son entered with a pitcher of fresh well water.

126Shortly another knock was heard, and Jailer Stigall, who had been down to the square, came in to report that Stephen Markham had been surrounded by a mob and run out of town. He said he thought Markham had gone to Nauvoo.

126No one in the room said anything.

126Stigall then suggested they would be safer in the cell, and Joseph said, "After supper we will go in."

126A few rods east of the county jail, a Carthage housewife stepped to her back porch to shake the dust from a rug. Looking up she saw a line of men emerge from the woods, trailing along silently at a slow run from the direction of the Nauvoo road. Their faces were painted dark. Jumping the fence, they surrounded the adjacent yard-that of the jail-and began rushing the guards.

127"It's the Mormons," she gasped. "They've come to free Joseph Smith!" She dropped her rug and ran down Walnut Street with the news.

127In the upstairs room the jailer had just left.

127"Brother Richards," the Prophet asked, "if we go into the cell, will you go with us?"

127The doctor answered, "Brother Joseph, you did not ask me to cross the river with you-you did not ask me to come to Carthage with you-you did not ask me to come to jail with you-and do you think I would forsake you now? But I will tell you what I will do; if you are condemned to be hung for treason, I will be hung in your stead, and you shall go free."

127"You cannot," Joseph said.

127"I will," the doctor replied.

127From below someone called to the guard and he went down.

127Immediately there was a rustling at the outer door. Taylor, who was standing near the window, looked out to see a force of men crossing the yard. He sprang to the door and found the others already against it.

127A shout of "Surrender!" went up from below, and three or four shots were fired.

127It is thought the cry of surrender came from one of the guards not in on the assassination plans. One later reported that he fired into the mob only to discover that his firelock was loaded blank, while those beside him fired over the heads of the onrushing mobbers.

127In the meantime, the prisoners braced themselves against the door after Joseph, Hyrum, and Taylor, who had taken off their coats, raced for a weapon-Joseph to his coat for the six-shooter, Hyrum to his coat for the single-barrel, and Taylor to the corner for Markham's cane, the hickory "rascal-beater." The doctor took up Jones' walking cane.

128When those on the stairs reached the door, they were unable to push it open, and one, assuming it to be locked, fired on the latch, shattering the edge of the door. At this, those inside jumped back, with Joseph, Taylor, and Richards hugging the west wall to the left of the door. Almost at the same time, from the landing, a ball was fired over the shoulder of the mobber nearest the door. It came through the panel four feet three inches above the floor and struck Hyrum in the face as he was jumping back from the door, and firing the single-shot. Through the east window, from the direction of the Greys, a second ball hit him in the back, passing through his body and striking his watch in his right vest pocket with such force that it pulverized the crystal, tore off the hands, and mashed the body of the timepiece.

128Hyrum fell full length to the floor, face up, his head below the east window, saying, "I am a dead man." He never moved afterward.

128The deep look of sympathy on Joseph's face when his brother fell, fastened itself to Taylor's mind for the remainder of his life. The Prophet fell to Hyrum and, bending over him, murmured, "Oh, my poor, dear brother Hyrum." Then he rose and with a firm step went to the door and opened it slightly. Pulling the pepperbox from his pocket and leaning against the door jamb, Joseph fired into the hall. He fired all six barrels, of which only three discharged. Then he and Taylor exchanged places and Taylor, using the "rascal-beater," continued to parry and beat down the muzzles and bayonets as they protruded through the opening.

128A momentary lull in the shooting followed the return of fire from the bedroom. By now the smoke from the powder flashes had clouded the room and landing. It added to the stairway confusion as those on the landing, who had shot their round, pushed their way back to ram in a new load, and those yet to fire pushed their way forward.

128Of the three barrels discharged by Joseph, it is believed he hit three men: an Irishman named Wells-who was in the mob from his love of a brawl-in the arm; Voorhees-an oversized kid from Bear Creek known for his lack of good sense-in the shoulder; and a man named Gallagher-a Southerner from the Mississippi Bottom-in the face.

129Two other men were known to get hit in the hall, one a man named Townsend from Fort Madison, Iowa Territory, who died nine months later from an arm wound that wouldn't heal, and another named Mills, who was shot in the arm; but it is assumed they were shot in the confusion of men and arms jammed in the passageway.

129With their firearms expended, the prisoners expected a sudden rush from without as those above were being pushed further and further into the room by the pressure from below. Cornered, they were engulfed by the haze of smoke, the demoniac oaths of yelling men, the stench of burnt powder. As he stood beating down the arms, Taylor remembered feeling as though his time had come. Later he wrote: "Streams of fire as thick as my arm passed by meÂ�it looked like certain deathÂ�but I do not know when, in any critical position, I was more calm, unruffled, energetic, and acted with more promptness and decision."

129As he pounded away, Joseph said from behind him, "That's right, Brother Taylor, parry them off as well as you can."

129They were the last words he heard the Prophet speak.

129When it became impossible to beat away the weapons any longer-the men and arms had become so dense as to make hitting ineffectual-Taylor crossed the room, thinking perhaps to jump to the yard below where he might hope to find friends. But as he reached the window, a ball from the door struck him midway up his thigh, which hit the bone, flattened out to the size of a quarter, and then passed through the fleshy part of his leg to within a half-inch of the surface. Taylor fell across the window sill, conscious that he was still alive but unable to move. "I am shot," he said.

129In his paralyzed condition he could feel himself slipping slowly through the window when suddenly he was thrown back into the room. Unknown to him at the time, a round fired up from the yard had hit his watch in the breast pocket of his vest, stopping the works.

130Thus the time of the martyrdom was set: 16 minutes, 26 seconds past five o'clock.

130Hitting the floor, Taylor found his animation restored. He crawled to the bed and pulled himself beneath it. In the ensuing period, he was hit three more times. He took one ball a little below the left knee; a second ball in the left arm above the wrist, which passed through the joint and lodged in the fleshy part of his hand; a third round in his left hip which splattered flesh and blood across the wall and left a hole the size of a hand in his leg. As he lay in his pain, Taylor had the sensation that should he live he would be lame and decrepit and an object of pity, and he felt as though it would be better to die.

130When Taylor fell, Richards, from behind the Prophet, took Taylor's place beside the door and continued the parrying with the smaller walking cane which he had. Then, in an effort to draw the mobbers' attention away from the room, the Prophet leaped to the window, the one from which Taylor had just fallen, and as he gained the ledge, two balls from the door hit him in the back, and from the outside a third hit him in the chest.

130The Prophet poised momentarily on the ledge and exclaimed, "Oh Lord, My God!"

130The fall to the ground was twenty-five feet. He hit on his left side near the well curb.

130Instantly the shout went up, "He's leaped the window," and the mobbers rushed downstairs, leaving Richards and Taylor alone.

130It was a miracle Richards was unhurt. He was a big man and an easy target. Yet he suffered only a nick in his left ear lobe. When the Prophet went to the window, he followed, but so heavy was the firepower as it chipped about the window, that he had to withdraw himself. Still, despite the risk, he again stuck his head out the window-determined to see the end of him whom he loved. The Prophet, however, was dead, and after watching for a few seconds, and being satisfied that Joseph was no longer alive, Richards hastened to the landing to see if the cell door was open. He felt that as he was the only one left, the mob would soon return to the room to finish the job.

131As he secured himself beneath the bed, Taylor was suddenly aware that all was silent. Gone were the execration, the flash of the pan, the thrust of the bayonet. In their place was a grey film of smoke, the smell of blood and powder. About him the walls were pockmarked with shot. Near his feet, the doctor was peering out the window. To his left, Hyrum lay dead. He could not see the Prophet. The hall door was open, the landing empty.

131He watched the doctor leave the window and try the cell door and it struck him that Richards was seeking the safety of the prison. He called, "Stop, doctor, and take me along."

131It was the first time Richards realized that Taylor was alive.

131Trying the door, and finding it to be open, Richards returned and, grabbing Taylor beneath the arms, dragged him into the cell. When he had Taylor inside, Richards leaned over him and said, "Oh, Brother Taylor, is it possible that they have killed both Brother Hyrum and Joseph? It cannot surely be, and yet I saw them shoot them." And then elevating his hands two or three times, his thoughts on Joseph and Hyrum, he said, "Oh Lord, my God, spare thy servants."

131Then he took Taylor beneath his arms and pulled him further into the cell. He was trembling, still aghast at what he had witnessed. "Brother Taylor, this is a terrible event," he said.

131In the inner cell, Richards pulled Taylor onto a pile of straw sometimes used for a bed and covered his companion with a dirty mattress.

132"This is a hard case to lay you on the floor," the doctor said, still anticipating the mobbers' return, "but if your wounds are not fatal, I want you to live to tell the story."

132As he lay concealed beneath the mattress, Taylor felt the sickening sensation, "that the Prophet of the living God had fallen and he had seen his brother in the cold embrace of death." To Taylor's mind there existed now a vacuum in the great field of human existence. He questioned: "Why must the good perish, and the virtuous be destroyed? Why must God's nobility, the salt of the earth, and the most perfect types of all excellence, fall victims to the cruel, fiendish hate of incarnate devils?"

132In the jailyard below the Greys and Warsaw boys mingled in silent recognition. The rabble from Hancock County and elsewhere was there, and a good number of pukes, as Missourians were called, from across the river. Colonel Levi Williams had shouted, "Shoot him, damn him, shoot him!" as the Prophet lay by the well curb, and there had been sporadic shots. Some had slapped the Prophet and cursed at him, but for the most part the men talked quietly, sat about reloading their firelocks, or looked from the corner of their eye toward the body now propped against the well curb.

132M. B. Darnell was a youth and new to the militia. Earlier, at the instigation of General Deming, he had been given a commission from the Governor as a lieutenant in the militia and with the others had gone outside of town where he pitched his tent and for a week "played soldier as well as I knew how." Being one of those whose command had been disbanded, he had been going to the pasture near the jail to pick up his horse when he saw coming towards him from the creek, dark figures.

132He felt "something terrible was going to happen and became transfixed to the spot."

132The young lieutenant remembered that those of the Greys and those with painted faces seemed about equal in number, After the killing, as he watched the men walk about, their muskets rattling and their speech heavy with swearing, he noticed Thomas Sharp, the editor, Jacob Davis, the lawyer, Alexander Sympson, the "land shark," and his sons, Lieutenant Frank Worrell of the Carthage Greys, and a man he knew only as Hendricks.

133Carthage was the halfway station on the stage run from Keokuk to Nauvoo. As the stage came into town, the driver noticed a large crowd of men on one side and regulars on the other as the stage passed the county jail. Delivering his passengers and mail to the hotel, he again passed the jail on his way to the stable. As he approached he heard shooting and saw a man come out the window as if someone had thrown him. As soon as the stableman took charge of the team, the driver jumped off the stage and ran, "frightened half out of my wits."

133Ebenezer Rand, the shoemaker, was in his ox cart near the courthouse when the shooting started. He was a member of the Greys, a close friend of Artois Hamilton and Calvin Cole, and spent much of his time talking about the War of 1812. When the shooting began he kicked up his animal and started over.

133In the lean-to kitchen, Mrs. Stigall lay flat on the floor. When the attack had started, she was bending over to place a pan of bread in the oven. Suddenly, one of the first shots fired drove into the stove above her head. She had dropped to the floor terrified. Now, the attack over, she began sobbing.

133As with the upper floor, the walls of the ground floor bore the marks of shot. Jailer Stigall, still on his hands and knees, began to work his way across the floor to see if members of his family were hurt.

133He could hear mobbers coming in the front door and running up the stairs.

133After covering his companion with the mattress, Richards, not wanting to draw attention to the concealed Taylor, went from the inner cell to the metal door that opened to the landing. He closed it and then "stood before the door awaiting the onset." At once he heard men coming up the stairs.

134Where less than an hour before the streets of Carthage had been quiet, now there was pandemonium. Word was scattered from door to door that the Mormons were coming. And if one had looked out of his window provided he lived in the northwest section of Carthage-he would have seen the Greys rushing to the jail, firing even before they were within distance. And this, it appeared, in defense of their city. Or perhaps one might have seen people rushing into the streets, carrying their arms. This also to repel the Mormons.

134Now the news first carried by the Carthage housewife made its full circle, and in the jailhouse yard the cry was sounded, "The Mormons are coming!"

134Even before they were fully into the room where only the dead Hyrum lay, the cry came, and those on the second floor of the jail dropped their search and ran downstairs.

134With the shout, men began running in all directions. Those of the Warsaw militia and the Greys took to the woods, while those citizens in the city loaded their wagons, or mounted horses, or if no conveyance was available, took to their feet.

134So rushed in their flight were the Warsaw troops that some were back in that city, eighteen miles distant, at a quarter to eight.

134As a new quiet settled over the jail, Richards, behind the door of the cell, realized that the siege was over. He slumped to the floor and his mind went to the Prophet, who had told him over a year before that the time would come when balls would fly around him like hail, but that he should not receive a hole in his robe.

134The assassination completed, an "anti" hastened to the edge of Carthage where a signal cannon had been placed on the Warsaw road and fired it. In Warsaw, on hearing the sound and according to plan, a merchant jumped on his horse and rode to Quincy to report that, "Joseph and Hyrum Smith have been killed; and all those who were with them in the jail. They were attempting to escape and were killed by the guard."

135In Nauvoo, when the cannon was fired, the Governor was concluding his address to the people. When the low rumble rolled across the city, many of those in the audience, on hearing it, cast an anxious glance at the threatening sky, thinking it to be the distant clap of thunder.

135There was an obvious pause on the part of the Governor. Soon after, he took up his line of march.

135The sun had arched to the west when Samuel Smith reached Plymouth. He had gone directly to the corral, got his workhorse, and left. The first that his family had known of his return was when he entered the house a short time later. He was excited, and said, "I sold that workhorse of mine and bought a race horse. It's the fastest horse in this section of the country, and I think I can break through the mob and get to Carthage."

135The actions of her father frightened seven-year-old Mary. She had never seen her father this way, haggard and shaken. She stood silently to the side as her father talked briefly to her stepmother. Then he mounted his new purchase and left at a gallop.

135At the house, Samuel had weighed the notion of taking a sidearm, but had discarded the idea with the reasoning that the sight of weapons often leads to misunderstandings. And so he went unarmed.

135The pounding from the running horse intensified his sideache, and now Samuel crouched in the saddle to ease the pain.

135Nearing Carthage, at the place where the picket line had previously stationed itself, Samuel saw people coming down the road toward him. A few were on foot, some on horseback, and others in buggies. Stopping one of the buggies in which a man and woman rode, he asked the trouble, and the man stood, flailed his arms, and shouted, "The Mormons are coming!"

136The woman beside him, seeing the horseman did not know, gripped her shawl and said, "The two Smiths have been killed by a mob!"

136The words sent Samuel reeling in his saddle. As he started to leave his mount, the man reached out and held him. "God help me," Samuel said, "I must go to them."

136From the thickets, those men who had previously been part of the picket line encountered earlier came onto the road. As they had said they would, one raised his musket and drew down on the Prophet's brother. Seeing this from his standing position, the man holding Samuel, not knowing if the ball was intended for him or the horseman, fell back shouting. The horses shied; the rider slumped. The shot went through Samuel's hat.

136 Samuel then bolted his horse and rode straight at the group, scattering them to each side of the road. In the length of time it took them to recover, the horseman had gained his distance, and the mobbers could not get off another shot.

136When two of the number mounted to give chase, Samuel turned his horse into the woods, reasoning he could not ride boldly into Carthage to face a hostile mob with two of their ranks in close pursuit.

136It was difficult riding. The horses sank in the soft loamy soil. The chuck-holes were full of rain water and hard to see. In the swales ran unbroken sloughs of black mud and water. And so began a game of hide-and-seek between Samuel and the two mobbers which, because of his fatigued condition, the Prophet's brother nearly lost.

136Slowly people began filtering to Carthage jail. Captain Robert Smith, his Greys scattered, returned and helped to assemble a coroner's jury. The "quack doctor" Barnes, along with "Doc" Morrison and John Wilson, came in from the Nauvoo road to report what they had seen, and Barnes, on hearing of the mobbing went to the jail and found Taylor still on the straw pile. Jailer Stigall, after a check on his family and the deserted jailyard, went up the stairs, and it was he who persuaded Taylor to let Barnes remove the ball in his left hand.

137The Mormon apostate Francis Higbee was in the neighborhood. On hearing this, Taylor swore his life against him, and Higbee left town soon after.

137From his farm, General Deming rode in to Carthage and began assembling what men of the Hancock militias he could still find in town.

137The first to view the Prophet, once the mobbers had fled, was William R. Hamilton, the fourteen-year-old son of the hotelkeeper. He and his companion had been lookouts atop the courthouse. Their instructions had been to "watch westward." On seeing the band of men approaching Carthage from the west but bearing north, they had reported their observations. They had been told to return to their posts, that when the force came out of the woods north of the town they were to signal those below.

137This they had done, and for a while young William watched the scene from his courthouse vantage point. He saw the attack and shortly thereafter a man come out of the window. By the time he had reached the street and made his way to the jail, however, the place was deserted.

137Now William stood by and looked at the body of the Mormon leader. Around the jail all was quiet. A woman passed swiftly from the lean-to through the back door of the jail. "Shoemaker" Rand came up in his wagon and looked across the yard. From behind him an occasional shout broke the air or, down a nearby street, the thump of a running horse.

137In the upstairs cell, the handful of men now gathered about the wounded Taylor felt that the Mormon should be removed to the Hamilton House. Taylor refused, saying he did not consider it safe.

138To their protests he replied, "I don't know you. Whom am I among? I am surrounded by assassins and murderers; witness your deeds. Don't talk to me of kindness or comfort; look at your murdered victims! Look at me! I want none of your counsel nor comfort. There may be some safety here; I can be assured of none elsewhere."

138The men about Taylor damned their souls to hell that they would stand by him to the death. In less than thirty minutes they had all fled the town.

138The doctor Barnes, alternately sawing with a dull penknife and prying with a pair of carpenter's compasses, finally dug the ball from Taylor's hand. The ball weighed about half an ounce, and Barnes later remarked that Taylor had "nerves like the devil" to stand what he did in its extraction. Taylor commented that he needed such nerves to stand the surgical butchery, and that, "whatever my nerves may be, his practice was devilish."

138Covered with blood from his wounds, his clothing and the straw sticking to his body, Taylor's appearance was shocking to those about him. Yet possibly it was the straw that saved his life. Matted as it was to his open wounds, it reduced the flow of blood and formed the barrier around which the blood later coagulated.

138Richards, in the meantime, had been busy with the coroner's inquest and with making preparations for removal of the bodies, and now, his duties taken care of, he returned to the suffering Taylor and suggested they go to the hotel.

138Taylor was helped to the landing and, looking into the bedroom, saw Hyrum still lying as he had left him in what now seemed hours ago. From the landing also could be seen the body of the Prophet where it had been carried from the well curb to the foot of the stairs.

138While Taylor lay on the landing, Richards sought out Artois Hamilton and prevailed upon him and his family, who were now planning to leave Carthage, to remain at the hotel. It was after considerable effort on the part of Richards that the hotelkeeper consented. (Taylor later said that while he was at the hotel Hamilton looked upon him as a hostage in the event of a Mormon attack.) It was with still more difficulty that Richards could find enough men left in Carthage to assist him in getting the bodies of Joseph and Hyrum and the wounded Taylor to the Hamilton House.

139It was 5:40 when Governor Ford and his party entered the Nauvoo Temple grounds. On hearing the cannon's report, the Governor had immediately taken up his line of march for Carthage, declining the Saints' invitation to stay over and observe Nauvoo for himself. While marching up Main Street, the Governor requested that his escort perform the sword exercise, giving all the passes, guards, cuts, and thrusts they knew to make an imposing show. This was done, no doubt, to intimidate the people, for in his speech the Governor had said that those in Nauvoo should never consider it wise to go against such "well-disciplined troops."

139On reaching Mulholland Street, Ford, not wanting his departure to seem hurried, ordered the troops to rest while he and the officers visited the temple.

139They inspected the first floor of the building only briefly, some of the junior officers mentioning its tactical position on the bluff in time of war. Then they entered the basement, the only part of the building completed and then in use.

139On learning of the Governor's plans to visit the temple, Alpheus Cuttler, a member of the building committee, dispatched William Sterrett to "quietly watch them while in and about the building," and now Sterrett arrived, his step silent on the unpaved floor. Looking about, he discovered that one of the men had climbed the stairs to the edge of the baptismal font, and Sterrett wanted to call out for him to descend-to Sterrett's mind the stairs were not for the dirt and clop of boots-but he hesitated to speak and for a while remained unnoticed.

140After a cursory glance the man descended.

140The font was a temporary one and would be replaced by a stone font once the temple was finished. Oval in shape and constructed of pine lumber staves that were tongued and grooved, it measured sixteen by twelve feet at the top of the inside edge. It stood seven feet high from the foundation, and its inside depth was four feet to comply with the Mormon belief of baptism by immersion. Two flights of steps on the north and south led up to it, both with iron banisters, one for entering and the other for leaving the water. Supporting the font were twelve wooden oxen, four on each side, two on each end, their legs immersed knee deep in the earth. The oxen were known for the expert carving by which they were fashioned.

140To B. H. Buckingham, a reporter from the Boston Courier, the whole affair was "quite simple and unostentatious."

140On reaching the ground again, the officer called to the Governor to come and observe one of the oxen with a missing horn. Smiling, Ford walked around to where the man was standing, the rest of the party drawing to the spot.

140Laying a hand on it, the Governor laughingly said, "'This is the cow with the crumply horn' that we read of."

140From the rear another added, "That tossed the maiden all forlorn!" The group broke into laughter.

140Now the officer that first noticed the oxen's horn broke off the animal's other horn, and following his cue several others took other horns. From behind someone said, "I'll be damned but I should like to take one of those horns with me, to show as a curiosity, but it is a pity to break them off," and then elbowed his way forward and took his piece.

141"This temple is a curious piece of workmanship," said another, coming around from behind the font, "and it is a damned shame they didn't let Joe Smith finish it so we could have seen what sort of finish he would have put on it, for it's altogether a different style of architecture from any building I've seen or read about."

142"But he is dead by this time," he was answered. "He will never see this temple again."

142On the last comment the Governor blanched and suggested they leave to visit the temple lot workshop.

142The word that the Saints should lose their Prophet by the hands of mortal man was more than Sterrett could stand. It demanded an answer, and as the officers began filing to the exit, he shouted, "They cannot kill him until he has finished his work!"

142The Governor hesitated briefly in front of Sterrett, then gave a knowing look to an aide at his side, who answered, "Whether he has finished his work or not, by God, he will not see this place again, for he's finished before this time."

142William Sterrett stood weak, motionless, the chamber echoing the steps of the horse soldiers as they followed the Governor up the stairs and out to the workshop. Hot tears welled in his eyes, and he had the mixed emotion to drop to his knees and cry, to run outside and fight.

142"They cannot," he murmured, gaining control of his emotions. "They couldn't kill him at Liberty, and they cannot kill him at Carthage."

142He thought to smile as he came up, leaving the impression that their remarks were taken only lightly by one who followed the Prophet, but of his arrival no one made any outward notice. Talk now was of many things, the temple, Nauvoo, the Smiths, the late season for planting. To the side, two men broke from discussing the Smiths' fate, and one, pulling his watch from his pocket, rubbed the crystal and said, "Governor, it's time we were off. We have been here too long already." He fumbled as he replaced the watch. "Whether you go or not, I'm going to leave, and that damned quick," he added.

143With the uttered intention, the others turned quickly to the Governor.

143"Yes," Ford said, "it's time for us to be going."

143Leaving the stone shop, the party picked up their horses where they were hitched near the temple, rode out of the temple grounds and down to Mulholland Street.

143William Sterrett stood alone on temple block, his eyes looking beyond the half-finished building toward Carthage.

143"They cannot kill him," he said again.

143Carthage had become a near-deserted town. Men with their families, in carts, in wagons, on horseback, or afoot, were emptying across the prairie to Augusta and St. Mary's. At Warsaw a similar pattern occurred, once the disbanded militia returned with the news, those in the city fleeing across the Mississippi River to Churchville (now Alexandra).

143Twelve miles east of Carthage the report overtook the troops of McDonough County, and Captain John Lawn, looking back across his men strung along the Carthage road, said, "Oh that I had known of this massacre, so soon to transpire. I would have remained, and, when the first ball was fired at the Smiths, I would have fired the second through the body of the villain who fired it or died in the attempt."

143In the Mormon settlement of Macedonia the news arrived, and young Johnny Smith, on picket duty on the main street, remembered the sound of firing he had heard earlier and hung his head and wept.

143At the English hamlet of Big Mound, eight miles from Nauvoo, Christopher Layton was putting in sod corn when he heard the news, and he dropped his load and walked slowly out of the field.

143At Fountain Green, shadows were filling the streets when the news came, and in the long-time county store of C. C. Tyler, Jr., of the firm of Tyler and McClaughey, the bookkeeper, C. C. Tyler, Sr., closed out the day's journal and in the margin added the entry':

144"This night, at five o'clock p.m., Joseph Smith and Hyrum Smith, his brother, were mobbed and shot at Carthage, Ill."
