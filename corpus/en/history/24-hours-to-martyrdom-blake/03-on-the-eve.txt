Reed H. Blake, Twenty-four Hours to Martyrdom ( 1973)

On the Eve

6:00 p.m.

Carthage jail, June 26, 1844. Joseph Smith stood framed against the open east window. It had been a sultry day, hot and humid. Behind him the jailhouse yard stretched away to recurring woodlots and the broad prairie that was Hancock County, and in the brake and bush, rising up with the festering heat now subsiding with evening, came the call of the frog and the pig. Below him to the east and south squatted the small, low-built town of Carthage, clinging haphazardly to the western flatlands and claiming a deference as a county seat. Not unlike the other towns of its time and place, Carthage presented a collection of homes and gardens, barns and empty lots, all clustered about a courthouse square.

3 The Prophet stood bareheaded, one hand thrust into his high-waisted, homespun black trousers. He wore a cutaway coat that struck him at the knees; a white, lace-trimmed shirt with stand-up collar; a wide, exposed necktie called a stock; and shoes of dark color.

3 From across the room at the table, his brother Hyrum watched him. Like Joseph, Hyrum too was dressed in the style of the time: trousers of homespun cotton serge, brown tinged, with two large pockets in front and cut high in the back; a light brown sleeveless vest with a low cut front vent; a pullover white shirt with detachable collar. In his lapel he wore a gold pin of cloverleaf design. It represented the Masonic movement. In his vest pocket he carried a Lion Password watch. Silver encased, it had Roman numerals and was inscribed, "London 1830."

4 As was their dress, Joseph and Hyrum were much alike.

4 There were several men in the room, but only two others were considered stationary: John Taylor, a newspaper editor, and Willard Richards, private secretary to Joseph Smith, who recorded all the Prophet's movements and conversations.

4 The men sat about the room talking.

4 Occasionally John Taylor would hum a song. Or Hyrum Smith would read aloud. The Prophet, perhaps, would tell an amusing story.

4 With so many in the room, it was difficult to move about, so the men mostly sat. The room contained a small table and chair and a bed. It had three windows and a fireplace. On the floor beside the bed was a mattress.

4 Some of the men reclined on the mattress and others sat on the bed.

4 It was a quiet time of day. Men were doing their evening chores and closing their shops; women were gathering the children and preparing the supper. After another hour the town would stir again. The supper and the chores would be over, and the inhabitants would go to their neighbors' to discuss the happenings, or to the tavern to drink and to do the same. Some would go to a meeting. A few would remain at home.

4 It was not a usual evening in Carthage.

4 George W. Stigall, jailer for Hancock County, his shirt off, sat at his dining-room table on the ground floor of the three-year-old county jail and gave a tug to his galluses that made two stripes over his underclothing. His hands were moist and his breathing heavy. It was an uncomfortable day, he observed.

4 On the table was a mittimus. It was the second he had received in twenty-four hours. On its demand, he had been upstairs returning his prisoners to their quarters, and now, as he sat at the table staring out of the curtained window, he wondered what extreme the present excitement would reach before it subsided.

5The mittimus was illegal-as was the first. He knew that now, in Hancock County, the due process of law was only a guise; popular opinion had dictated his course and would, for a time at least, continue to do so.

5He could find no law authorizing a justice of the peace to demand prisoners committed to his charge, and so, an hour earlier when Constable David Bettisworth had come demanding the prisoners, he had refused to give them up.

5"What's the matter with you?" the constable had said.

5"The magistrate committed them without an examination and therefore has no further jurisdiction in the case."

5"You ain't giving them up?"

5"I am not."

5The constable and his companion had left, leaving an oath behind them, and reported his actions to the justice of the peace, Robert F. Smith. It was Smith who had issued the mittimus. He also doubled as captain of the local militia the Carthage Greys. Justice Smith in turn had reported the incident to Thomas Ford, the governor of Illinois, now staying in Carthage at the Hamilton House, a hotel and tavern east of the courthouse square. The Governor had replied, "We have plenty of troops; there are the Carthage Greys under your command, bring them out."

5And so the militia had poured into his jail, and under the direction of the officer of the guard, Lt. Frank A. Worrell, delivered his prisoners to their commander, Robert Smith.

5Returned to the jail after a brief meeting with Justice Smith and the counsel on each side, the prisoners were now awaiting trial set for noon the next day.

5Stigall tossed the mittimus on the table, then lifted a second note which had been given him as he was descending the stairs after returning the prisoners. It was delivered by a friend of the prisoners who had come up from the Hamilton House.

6The note was in longhand and read:

6"I would advise the jailer to keep the Messrs. Smith in the room in which I found them this morning, unless a closer confinement should be clearly necessary to prevent an escape.

"Thomas Ford Governor and Commander-in-Chief June 26th, 1844"

6Stigall smiled to himself when he read the word escape. He had heard the rumor of a mock escape try.

6The Smiths were his prisoners. Joseph, the Prophet and founder of the fourteen-year-old Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, more commonly referred to as the Mormon Church, and his elder brother Hyrum, the Church patriarch.

6There were others in the room with them, friends of the Prophet, but only the two Smiths were prisoners. The room to which the Governor referred was an upstairs bedroom used sometimes by those charged with minor offenses.

6It wasn't a minor offense charged against the Smiths, however; their charge was treason!

6The jailer parted the curtains on the window to his right. On the grounds outside and in the hall leading to the prisoners' room, as protection for the Smiths, were eight militiamen, members of the Carthage Greys.

6Stigall recalled his surprise earlier in the day on seeing the Governor, accompanied by Colonel Thomas Geddes, standing before him, asking to see the prisoners, and the ensuing conversation, which even the guards on the landing remained quiet long enough to hear.

6Governor Thomas Ford: "General Smith, I believe you have given me a general outline of the difficulties that have existed in the country, in the documents forwarded to me by Dr. Bernhisel and Mr. Taylor; but, unfortunately, there seems to be a great discrepancy between your statements and those of your enemies. It is true that you are substantiated by evidence and affidavit, but for such an extraordinary excitement as that which is now in the country, there must be some cause, and I attribute the last outbreak to the destruction of the Expositor, and to your refusal to comply with the writ issued by Esquire Morrison. The press in the United States is looked upon as the great bulwark of American freedom, and its destruction in Nauvoo was represented and looked upon as a high-handed measure, and manifests to the people a disposition on your part to suppress the liberty of speech and of the press. This, with your refusal to comply with the requisition of a writ, I conceive to be the principal cause of this difficulty; and you are, moreover, represented to me as turbulent, and defiant of the laws and institutions of your country."

7Joseph Smith: "Governor Ford, you, sir, as governor of this state, are aware of the persecutions that I have endured. You know well that our course has been peaceable and law-abiding, for I have furnished this state, ever since our settlement here, with sufficient evidence of my pacific intentions, and those of the people with whom I am associated, by the endurance of every conceivable indignity and lawless outrage perpetrated upon me and upon this people since our settlement here; and you yourself know that I have kept you well posted in relation to all matters associated with the late difficulties. If you have not got some of my communications, it has not been my fault.

7"Agreeable to your orders, I assembled the Nauvoo Legion for the protection of Nauvoo and the surrounding country against an armed band of marauders; and ever since they have been mustered I have almost daily communicated with you in regard to all the leading events that have transpired; and whether in the capacity of mayor of the city, or lieutenant-general of the Nauvoo Legion, I have striven to preserve the peace and administer even-handed justice to all; but my motives are impugned, my acts are misconstrued, and I am grossly and wickedly misrepresented. I suppose I am indebted for my incarceration here to the oath of a worthless man, who was arraigned before me and fined for abusing and maltreating his lame, helpless brother.

8"That I should be charged by you, sir, who know better, of acting contrary to law, is to me a matter of surprise. Was it the Mormons or our enemies who first commenced these difficulties? You know well it was not us; and when this turbulent, outrageous people commenced their insurrectionary movements, I made you acquainted with them, officially, and asked your advice, and have followed strictly your counsel in every particular.

8"Who ordered out the Nauvoo Legion? I did, under your direction. For what purpose? To suppress these insurrectionary movements. It was at your instance, sir, that I issued a proclamation calling upon the Nauvoo Legion to be in readiness, at a moment's warning, to guard against the incursions of mobs, and gave an order to Jonathan Dunham, acting major-general, to that effect. Am I then to be charged for the acts of others; and because lawlessness and mobocracy abound, am I, when carrying out your instructions, to be charged with not abiding the law? Why is it that I must be made accountable for other men's acts? If there is trouble in the country, neither I nor my people made it; and all that we have ever done, after much endurance on our part, is to maintain and uphold the Constitution and institutions of our country, and to protect an injured, innocent, and persecuted people against misrule and mob violence.

8"Concerning the destruction of the press to which you refer, men may differ somewhat in their opinion about it; but can it be supposed that after all the indignities to which we have been subjected outside, that this people could suffer a set of worthless vagabonds to come into our city, and right under our own eyes and protection, vilify and calumniate not only ourselves, but the character of our wives and daughters, as was impudently and unblushingly done in that infamous and filthy sheet? There is not a city in the United States that would have suffered such an indignity for twenty-four hours.

9"Our whole people were indignant, and loudly called upon our city authorities for redress of their grievances, which, if not attended to, they themselves would have taken the matter into their own hands, and have summarily punished the audacious wretches, as they deserved.

9"The principles of equal rights that have been instilled into our bosoms from our cradles, as American citizens, forbid us submitting to every foul indignity, and succumbing and pandering to wretches so infamous as these. But, independent of this, the course that we pursued we considered to be strictly legal; for, notwithstanding the result, we were anxious to be governed strictly by law, and therefore convened the City Council; and being desirous in our deliberations to abide by law, we summoned legal counsel to be present on the occasion.

9"Upon investigating the matter, we found that our city charter gave us power to remove all nuisances; and, furthermore, upon consulting Blackstone upon what might be considered a nuisance, it appeared that that distinguished lawyer, who is considered authority, I believe, in all our courts, states, among other things, that 'a libelous and filthy press may be considered a nuisance, and abated as such.'

9"Here, then, one of the most eminent English barristers, whose works are considered standard with us, declares that a libelous press may be considered a nuisance; and our own charter, given us by the legislature of this state, gives us the power to remove nuisances; and by ordering that press abated as a nuisance, we conceived that we were acting strictly in accordance with law. We made that order in our corporate capacity, and the City Marshall carried it out. It is possible there may have been some better way, but I must confess that I could not see it.

9"In relation to the writ served upon us, we were willing to abide the consequences of our own acts, but were unwilling, in answering a writ of that kind, to submit to illegal exactions sought to be imposed upon us under the pretense of law, when they knew they were in open violation of it.

10"When that document was presented to me by Mr. Bettisworth, I offered, in the presence of more than twenty persons, to go to any other magistrate, either in our city, in Appanoose [a township north of Nauvoo], or any other place where we should be safe, but we all refused to put ourselves into the power of a mob.

10"What right had that constable to refuse our request? He had none according to law; for you know, Governor Ford, that the statute law in Illinois is, that the parties served with the writ 'shall go before him who issued it, or some other justice of the peace.' Why, then, should we be dragged to Carthage, where the law does not compel us to go? Does not this look like many others of our persecutions with which you are acquainted? And have we not a right to expect foul play?

10"This very act was a breach of law on his part-an assumption of power that did not belong to him, and an attempt, at least, to deprive us of our legal and constitutional rights and privileges. What could we do, under the circumstances, different from what we did do? We sued for, and obtained a writ of habeas corpus from the municipal court, by which we were delivered from the hands of Constable Bettisworth, and brought before and acquitted by the municipal court.

10"After our acquittal, in a conversation with Judge Thomas [Jesse Thomas of the circuit court, in Hancock County at the time], although he considered the acts of the party illegal, he advised that, to satisfy the people, we had better go before another magistrate who was not in our Church.

10"In accordance with his advice we went before Esquire Wells, with whom you are well acquainted; both parties were present, witnesses were called on both sides, the case was fully investigated, and we were again dismissed.

10"And what is this pretended desire to enforce law, and these lying, base rumors put into circulation for, but to seek, through mob influence, under pretense of law, to make us submit to requisitions that are contrary to law, and subversive of every principle of justice?

11"And when you, sir, required us to come out here, we came, not because it was legal, but because you required it of us, and we were desirous of showing to you and to all men that we shrunk not from the most rigid investigation of our acts.

11"We certainly did expect other treatment than to be immured in a jail at the instance of these men, and I think, from your plighted faith, we had a right to, after disbanding our own forces, and putting ourselves entirely in your hands; and now, after having fulfilled my part, sir, as a man and an American citizen, I call upon you, Governor Ford, to deliver us from this place, and rescue us from this outrage that is sought to be practiced upon us by a set of infamous scoundrels."

11Governor Ford: "But you have placed men under arrest, detained men as prisoners, and given passes to others, some of which I have seen."

11 John P. Greene, Nauvoo City Marshall: "Perhaps I can explain. Since these difficulties have commenced, you are aware that we have been placed under very peculiar circumstances; our city has been placed under a very rigid police guard; in addition to this, frequent guards have been placed outside the city to prevent any sudden surprise, and those guards have questioned suspected or suspicious persons as to their business.

11"To strangers, in some instances, passes have been given, to prevent difficulty in passing those guards. It is some of those passes that you have seen. No person, sir, has been imprisoned without a legal cause in our city."

11Governor Ford: "Why did you not give a more speedy answer to the posse that I sent out?"

11Joseph Smith: "We had matters of importance to consult upon. Your letter showed anything but an amicable spirit. We have suffered immensely in Missouri from mobs, in loss of property, imprisonment, and otherwise.

12"It took some time for us to weigh duly these matters. We could not decide upon the matters of such importance immediately, and your posse were too hasty in returning. We were consulting for a large people, and vast interests were at stake.

12"We had been outrageously imposed upon, and knew not how far we could trust anyone; besides, a question necessarily arose, how shall we come? Your request was that we should come unarmed. It became a matter of serious importance to decide how far promises could be trusted, and how far we were safe from mob violence."

12Colonel Thomas Geddes, Fountain Green Militia: "It certainly did look from all I have heard, from the general spirit of violence and mobocracy that prevails here, that it was not safe for you to come unprotected."

12Governor Ford: "I think that sufficient time was not allowed by the posse for you to consult and get ready. They were too hasty; but I suppose they found themselves bound by their orders. I think, too, there is a great deal of truth in what you say, and your reasoning is plausible; yet, I must beg leave to differ from you in relation to the acts of the city council. That council in my opinion had no right to act in a legislative capacity, and in that of the judiciary.

12"They should have passed a law in relation to the matter, and then the municipal court, upon complaint, could have removed it [the Expositor press]; but for the city council to take upon themselves the law-making and the execution of the law, in my opinion was wrong; besides, these men ought to have had a hearing before their property was destroyed; to destroy it without was an infringement of their rights; besides, it is so contrary to the feelings of the American people to interfere with the press.

12"And furthermore, I cannot but think that it would have been more judicious for you to have gone with Mr. Bettisworth to Carthage, notwithstanding the law did not require it. Concerning your being in jail, I am sorry for that, I wish it had been otherwise. I hope you will soon be released, but I cannot interfere."

13Joseph Smith: "Governor Ford, allow me, sir, to bring one thing to your mind, that you seem to have overlooked. You state that you think it would have been better for us to have submitted to the requisition of Constable Bettisworth, and to have gone to Carthage.

13"Do you not know, sir, that that writ was served at the instance of an anti-Mormon mob, who had passed resolutions and published them to the effect that they would exterminate the Mormon leaders; and are you not informed that Captain Anderson was not only threatened when coming to Nauvoo, but had a gun fired at his boat by this mob at Warsaw, when coming up to Nauvoo, and that this very thing was made use of as a means to get us into their hands; and we could not, without taking an armed force with us, go there without, according to their published declarations, going into the jaws of death?

13"To have taken a force would only have fanned the excitement, as they would have stated that we wanted to use intimidation; therefore we thought it the most judicious to avail ourselves of the protection of the law."

13Governor Ford: "I see, I see."

13Joseph Smith: "Furthermore, in relation to the press, you say that you differ with me in opinion; be it so, the thing after all is a legal difficulty, and the courts, I should judge, are competent to decide on that matter.

13"If our act was illegal, we are willing to meet it; and although I cannot see the distinction that you draw about the acts of the city council, and what difference it could have made in point of fact, law, or justice, between the city council's acting together or separate, or how much more legal it would have been for the municipal court, who were a part of the city council, to act separate, instead of with the councilors, yet, if it is deemed that we did wrong in destroying that press, we refuse not to pay for it. We are desirous to fulfill the law in every particular, and are responsible for our acts.

14"You say that the parties ought to have had a hearing. Had it been a civil suit, this of course would have been proper; but there was a flagrant violation of every principle of right, a nuisance; and it was abated on the same principle that any nuisance, stench, or putrefied carcass would have been removed.

14"Our first step, therefore, was to stop the foul, noisome, filthy sheet, and then the next, in our opinion, would have been to have prosecuted the men for a breach of public decency.

14"And furthermore, again, let me say, Governor Ford, I shall look to you for our protection. I believe you are talking of going to Nauvoo; if you go, sir, I wish to go along. I refuse not to answer any law, but I do not consider myself safe here."

14Governor Ford: "I am in hopes that you will be acquitted; but if I go, I will certainly take you along. I do not, however, apprehend danger. I think you are perfectly safe, either here or anywhere else. I cannot, however, interfere with the law. I am placed in peculiar circumstances and seem to be blamed by all parties."

14Joseph Smith: "Governor, I ask nothing but what is legal. I have a right to expect protection at least from you; for, independent of law, you have pledged your faith, and that of the state, for my protection."

14Governor Ford: "And you shall have protection, General Smith. I did not make this promise without consulting my officers, who all pledged their honor to its fulfillment. I do not know that I shall go tomorrow to Nauvoo, but if I do, I will take you along."

14It was over.

14The Governor and Colonel Geddes bade the prisoners goodbye and left the room. As they walked down the stairs, the two continued to converse on the matter. Leaving the grounds, Ford said, "Oh, it's all nonsense; you will have to drive these Mormons out yet." To this the colonel replied, "If we undertake that, Governor, when the proper time comes, will you interfere?"

15The Governor answered, "No, I will not," and then, after a pause, added, "until you are through."

15It had been quite a morning, Stigall reflected.

15Now through the window he could see the officer of the guard, Lt. Frank A. Worrell, marching a change of guard up Walnut Street from the bivouac area on the courthouse square.

15Stigall laughed aloud. The lieutenant was approaching with a guard mount composed of men from the ranks of the permanent Greys. Since the excitement began, many of the new men had come into the Greys for the pay check or the beckon of adventure, and when on duty at the jail delighted in talking to the Mormon Prophet. This had caused Worrell some trouble in keeping a full guard, for after their visits, Stigall had more than once heard the words, "Let's go home men, for I will not fight any longer against these men."

15As Worrell stationed the guards, Stigall could hear him ordering the guards to refrain from talking with the prisoners or their friends; only one man was to be inside the building and he on the landing away from the door; and the others-two at the outside entrance and the other five stationed around the building-were to permit only those with a pass to come or go from the jail.

15Carthage jail was home for George W. Stigall. It stood on the north side of Walnut Street on the northwest edge of town, and sometimes in the late afternoon on an overcast day, if the sun broke through on it, the yellow stone seemed to glow. A two-story building, it was nearly three blocks from the courthouse square: one block north and one and three-fourths blocks west of the square's northwest corner.

15The building went by three names, the "old jail," the county jail, and Carthage jail. It was a well-constructed building. Many years later, when being restored as an historic site, high praise would be given to those who built it.

16Situated on one-fourth of a city block, or about one acre, it had seven rooms in the building and a lean-to on the northeast comer used as a kitchen. The jail measured thirty-four feet north to south and was twenty-eight feet wide.

16On the east side of the building in front of the lean-to and just forward of the east windows was a well.

16The jail stood eighteen feet back from the road.

16On the first floor were the living room, dining room, and debtor's room. Entering the oversized front door set on the extreme left of the building's south face, one was greeted by a long hall. To the left the stairs going to the second floor hugged the wall, and straight down the hall a barred door opened to the debtor's room. The debtor's room was about twelve by twelve feet in size and had two windows, one across the room from the door, on the north, the second opening to the west where one could look on the woods that fringed the jail. Both windows were barred.

16It was in this room that Joseph Smith, with ten of his friends, spent his second night in Carthage.

16Immediately to the right on entering the front door was the living room. A large room, it had a fine fireplace in the center of the north wall, on the right of which a door opened into the dining room. Two windows in the living room faced front for a southern exposure, and a third opened to the east where one could look out on the well, the homes across the street, the large unkept lots between, and weed-lined Walnut Street.

16The dining room had one window, opening north, and a door on the east that opened onto the lean-to porch. The kitchen was made of wood.

16The stairs to the second floor opened onto a landing which ran all the way back to the south wall where a window emitted light. Slightly to the right, on reaching the top of the stairs, a door opened to the cell that extended the full width of the jail. To the left, in the cell, was a window, to the right an inner cell.

17It was to this cell that Stigall hoped to persuade the Mormons to move.

17On the east of the landing, a few feet back from the head of the stairs, another door led to the jailer's bedroom-the room in which the prisoners were now staying. The bedroom was similar to the living room directly below it: a fireplace on the north wall, a window on the east, and two windows on the south. The room measured sixteen by sixteen feet.

17A small pole fence surrounded the jail, and within the one-acre enclosure were a half-dozen trees. Beyond the fence on the north and west began the irregular woods and the prairie, over which people now trailed. The angry, the curious, the worried.

17Joseph Smith, the Mormon leader, was in Carthage jail, and it was rumored he was to be killed.

17While Jailer Stigall was pondering over his note from the Governor delivered to him by the prisoners' friend coming up from the Hamilton House, Joseph Smith, above him on the second floor, again brought up the note he held in his hand. It had been carried to him by Joel S. Mills and came from William Clayton, who in 1847 would write the hymn, "Come, Come, Ye Saints," during the exodus to the Great Basin.

17Wrote Clayton:

"Nauvoo, June 26th, 1844 "Dear President,-

17"I write this line to inform you that Mr. Marsh, who lives down the river, and of whom you have had corn, pork, etc., has sent word if you want any bail he is ready for one to any amount; and further, that he has got some corn left which he wants you to have, lest the mob get it. (We will endeavor to obtain it.)

17"They have already taken two loads, but he has charged them a dollar a bushel for it.

18"The Amaranth has just landed at the foot of Main Street, and unloaded 200 bbls. flour,-95 for Mr. Kimball, and the balance for Bryant.

18"Captain Singleton, who came at the head of the police this morning, is sending a request to the Governor to call them home. He says he finds no difficulties to settle here, but there is plenty to settle at home. He furthermore says that while the police were at Carthage they were treated as soldiers, but since they came to Nauvoo they have been treated as gentlemen.

18"The company all got home safe and well last night.

18"A messenger is about to start forthwith to Judge Thomas.

18"All is peace in Nauvoo. Many threats keep coming that a mob are determined to attack the city in your absence, but we have no fears.

18"With fervency and true friendship, I remain yours eternally,

William Clayton"

18The Captain Singleton of whom Clayton wrote headed the militia from Brown County, some 12 miles southeast of the Hancock County line. He had been brought to Carthage by the Governor and sent to the Mormon city of Nauvoo to put down supposed rioting by the Saints-as the Mormons were called-and to guard against a rumored plundering of the city by those who had pressed the charges against the Smiths.

18The militia from Brown County was one of several called by the Governor, the others being from the counties of McDonough and Schuyler and the cities of Green Plains, Augusta, Warsaw, and Carthage in Hancock County; the last-named city having two companies, the Carthage Greys and the Riflemen. In all, the Governor estimated his forces to be from twelve to thirteen hundred men in Carthage with an additional four to five hundred men assembled in Warsaw, a total of over seventeen hundred troops.

19During the day there were many men coming and going from the prisoners' quarters. They included Dan Jones, Stephen Markham, Dr. John Bernhisel, Cyrus C. Canfield, Lorenzo D. Wasson, John S. Fullmer, Cyrus P. Wheelock, John Smith, and John P. Greene, all members of the Mormon Church, and H. P. Reid of Madison, Iowa Territory, and James W. Woods of Burlington, Iowa Territory, the Prophet's two lawyers.

19Other men stopped at the jail briefly and it was by one of these men that the Prophet sent a message to his lawyer, Woods, now at the Hamilton House, to get subpoenas for Samuel Jones, Edward Hunter, and Philip B. Lewis to appear in court the next day with the papers they had carried to the Governor at Springfield-papers which the Governor had not seen, he having started for Carthage before they arrived.

19It was nearing seven o'clock in the evening when the man left bearing the message.

7:00 p.m.

There were still scattered black clouds in the sky as the sun dropped beyond the Mississippi River to the west. Beginning at about nine o'clock the previous evening it had rained heavily for several hours. The rain had been general throughout the tri-state area of Illinois, Iowa, and Missouri. The high ground was damp and the low ground muddy.

19It had been an unusually wet spring. The season had only recently changed to permit ploughing, and very little planting had been done.

19There was high flood water on the river, with reports that many grist mills had been swept away, water up to the second stories on warehouses on Water Street in St. Louis, and the loss of many homes and stock all along the rivers.

20Captain Singleton finished his organization among the members of the police force at Nauvoo. He had arrived from Carthage at eight that morning with a mounted militia of sixty men taken from the McDonough County militia, although he himself was from Brown County. They were meeting in the Masonic hall, a handsome three-story building of red brick and white trim where earlier at the Governor's request the state arms had been collected from the Nauvoo Legion.

20Singleton had been sent to the Mormon city with orders from the Governor to take command of the police force and to exercise such means as necessary to maintain peace and order. Since arriving he had found little to do in that regard. Prior to his leaving for Nauvoo, he was in a group with Dr. Robert D. Foster, one of the apostate Mormons bringing charges against Joseph Smith. Foster declared he could offer solid proof of that segment of the charges concerning the printing of bogus money in Nauvoo. When challenged by Singleton, Foster named three separate locations where presses could be found.

20But after a day of thorough searching, Singleton's men could find nothing.

20On entering the city he had publicly read his orders from the Governor, then asked the citizens if they would obey his orders as head of the police force. The answer was yes. Since many members of the police force had been pressed into service with the Nauvoo Legion, the system needed reorganizing; and he had called the just-completed 6 p.m. meeting at the Masonic hall for that purpose.

20Association with the citizens proved the Governor's fears of rebellion in the city unfounded and the Nauvoo Legion sufficient to care for the safety of the area.

20Singleton and his men wished to return to Carthage, thence to home; there was soil that needed tilling and winter wheat soon to be harvested. In the afternoon he had written a letter to the Governor to that effect.

20Now, the temporary organization under his command completed, he left the Masonic hall. He carried in his breast pocket an order which he would issue at midnight notifying the Nauvoo Legion to be ready to parade at an hour's notice.

21There was the smell of rain in the air.

21In the upper bedroom at Carthage jail, Willard Richards lighted a candle and placed it on the table. There was a great variety of conversation among those in the room. Jumping from one experience to another, they discussed their present and former grievances, the spirit of the troops about them, the temperament of the Governor.

21While they were talking of deliverance, John Taylor said, "Brother Joseph, if you will permit it, and say the word, I will have you out of this prison in five hours, if the jail has to come down to do it." It was Taylor's idea to go to Nauvoo and collect a force sufficient to do the job, since he considered the whole affair a "legal farce and a flagrant outrage upon our liberty and rights."

21Joseph refused.

21A little later they began laying their legal plans-the nature of testimony required, the gathering of witnesses, the disposition of the court.

21They concluded by talking of their hopes for the Church. At a quarter to eight they took supper.

21Of the men, Joseph was the most imposing figure. He stood six feet high and weighed two hundred pounds. His chest and shoulders were broad and muscular, while his hands were small for the size of his body. He was known as a powerful speaker, yet his voice was low and soft. Of light complexion, he had light brown hair that gave way to a full brow, full cheeks, and thin lips. Of his person, it was his eyes that gained one's attention, and all who met him commented upon them. Only a month earlier the Weekly Gazette of St. Louis had said his eyes were "the Prophet's most remarkable featureÂ�.The hue is light hazel, and it is shaded, and, at times, almost veiled by the longest, thickest light lashes you ever saw belonging to a man." The Prophet's friend Parley P. Pratt recorded that his eyes were blue, "Â�and there was something connected with the serene and steady penetrating glance of his eye, as if he would penetrate the deepest abyss of the human heart, gaze into eternity, penetrate the heaven and comprehend all worlds."

22Those visiting Nauvoo often commented upon the Prophet's quick wit, his hospitality and cheerfulness, and that upon seeing him for the first time they knew instantly he was the Mormon Prophet.

22He was fond of little children, and was never known to pass a child, however small, without speaking to him.

22In running, jumping, wrestling, and pulling sticks he was a champion. Through the years he pulled sticks and wrestled with many county champions, but seldom met his match.

22Despite the fact that the Saints loved Joseph deeply, few, if any, fully comprehended him. On occasion he had said, "Brethren, you do not know me, you do not know who I am."

22Of this, George Q. Cannon wrote: "The Saints could not comprehend Joseph Smith; the Elders could not; the Apostles could not. They did so a little toward the close of his life; but his knowledge was so extensive and his comprehension so great that they could not rise to it."

22Including an adopted boy and girl, Joseph and Emma were the parents of eleven children, one of whom was born five months after the martyrdom. Three boys and the adopted girl, the oldest, were living when Joseph made his fateful journey to Carthage.

22Hyrum Smith was Joseph's older brother by five years. An immediate believer in his brother's mission, he was one of the eight persons permitted to view and handle the plates from which the Book of Mormon was translated, and his name is prefixed to it as a witness. In 1844 he was forty-four years of age and the father of eight children, six by his first wife Jerusha Barden who died in 1837, two by Mary Fielding whom he married later the same year.

23Like his brother, Hyrum was a large man and born in Vermont. He spent his early years engaged in agriculture in western New York. He deeply loved his brother Joseph and was never away from him more than six months at a time during their lifetime. Had he lived he would have followed Joseph to the presidency of the Church.

23Both the other stationary men, Richards and Taylor, were members of the Council of the Twelve Apostles. At the time they were the only two members of the Council on the western frontier, the other ten being on missions to the east.

23The Prophet's secretary, Willard Richards, was forty years old, a native of Massachusetts, and a physician by profession. Typical of many doctors, he was careless in his dress and portly through the waist. In 1835, while practicing medicine near Boston, a Book of Mormon, left with his cousin by Brigham Young, fell into his hands. Opening the book at random, he read only half a page, then declared: "God or the devil has had a hand in that book, for man never wrote it."

23Soon after, he set about the task of settling his affairs so that he could join the Saints in Kirtland, Ohio. He was baptized in that city by Brigham Young in 1836. He had served a mission to the eastern states and to England. After joining the Saints in Illinois, prior to moving to Nauvoo, he and his wife had lived in Warsaw.

23By birth, John Taylor was an Englishman. When twenty, he moved to Toronto, upper Canada. Converted to the Mormon faith by missionaries in that country, he moved with the Saints to Missouri and later to Illinois. Like Richards, he had served a mission to England, and while there had introduced the gospel he preached to Ireland and the Isle of Man.

23He was the editor of the Church newspaper, the Times and Seasons, and a city news sheet, the Nauvoo Neighbor. A handsome man, Taylor was thirty-five, married, and the father of two.

Following supper, Dan Jones, who had accompanied the Prophet to jail, continued to hew on the door to the room. He and Stephen Markham had spent the forenoon working on it with a penknife. It was warped and would not latch. As he scraped, Jones heard the front door open below and the guards move. Looking, he saw the two counselors, Woods and Reid, and John P. Greene, the Nauvoo City Marshall, coming up the stairs.

24The three reported that they had just left a meeting at the Hamilton House in which the Governor and the military officers had decided to march to Nauvoo at eight o'clock the next morning.

24A silence fell over the room.

24The question, Will the prisoners go also? was not asked. Nor did any comment follow on the breaking of the chief executive's word. Whether or not Ford's action came as a surprise is unknown. The action did, however, confirm the Mormon position. From this point forward, regardless of the overt action displayed by either party, the line was clearly drawn and clearly understood.

24Finally, Greene said the move was to gratify the troops. "The Governor expects to return the following day," he concluded.

24"All the troops?" Joseph asked.

24"All but fifty-whose fidelity the Governor can most rely on to guard the jail."

24These, of course, the prisoners felt, would come from the ranks of the McDonough troops.

24"And the trial?"

25"Postponed until Saturday. The justice wants to accompany his troops."

25The question, Why do the troops want to march to Nauvoo? was not asked either. Nor was it necessary to do so. The answer was known.

25It was 8:15 when Patriarch John Smith met Almon W. Babbitt, a Mormon lawyer, in Macedonia. Earlier in the day, in the jail at Carthage, his nephew, the Prophet, had asked him to contact Babbitt to "come and assist me as an attorney at my trial tomorrow before Captain R. F. Smith."

25The patriarch had left Macedonia-a town nine miles northeast of Carthage-upon learning that his nephews had been charged with treason. Recognized by mobbers en route, he was stopped and his life threatened, but he refused to turn back, and as he pushed on to Carthage three of the men raised their firearms and snapped their guns at him. When he had gained the jail he was refused admission, and Joseph, seeing him from the window, called down to the guard, "Let the old man come in, he is my uncle."

25"I don't care who the hell he's uncle to, he'll not go in," answered the guard.

25"You'll not hinder so old and infirm a man as he from coming in," the Prophet had joked, and then added, "Come in, Uncle."

25The guard grunted, searched him thoroughly, and passed him on.

25For an hour he had conversed with the men in the upstairs room. At one point he asked Joseph if he thought he should again get out of the hands of his enemies, and Joseph replied, "My brother Hyrum thinks I shall."

25Now, back in Macedonia, John Smith conveyed his nephew's message to Babbitt, to which the lawyer returned, "You are too late. I am already engaged on the other side!"

25Captain Robert F. Smith of the Carthage Greys, and a Carthage justice of the peace, stepped out onto the board sidewalk that fronted the Hamilton House. Lounging along the picket fence and squatting on the sidewalk with their feet in the street, passing whiskey and spitting tobacco, were dozens of men in and out of uniform. To his left the courthouse building was silhouetted against the skyline, and from somewhere in the shadows beneath came the sound of a Jews' harp and, about it, voices singing a doggerel.

26Walking west to the corner and then turning left, Captain Smith angled across the courthouse square, picking his way among the horse dung and the idle traffic. As did others, he avoided the sidewalk after dark because the frequent holes in the planking made its use dangerous. Going into a little box he used as an office, he lighted a candle and sorted through some papers on his desk. Picking up one, he placed it nearer the candle and, dipping his pen in the ink, marked through the figure 27 and above it wrote 29.

26In turn he altered the dates on the other papers-subpoenas for the trial the next day.

26He had not consulted the Prophet's lawyers concerning the change, nor did he intend to. An opportunity to lead the Carthage Greys to Nauvoo had finally presented itself, and he was not going to let his job as justice of the peace keep him from it. He smiled. The Prophet shouldn't care about the change-when the captain had set the trial so early, the Mormons charged he wasn't allowing them sufficient time to prepare their defense. Now they had the time.

26He put the altered papers into a pigeonhole at the top of the desk and went into the street. He had promised the boys at Hamilton's a dram of whiskey, so he made his way there through the troops camped in small, strung-out squads.

26There was a noticeable difference between the Greys on the square and those men of the militia camped at the edge of town. The Greys were a well-drilled company, their officers calling them together often. They held a long and proud tradition of service in the less populated area of northern Illinois. As one would surmise, their uniforms were a silver grey in color, and in appearance were much like those used in the Mexican War.

27In contraposition, the other militias' military duties were generally limited to occasional dress parades and as required by law-the annual "cornstalk" musters, a burlesque military drill that offered, under the cloak of patriotism, all the necessary ingredients for good fellowship.

27Captain Smith was a good military man. At the outbreak of the War between the States, he would be made a colonel of the 16th Illinois Regiment at Quincy. In time he would march with Sherman to the sea. Upon the occupation of Savannah, Georgia, he would be made its military governor. By the spring of 1865 he would be brevetted as a brigadier general.

27Now the captain swept a last glance over his troops in the square. Tomorrow he would lead them to Nauvoo.

27Hamilton's was the city's only hotel. The place went by many names-Hamilton House, Hamilton's Tavern, and Hamilton Hotel, of which any was considered proper; and by the hotel, the tavern, and Hamilton's. It was generally referred to as Hamilton's.

27The building stood half a block east of the courthouse square on the south side of North Main street. It was a two-story board building with a wing facing the street and a second wing facing west. It was called after its owner, Artois Hamilton, one of the early settlers in the county.

27When Captain Robert Smith entered, the boys were joshing Hamilton about being an old citizen who had turned anti-Mormon, and someone said, "Artois is an anti-Mormon who wants to be known as an old citizen." Everyone laughed.

27There were four sharply defined classes of people in Hancock County: (1) Mormons; (2) Jack-Mormons, not members of the Church, but supporters for political or personal gain, or sympathizers; (3) Old citizens, those who were anti-Mormon at heart, but opposed to lawlessness; (4) Anti-Mormons, those advocating war and extermination either as a lawless adventure or for economic betterment.

28Carthage had very few of the first two groups among its near-four-hundred population. The city was eleven years old and was known to have a neglected appearance and an abundance of immense rodents in the surrounding underbrush. Otherwise, the city was like most other midwest county seats of the time, with nearly all the business houses clustered around the courthouse square, and with homes of clapboard, stone, and brick.

28Carthage-from the Phoenician and Hebrew tongues, meaning "New City"-is eleven miles east of the Mississippi River at its nearest point. When Illinois was made a state, the area was encompassed in Madison, and later in Pike and Adams Counties. On January 13, 1825, Hancock County was organized by an act of the legislature, but it was a dependent county until 1829 (its business being transacted in Adams) when it had a population of 350 persons. Then a court order called for the election of officers. In 1833 an act of the legislature designated the county commissioners of Morgan County to select a site on the prairie for a "new city" to be the county seat.

28Fort Edwards, later known as Warsaw, made a bid to be the permanent seat of government. It had previously been made the voting place for Hancock County by the Adams County court in 1825. There is no mention of any real trouble on the decision to create a new county seat, although through the ensuing years several attempts have been made to move the county seat to Warsaw. These attempts have at times generated heated feelings.

28As with most neighboring cities, petty jealousies existed between Carthage and Warsaw. Both expected a strong, steady growth, if not a boom, neither of which came. Land speculators occupied both cities, as was common on the American frontier, and their voices were loud.

29At first the land speculators welcomed the new settlement of Nauvoo, feeling that a new prosperity would fall to Warsaw and Carthage as a result. But when the Mormon city continued to grow while their cities remained fairly constant in size, their resentment grew. At one time, a delegation from Warsaw visited the Prophet in Nauvoo in an attempt to have him establish part of the Mormon gathering-the details of which were never revealed-in Warsaw, but outside the scattering of the Mormons throughout the county of their own accord, the Prophet never acted on the suggestion, and this only heightened the feeling against the new city of Nauvoo.

29It was 1839 when Joseph Smith stood on the Nauvoo bluff overlooking the Commerce tract and decided to build a city there. West of the river below him marked the beginning of the uncharted miles of prairie and mountains that were inhabited by hostile Indians and a scattering of white men who had been disgorged from regulated eastern society. The American frontier ran from the Green Bay in Wisconsin southwest to the Mississippi River, down the river to the Territory of Iowa and on to the Missouri line, thence west to the western boundary of Missouri and south to Arkansas and the Republic of Texas.

29Admitted to the Union in 1818, Illinois in 1844 was a sparsely settled state of 625,000 people, most of whom resided south of Springfield. One of the great thoroughfares from the east to the west passed through southern Illinois, crossing the Ohio river at Golconda. Much of the country is hilly, and at that time was heavily timbered. Mammoth oaks, poplars, hickories, pecans, and other trees were abundant. The hillsides abounded in springs of clear water. The forests offered game, the rivers fish. The bottomlands were covered with cane and grass.

29Those making the crossing from east to west found themselves in a country where a living could be made with little work and above all, where they were on free government soil. And so they stopped. Others came down the Ohio River on flat boats or keel boats, and they too remained.

30The early immigrants came from Georgia, the Carolinas, Tennessee, Kentucky, and Virginia.

30So it was that southern Illinois was thickly settled when the greater portion of the state was almost uninhabited.

30Relying on the growing of grain crops and the grazing of livestock on the grassy plains, Illinois farmers in 1844 were for the most part without adequate money to buy a comfortable home, seed, or farm equipment. (Cyrus McCormick's automatic reaper and John Deere's steel plow had both appeared in 1835.) The farmers were on a subsistence existence, selling what they could, buying only what they needed. All looked for better times, but the panic of 1837 had depressed prices, and they didn't rise until nine years later.

30Farm laborers were paid their board and room and fifty cents a day when they worked. Horses sold for sixty dollars; beef was three dollars a hundredweight; pork, eight dollars a barrel. Wheat sold for thirty cents a hundred pounds; flour, two dollars for a barrel; corn, for fifteen cents a bushel; and the best hams, for three cents a pound.

30Of the other large class of people in Illinois at the time, the merchants, Governor Ford commented: "The great majority, in fact nearly all the merchants, were merely bloodsuckers, men who with but very little capital, a small stock of goods, and with ideas of business not broader than their ribbards, nor deeper than their colors, sold for money down or on credit for cash, which when they received they sent out of the country."

30It wasn't until eleven years later (1855) that a public common school system was established, and thirteen years later that the first public secondary school was created.

30Newspapers mushroomed overnight, only to disappear. It was the editor of one of these, Elijah P. Lovejoy, who left his position as editor of the St. Louis Times to become a Presbyterian minister, who kindled the flames of abolition in Illinois. After writing in his St. Louis Observer several heated anti-slavery articles, he was driven by the Missourians from their state. Landing in Alton, Illinois, his press was destroyed by the citizens of that city, after which Lovejoy disclaimed being an abolitionist. After several months of quiet publishing he again took up the pen for the cause, and his press was again destroyed. He set up a third press and it too was destroyed. During a riot, while defending the arrival of his fourth press in 1837, he was killed.

31Nevertheless the movement to abolish slavery was under way, and in 1844 it was a force to be reckoned with in Illinois.

31Financially the state was bankrupt. In 1841 Governor Ford reported that the state debt was $12,186,756, on which the yearly interest was $696,530. Not one project, said the Governor, was returning profits to the treasury; nor was there enough money in the hands of the people to pay the interest on the debt for one year. The condition was reflected in the sale of Illinois bonds which were quoted at eleven cents on the dollar. In 1842 both the State Bank at Springfield and the Bank of Illinois at Shawneetown went bankrupt, and the following year they were forced into liquidation.

31As with Missouri across the river, mobocracy was common in the scattered areas of the state. Earlier, Hancock County had been overrun by horse thieves and counterfeiters. They counted in their numbers sheriffs, judges, and constables. In 1831, a gang in Pope County became so strong that they constructed a fort.

31Thus it became necessary for the law-abiding citizens to band together in "regulator" groups to stand against the lawless, which activity was encouraged by high state officials. The Pope County gang was stamped out, yet lawlessness was the most rampant during the term of Governor Ford. The Governor explained this condition by the fact that the law couldn't furnish enough strength "in the face of popular excitement."

32In time, however, it was the regulators whom the citizens grew to fear.

32The frontier social life included poker and the grand ball on Saturday night, depending upon one's taste. During the week, for the women, it might include gossiping while at the "mercantile," or participating in a quilting bee; and for the men some such athletic contest as pulling sticks, wrestling, pitching horseshoes, or racing horses.

32In New York at that time three modern newspapers were the New York Sun, published since 1832; the New York Herald, since 1835; and the New York Telegram, which had arrived in 1841. The women read Lady's Book, and the men read the North American Review. Most of the books published were cheap reprints of foreign classics.

32In the east, employers paid twelve to fifteen cents an hour, had morning prayers, and required employees to be churchmen-and, in many cases, abolitionists.

32Only 5 percent of the population were literate in 1840, yet in most of the twenty-six states in the Union public opinion was strongly against free public education.

32A decade earlier a religious revival had swept the nation in the Protestant sects, and now the Catholic Church in America was enjoying a substantial growth through the acquisition of the Louisiana Purchase and the rapid influx of Irish immigrants. With this growth came added discrimination and persecution.

32The twenty-six states of the Union would increase to twenty-eight a year later with the addition of Florida and Texas, and in 1850 far-off California would be admitted. During 1844 Samuel Morse's electric telegraph began operating between Washington and Baltimore, and James Knox Polk was elected the eleventh president of the United States.

32People's eyes were turning west ideas of expansion were in the air; it was the rise of the common man; the call of manifest destiny.

9:00 p.m.

Thomas Ford walked to his bed chambers in the Hamilton House. For the past hour he had been in council on the Mormon question; that, coupled with his journey from Springfield and the events of the past few days, had left him fatigued and he desired to retire early.

33He knew, however, he would not be allowed the pleasure.

33In the Hamilton House were dozens of men, some allied with the Mormon cause, while others, who comprised the majority, were anti-Mormon. In the streets were others, all gathered to determine one point-the fate of the Smith followers.

33Following several days' journey from the capital, during which he collected forces along the way, the Governor had arrived in Carthage to find the city in great excitement. Of his journey and early action in Carthage, he recorded: "On the morning before my arrival at Carthage, I was awakened at an early hour by the frightful report, which was asserted with confidence and apparent consternation, that the Mormons had already commenced the work of burning, destruction, and murder, and that every man capable of bearing arms was instantly wanted at Carthage for the protection of the country. We lost no time in starting; but when we arrived at Carthage we could hear no more concerning this story.

33"Again: during the few days that the militias were encamped at Carthage, frequent applications were made to me to send a force here and a force there, and a force all about the country, to prevent murders, mobberies, and larcenies, which it was said were threatened by the Mormons. No such forces were sent, nor were any such offenses committed at that time, except some stealing of some provisions, and there was never the least proof that this was done by a Mormon.

33"Again: on my late visit to Hancock County, I was informed by some of their violent enemies that the larcenies of the Mormons had become unusually numerous and insufferable. They indeed admitted that but little had been done in this way in their immediate vicinity; but they insisted that sixteen horses had been stolen by the Mormons in one night, near Lima, in the county of Adams.

34"At the close of the expedition, I called at this same town of Lima, and upon inquiry was told that no horses had been stolen in that neighborhood, but that sixteen horses had been stolen in one night in Hancock County. This last informant being told of the Hancock story, again changed the venue to another distant settlement in the northern edge of Adams.

34"As my object in visiting Hancock was expressly to assist in the execution of the laws, and not to violate them, or to witness or permit their violation, as I was convinced that the Mormon leaders had committed a crime in the destruction of the press, and had resisted the execution of process, I determined to exert the whole force of the state, if necessary, to bring them to justice.

34"But seeing the great excitement in the public mind, and the manifest tendency of this excitement to run into mobocracy, I was of opinion that before I acted I ought to obtain a pledge from the officers and men to support me in strictly legal measures, and to protect the prisoners in case they surrendered; for I was determined, if possible, that the forms of law should not be made the cat's-paw of a mob, to seduce these people to a quiet surrender, as the convenient victims of popular fury.

34"I therefore called together the whole force then assembled at Carthage, and made an address, explaining to them what I could, and what I could not legally do, and also adducing to them various reasons why they, as well as the Mormons, should submit to the laws, and why, if they had resolved upon revolutionary proceedings, their purpose should be abandoned.

34"The assembled troops seemed much pleased with the address, and upon its conclusion the officers and men unanimously voted, with acclamation, to sustain me in a strictly legal course, and that the prisoners should be protected from violence.

34"Upon the arrival of additional forces from Warsaw, McDonough, and Schuyler, similar addresses were made, with the same result.

35"It seemed to me that these votes fully authorized me to promise the accused Mormons the protection of the law in case they surrendered.

35"They were accordingly duly informed that if they surrendered they would be protected, and if they did not, the whole force of the state would be called out, if necessary, to compel their submission."

35Often Thomas Ford found himself looking beyond the troubles in Hancock County. As of late a bill had been passed in the legislature providing for the creation of a new county-Massac-taken from the confines of the already troubled Pope and Johnson Counties. To Ford's mind, the 1843 legislature had erected a ready-made hotbed for currently existing differences between the Regulators and Flatheads in southern Illinois.

35Horse thieving and counterfeiting were common-and so were their aftermaths: barn-burning, whipping, and assassination. What had gone before, he feared, was only laying the groundwork for what would come.

35The trouble had begun in 1831. So entrenched had the Flatheads become that those desiring peace were driven to form regulator groups. The conflict had now reached the point-which is always reached in such movements-where all good men were not Regulators and all bad men Flatheads. Indeed, the cruelties perpetrated by some of the Regulators were such that many good men had begun to revolt. "It was a daily occurrence at this time for men under the charge of being Flatheads to be taken out and unmercifully beaten or tortured. Men were strapped across logs and their bare backs beaten to a pulp with hickory withes. Some were tied to trees with weights hung to their arms and compelled to stand until their tongues protruded from their mouths."

35Ford viewed with alarm the struggle for political control of the new county. The Regulators, following the example set for them in other counties, formed themselves into companies with captains and leaders, and the Flatheads, likewise, had their organization. Now open warfare was breaking out. Hundreds of men were marching against one another. "Undesirable" families were being expelled from their homes. Death had ceased to be a stranger.

36As Ford feared, the situation would worsen, finally coming to the point that citizens in a mass convention in Franklin County would declare:

36"Whereas, The unfortunate difficulties in Massac County, continue unabated; the party called 'Regulators,' not only killing, whipping and torturing, in every way possible, men, but are engaged in tearing down houses, over the head of defenceless women and children, turning them adrift in the inclemency of the weather, unprotected; insulting and abusing them; trampling under their feet all law and order, and the dearest and best rights of American citizens; therefore, be it

36"Resolved, that we have lost all confidence in the Legislature passing any law to restore order, and punish the guilty, and secure the innocent in the enjoyment of their rights as American citizens.

36Resolved, that if the Legislature is disposed to spend weeks in making 'buncombe' speeches, while the cries of innocent women and children fill the air with their lamentations of distress,

36Resolved, that in the absence of any action in the Legislative department, we call upon the Executive of Illinois to take the 'responsibility,' and with any necessary force put down the insurrection, punish the guilty, and protect the interest of the innocent; and that he be earnestly requested to act immediately and promptly.

36"Resolved, that as lovers of justice, humanity, and the maintenance of the supremacy of the laws, we cannot look on with indifference, while such scenes of violence are being perpetrated, and unless the proper authorities act, and act promptly and efficiently, we believe it our duty to take the responsibility into our hands, and save innocent women and children harmless in the enjoyment of their homes, let consequences be what they may."

37After nineteen years-at the close of 1850-peace and quiet would finally settle over southern Illinois. "Perhaps no section of the country," a historian would later write, "has undergone a greater reign of terror."

37Now Ford brought himself back to the Hancock troubles. If he could quell the Mormon problem-one way or the other-he could then direct his attention to the bigger trouble to the south. Illinois, he reflected, was still trying to find itself.

37From across the room, there was a knock at the door, and Ford sighed as he nodded to his aide to open it.

37At the turn of the century, the Illinois historian Dr. J. F. Snyder would write of the chief executive: "Governor Ford was one of the ablest jurists in the State, a man of singularly clear, philosophical mind, largely endowed by nature with vigorous, comprehensive intellect which was reinforced by a fair education and much study. In stature he was small with thin, homely features, deep-set eyes, and a long, sharp nose turned slightly at the point to one side. Well supplied with vanity and self-esteem, his prejudices were invincible, and his arrogance at times, intolerable and ludicrous. As insignificant in body and soul as he was admirable in mental power, lacking in physical and moral courage, vindictive, obstinate and spiteful, he hated those whom he could not control, and, when opportunity offered, caused them to feel the sting of his resentment. His spirit of vengeance outlived the lapse of time. He might forget a benefaction, but never forgave an injury."

39The Warsaw Signal was Thomas Sharp's paper; the Mormons of Hancock County, his whipping boy.

39His was not the only anti-Mormon newspaper of the time-in years to come history would record five: The Alton Telegraph, Democratic Review, Alton (1858-47), John Bailhache, publisher; Lee County Democrat, Fort Madison, Iowa (1842-46), R. Wilson Albright, editor; Quincy Whig, Quincy (1838-47), S. Francis, editor; Warsaw Message, Warsaw (1843-44), Thomas Gregg, publisher; Warsaw Signal, Warsaw (1840-46), Thomas Sharp, editor.

39Tom Sharp was an "anti"-as those against the Mormons were called-and, perhaps because he was so open about it, the "anti" best known by the masses at the time. Earlier he had been an anti-Masonic editor in western New York. It was he who coined the name "Jack-Mason" for those who refused to take part in the anti-Masonic movement of his area. In time, it was he who labeled as "Jack-Mormon" such a person that did not sanction the illegal acts and mob violence of him and his associates in driving the Mormons from Hancock County.

40The Illinois State Register, Springfield, recorded that Sharp was the chief "organ of a gang of town lot speculators at Warsaw who are afraid that Nauvoo is about to kill off their town and render speculations abortive."

40Thomas Sharp was more than this. He was a man of the bar and later even a school principal. He and the other non-Mormons with whom he worked closely to bring about the Smiths' assassination, the following "wolf hunts," and the Mormon war-Calvin Cole, Artois Hamilton, Thomas Gregg, Frank Worrell, Ebenezer Rand, Mark Aldrich, and Joseph Jackson-were all prominent men along this upper Mississippi area. With the possible exception of Joseph Jackson, all were well thought of by their fellow citizens.

40It had been a long-time effort getting the Mormon leader out of Nauvoo. The bulwark of American freedom, the writ of habeas corpus, had been a constant thorn in their side.

40Now, however, he was in the hands of the "antis."

40It had taken heavy pressure on their part, but the Governor had finally done the job which they could only begin but never complete. Now, with Captain Robert Smith as the judge, they had no fear but what the Mormon Prophet would remain in their control until they could end his career. (Joseph Jackson on the twenty-fifth had said they had eighteen accusations against Joseph, and that as one failed, they would bring up another to detain him. "The balls are in there," he said, pointing to his pistols, "that will decide the case.") The only thing that could stop their plans now, Sharp felt, was the Nauvoo Legion.

40He had only minutes before returned from Carthage. It was at a meeting there that the assassination plans had been confirmed. The guard would be manned by "antis," their flintlocks loaded blank. Twenty men would be dispatched from Warsaw who, together with another group from Carthage and Missouri, would rush the jail and do the job.

40Earlier in the day he had sat as counsel for the state during the Mormons' short hearing when the Greys had brought them out of the jail. Later he had attended the assassination meeting. Following the meeting he had hurriedly covered the distance back to Warsaw.

41Now he was tired and thirsty.

41There would be a good crowd of men yet at Calvin Cole's tavern, he determined, and on the eve of the "Nauvoo picnic" one had more to do than talk to himself. He left the printing office steps.

41Even today in rural America, as it was then in the towns of the western frontier, forgetting is a long-time thing. Sharp did not forget, nor did his fellow "antis" forget, and so it was that once at the tavern, while they were talking about Nauvoo and the march, the matter of the Wasp evolved.

41Someone said, "It's too bad the Wasp isn't printing," and Sharp moved his obese body, making the chair rub along the chair rail, and nodded his head. If the Warsaw editor enjoyed his biting editorials against the Mormons, he had found ample return for such a venture in the Nauvoo answer, the Wasp. So called because it was supposedly a "stinger of a paper," it had published for a few months two years previously in 1842. Edited by the Prophet's brother, William, it attacked the Signal editor with such names as "long-nosed Sharp," "Tomy," "Thom-ass C. Sharp," and "Polecat."

41In its fourth edition it editorialized: "We promised an apology to our readers for having condescended to notice in our columns so unprincipled a man as Tom Sharp, the editor of the Warsaw Signal."

41However, the Wasp became too peppery even for those who applauded it, and before many months had passed, Church leaders in Nauvoo asked that it cease publication. It was replaced by the Nauvoo Neighbor-John Taylor, editor-much to the pleasure of those in Warsaw and surrounding areas.

41Now, however, for a fleeting moment, as he sat drinking in Cole's tavern, Thomas Sharp wished that the Wasp was still alive. He pictured himself, once the confusion began in Nauvoo, being the one to throw the first pi of type into the street.

42Warsaw was the oldest of the three principal cities of Hancock County. Located on the bluffs in the bend of the Mississippi River opposite the mouth of the Des Moines River, the city had a population of about 450 people. Among those who laid it out were Sharp's good friend Mark Aldrich-who, like Sharp, commanded one of the companies of the Warsaw militia-and the former Illinois governor, Joseph Duncan. The city was "handsomely situated" above the river, though portions of it were rough and cut through by deep ravines.

42It was a source of pride to the people of Warsaw that there were no foreigners in their city.

42When the Warsaw citizens met in mass convention against the Mormons-and later in Carthage, where the Warsaw resolutions were adopted-it was to the credit of Sharp and his followers. They clamored for the meetings. They clamored for the resolutions that resulted.

42Printed Sharp in his Signal:

42"Whereas information has reached us, about which there can be no question, that the authorities of Nauvoo did recently pass an ordinance declaring a printing press and newspaper published by the opponents of the Prophet a nuisance, and in pursuance thereof did direct the Marshall of the city and his adherents to enter by force the building from whence the paper was issued, and violently (if necessary) to take possession of the press and printing materials, and thereafter to burn and destroy the same; and whereas, in pursuance of said ordinance, the Marshall and his adherents, together with a mob of Mormons, did, after sunset on the evening of the 10th instant, violently enter said building in a tumultuous manner, burn and destroy the press and other materials found on the premises.

42"And whereas Hyrum Smith did, in the presence of the City Council and the citizens of Nauvoo, offer a reward for the destruction of the printing press and materials of the Warsaw Signal, a newspaper also opposed to his interests;

43"And whereas the liberty of the press is one of the cardinal principles of our government, firmly guaranteed by several constitutions of the states, as well as the United States;

43"And whereas, Hyrum Smith has within the last week publicly threatened the life of one of our valued citizens, Thomas C. Sharp, the editor of the Signal;

43"Therefore, be it solemnly

43"Resolved by the citizens of Warsaw in public meeting assembled, that we view the recent ordinance of the city of Nauvoo, and the proceedings thereunder as an outrage of an alarming character, revolutionary and tyrannical in tendency, and being under color of law as calculated to subvert and destroy in the minds of the community all reliance on the law.

43"Resolved, that as a community we feel anxious, when possible, to redress our grievances by legal remedies; but the time has now arrived when the law has ceased to be a protection to our lives and property. A mob at Nauvoo, under city ordinance, has violated the highest privilege in government; and to seek redress in the ordinary mode would be utterly ineffectual.

43"Resolved, that the public threat made in the Council of the city, not only to destroy our printing press, but to take the life of its editor, is sufficient, in connection with the recent outrage, to command the efforts and the services of every good citizen to put an immediate stop to the career of the mad prophet and his demoniac coadjutors. We must not only defend ourselves from danger, but we must resolutely carry the war into the enemy's camp. We do therefore declare that we will sustain our press and the editor at all hazards; that we will take full vengeance, terrible vengeance, should the lives of any of our citizens be lost in the effort; that we hold ourselves at all times in readiness to co-operate with our fellow-citizens in this state, Missouri and Iowa, to exterminate, utterly exterminate the wicked and abominable Mormon leaders, the authors of our troubles.

44"Resolved, that a committee of five be appointed forthwith to notify all persons in our township suspected of being the tools of the prophet to leave immediately on pain of instant vengeance. And we do recommend the inhabitants of the adjacent townships to do the same, hereby pledging ourselves to render all the assistance they may require.

44"Resolved, that the time, in our opinion, has arrived, when the adherents of Smith, as a body, should be driven from the surrounding settlements into Nauvoo. That the prophet and his miscreant adherents should then be demanded at their hands; and, if not surrendered, a war of extermination should be waged to the entire destruction, if necessary for our protection, of his adherents. And we hereby recommend this resolution to the consideration of the several townships, to the Mass Convention to be held in Carthage, hereby pledging ourselves to aid to the utmost the complete consummation of the object in view, that we may thereby be utterly relieved of the alarm, anxiety and trouble to which we are now subjected.

44"Resolved that every citizen arm himself to be prepared to sustain the resolutions herein contained."

44Of the charges, only one-the destroying of a press and printing materials-was true. That there had been no riot, no mob action, no reward by Hyrum for the destruction of the Signal or for the life of its editor-all this was immaterial now. The "antis" had their toe-hold, the first concrete Mormon action they could point to, and it was all they needed.

11:00 p.m.

It was late to be retiring. Outside the stars stood out against a black velvet sky, and across the roof tops was a soft glow of light that was the courthouse square. Around the jail, in its position on the outskirts of the city, all was quiet. Below the upper windows the occasional flickers of light, the sound of sporadic movement, and the hushed rise and fall of voices gave evidence of the posted guard.

45There were now seven men in the upstairs bedroom.

45Joseph and Hyrum occupied the room's only bedstead. It was against the wall away from the door and caught the draft of cool air between the window on the east and the two south windows above the footboard.

45Four others-Dan Jones, Stephen Markham, John S. Fullmer, and John Taylor lay side by side on the floor on a mattress provided by the jailer.

45When the others retired, Dr. Richards was still writing at his table near the fireplace. As Joseph's secretary, he painstakingly recorded all that transpired in the life of the Prophet in addition to handling the Church's official correspondence and the Prophet's private correspondence. Many of the things he had done today pertained to the coming trial-subpoenas, affidavits, instructions, letters of request. All were done in duplicate, and, as was the mode of the day, were taken down in longhand.

45Finally, his last candle a puddle of wax, he put away his papers and lay down with the others.

45The sound of a gun fired near the jail caused the men to awaken, and Joseph left the bed and walked about the room. Later he lay on the mattress between Dan Jones and John Fullmer. Extending his right arm, the Prophet said to Fullmer, "Lay your head on my arm for a pillow, Brother John," and later, when all seemed asleep again, the two conversed in low tones.

45At one time the Prophet said, "I should like to see my family again." And still later, "I would to God that I could preach to the Saints in Nauvoo once more." Fullmer returned that he thought the Prophet would often have that privilege to preach. Joseph thanked him for his thoughts.

45As the hours passed, Dan Jones awoke, and finding the Prophet still awake, began to exchange whispers with him. At one point the Prophet asked, "Are you afraid to die?"

46"Has that time come, think you?" questioned Jones.

46Thirty-three and a native of Wales, Jones had come to America in 1840 and joined the Church in 1843. He had graduated from college in Wales-not a common thing-and was at the time probably the only member of the Mormon Church who could speak, write, and read the Welsh language.

46A month after joining the Church, Jones had been called on a mission to Wales. Six weeks ago he had sold to the Church his remaining half-share in the Maid of Iowa-which gave the Church full ownership-and was now preparing to embark on his calling.

46Reflecting for a moment, Jones continued, "Engaged in such a cause I do not think death would have many terrors."

46The Prophet clutched his companion by the arm. "You will yet see Wales and fulfill the mission appointed you before you die."

12:00 Midnight

Across the city of Carthage the lamps, after burning later than usual, were nearly dim. In the Hamilton House, Governor Ford had finally bade his last visitor goodbye and had lost himself in sleep. Some in Carthage slept soundly, the stupor of liquor heavy upon them; others slept nervously, uneasy with the militias in their city, still more uneasy with a fear that another militia, the Nauvoo Legion, would begin marching to free the Prophet by force. In many homes in which the lights burned late, preparations were being made to abandon the city; in others the residents were getting ready to fight.

46Nauvoo, too, was finally resting. At midnight Captain Singleton had issued his order for a review preparation and retired. There had been no dancing on the deck of the Maid of Iowa this night. No quilting bees, or gab fests, or horseshoe pitching. The people of Nauvoo gathered into their homes, the shadow of Missouri falling across the hearthstone, the news in their ears that, now they had the Prophet, a Gentile mob would soon descend on their city and reduce it to ashes.

47In Warsaw, Thomas Sharp had delivered his last oration in the streets and returned to his home. The swollen city's inhabitants snuffed the candle and turned low the lamp, and except for a few with whiskey yet to be drunk, the city slept.

47By now the news of the imprisonment had reached La Harpe, Fountain Green, Big Mound, and Green Plains, and by the time the sun was once again in the sky, it would be known in Plymouth, St. Mary's, Augusta, and Bear Creek.

47Like the drying of a wet string of rawhide stretched between poles, the tension that gripped Hancock County in the triangle of Carthage, Warsaw, and Nauvoo was becoming more taut with each passing hour.

47If unabated, it would soon reach the breaking point.
