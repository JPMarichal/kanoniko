Temple Lot Discoveries and the RLDS Temple

Ronald E. Romig

Three notable religious denominations associated with the Restoration movement share an interest in the history of the area at Independence, Missouri, identified as the Temple Lot. Each of these groups has developed and held its own unique perspective, and deep convictions about what happened there and what they believe will yet transpire in connection with this sacred location. Over the years, the depth of these convictions has only been intensified by a perception of the Restoration movement, as a whole, having suffered a great wrong at the hands of the larger society.

As a result of these diverse religious expectations, a great deal of myth and misunderstanding has developed over the significance of the Temple Lot, within and among the denominations. However, the purpose of this paper is not to provide a comparison of various beliefs and expectations, but to supply solid historical details, many of which have not been readily available, to those who may share an interest in this early period of the movement's history. In addition, I wish to note that the discoveries discussed here are not of a sensational nature, such as digging up buried cornerstones or artifacts, but rather they are the bringing together of rich documentary treasures that shed important light on the development of the early Church's plans for building cities and temples of God.

Joseph Smith, Jr., proclaimed that the Church would be empowered in the development of a scriptural, social, and economic Christian society, by the establishment and practice of a heavenly pattern observed by members in their earthly affairs. Thus, the Church might claim the privilege of inaugurating what was anticipated to be the literal kingdom of God on earth, reestablishing practices associated with the "Ancient Church." [^1]

Joseph's conception of this kingdom, or Zion, meshed two theological concepts-holy cities and temples of the Lord-into a potent symbiotic relationship. This core concept seems to have developed in Joseph's mind as he worked on the Book of Mormon. An anxiety in the emerging Church to realize the promises contained in this book led to the identification of Independence, Jackson County, Missouri, as the center of this Zion. Preliminary details relating to the construction of a temple emerged during Joseph's 1831 visit, and a spot was dedicated for the northeast corner of a temple.

Edward Partridge, the Church's first bishop, was responsible for making arrangements for the colonization of Zion. Meanwhile, Joseph Smith returned to Kirtland, residing in Ohio for an extended period. During this time, he received revelations which enabled him to define his growing conception of temple and city design more precisely.

The young Church's rapid growth fostered the concurrent establishment of two church centers, and Joseph gained insights about specific practical problems as he guided the building of both Zion and Kirtland. In 1832, Joseph revealed additional elements of a prototype New Jerusalem city pattern, reinforcing the concept that the city would be built "beginning at the Temple." [^2] An exciting period soon followed in June 1833, during which specific details of temple construction were proposed. Though plans for building a temple were slated first for Independence, temple construction actually began at Kirtland. On 3 June 1833, at a conference of high priests, the size of the inner court of a "house" for Kirtland was discussed. As new design understandings emerged for Kirtland, Joseph indicated that the Lord required a parallel response of the church in Zion as well. [^3] With this in mind, elements of a comprehensive plan for the city of Zion and its first temple were prepared and sent to Partridge on 25 June 1833. [^4]

Joseph Smith, Sidney Rigdon, and Frederick G. Williams, in the capacity of the First Presidency, sent detailed sketches of the first of 24 houses or temples proposed for Independence, along with a draft of the city of Zion [^5] (Fig. 1; see Fig. 2, Richard Cowan, "The Great Temple of the New Jerusalem," 141). These sketches delimit the size, form, and dimensions of both the proposed city and a temple. The description of this first proposed temple, except for some notable variations, architecturally suggests design elements later realized in the Kirtland Temple.

Unfortunately, the 25 June 1833 plat for the city provides no contextual clue to its true geographic place of beginning. The only mention of a point with which to locate the plat upon the actual terrain of the Temple Lot area is "the circle with a cross on it." [^6] Apparently the actual geographical place of beginning was well understood at the time, but no extant document clearly establishes the actual geographic location to which the "circle with the cross" refers. Therefore, while searching for an answer to this puzzle, some assumptions based on the larger patterns revealed in related historical sources are necessary.

It is known that "the center place for the city of Zion, and the northeast corner" of a temple had been designated in 1831. Consistent with the "pattern" that the temple was to be the place of beginning for the city, it is a relatively good assumption that the northeast corner of the proposed temple, "appointed by the finger of the Lord," was the point of beginning for the city of Zion. Accepting this and the traditional location of the northeast corner of the temple as somewhere near the marker placed on the Hedrickite lot, it becomes possible to superimpose the 25 June plat description and measurements upon present landmarks (see Fig. 2).

Though these early plans were part of this "pattern" of development for both Church centers, a remarkable series of developmental changes resulted from the interplay of the two cities, as well as from external influences. [^7] Following the mob action which resulted in the destruction of the Church's printing establishment on 20 July 1833 and a pledge by the primary Church leaders to leave the county, Oliver Cowdery hastily returned to Kirtland, arriving 9 August 1833. [^8] The Presidency, unaware of the seriousness of the situation in Jackson County, had already developed further plans for Zion. Letters of advice and another "draft for the house of the Lord and a plan for the city" had been sent to Zion just before Oliver's arrival.

Those documents read, in part:

The revelation respecting the two houses to be built in Kirtland in addition to the one we are now building for the presidency and the other for the printing are also binding upon you that is you at Zion have to build two houses as well as the one of which we have sent the patternÂ�the last mentioned houses are to be built as soon after the other as means can be obtained so to do. The pattern of the last mentioned houses as yet to be given. [^9]

As a result of the firsthand knowledge Oliver communicated about the situation in Zion, further changes were made in its physical arrangement. [^10] Oliver wrote that another revision was on its way to Missouri. [^11] Soon after this, Joseph indicated, via letter to Vienna Jaques, that Elders Orson Hyde and John Gould were on their way as messengers to Zion with further information about the city and temple. [^12] These men traveled with the plans and letters in valises on their backs and arrived in Independence in the latter part of September 1833. [^13]

Many adjustments to the pattern of the city of Zion would have probably occurred at this time, even had Oliver not provided specific information about the situation in Zion. It was a time of general change and adjustment throughout the Church, and plans for the city of Zion and Kirtland were continually revised. An augmented Kirtland plan was disclosed as via a revelation dated 2 August 1833:

A commandment I give unto you, that ye shall commence a work of laying out and preparing a beginning and foundation of the city of the stake of Zion, here in the land of Kirtland, beginning at my house; and behold it must be done according to the pattern which I have given unto you. And let the first lot on the south be consecrated unto me for the building of an house for the Presidency, for the work of the PresidencyÂ�. and again,Â�The second lot on the south shall be dedicated unto me, for the building of an house unto me, for the work of the printing of the translation of my Scriptures. [^14]

The important element of this revelation is its substantiation of a city pattern for Kirtland that reflects the prototype city New Jerusalem. [^15] Following the revelation, a plat was prepared to reflect these congealing plans for the Kirtland city. Such a plat may be found in the LDS Archives. Until recently it was identified as "Plat of Far West" [^16] ((see Fig. 3).

This plat provides a glimpse of the early development of Kirtland. Names and initials are penciled in on many of the lots. Many of these names match locations associated with the early members living at Kirtland, most notably Oliver Cowdery's location on lot number one, west of the temple, in the center block. The location of labeled lots also matches the geographic pattern of usable terrain around the center of Kirtland (see Fig. 4).

The plat provides remarkable visual clues which aid in deciphering lot locations and inheritances mentioned in RLDS Doctrine and Covenants Section 91 (LDS 94). This section calls for building two houses in addition to the temple under construction. Three buildings (houses) are depicted in a row on the east end of the center block. The house at the northeast corner represents the Kirtland Temple. The other two, drawn in a similar pattern, correspond to the house for the Presidency to be built on the first lot south, and a house for the work of printing on the second lot on the south. Reynolds Cahoon's and Jared Carter's inheritances are indicated on the first and second available lots in the city block to the north (lots #112 and #111). [^17] Hyrum Smith's inheritance is located on the first lot (#15) in the city block south of the proposed houses.

This Kirtland plat is remarkably similar in style and technique to the revised plat of Zion sent to Independence, Missouri, sometime in August (see Fig. 5). Both plats reflect identical patterns of city development, except in the center blocks surrounding proposed houses of the Lord. Much about these plats suggests that they were prepared at about the same time. Block and lot dimensions, as well as the consecutive numbering of lots, provide examples of this similarity. [^18]

The August 1833 Plat of Zion represents a significant revision of earlier plans. This modification affected both the proposed temple and the city. The revised description of the temple for the Presidency is similar, though somewhat improved, in relation to the earlier 25 June temple description. These second drafts of the temple, signed by F. G. Williams, are more detailed. [^19] In response to Joseph's desire that the building in Zion be somewhat larger than the house already started in Kirtland, the overall dimensions of the proposed building were enlarged. [^20] On the city plat, the area allotted for the construction of temples was significantly decreased, and the orientation of temple complex pivoted from a north-south alignment to an east-west orientation. [^21]

A close examination of the center section of the original August 1833 plat, showing the location of the 24 houses or temples on two ten-acre blocks, reveals that it has been glued onto the drawing. This indicates that an additional revision took place between the time when it was drawn and when it was sent. As a consequence of these continual changes, Partridge and other Church officials no doubt found it difficult to actually stake out any portion of these city designs. Although the corner of the first temple was fixed, each new proposed arrangement of the city or the temples would significantly shift the location of blocks and streets.

Fig. 5. August 1833 Plat of Zion, drawn by Frederick G. Williams. Courtesy of LDS Church Archives.

Such ongoing alterations were confusing and never provided final answers.

After the destruction of The Evening and The Morning Star press, in Independence, Church leaders agreed to remove from Jackson County. Joseph, through Oliver Cowdery, acceded to the Church's removal and advised leaders in Zion to move by the end of the year. However, the communications lag at this time, caused by letters crossing en route, appears to have worked a curious illusion. Leaders in Zion continued to request and apparently receive more complete information about the physical arrangement of Zion. Because their efforts had been prompted by revelation there was still, no doubt, considerable desire in their minds to fulfill the command of God. Edward Partridge continued working under the expectation that work on the temple should somehow be completed. After reviewing the August documents, no doubt seeing the glued on center section, Partridge ventured to suggest an arrangement of his own for the 24 houses or temples (see Fig. 6 and 7). He copied "A view of the 24 houses as sent on the plat" on one side of a piece of paper and inscribed the following alternative arrangement on the other:

I have marked out the 2 squares below & also the 24 buildings. I have arranged them so as to leave the spaces between them more equal, and according to the natural judgment of man would be preferable to the arrangement on the plat you sent-Thinking perhaps that their arrangement was not by revelation, and also that you had not seen them platted out has induced me to plat them this way for you to view & reflect upon. If this plan on this[s[ketch] should be considered preferable to the other perhaps it would be wisdom to set the buildings [ne]arer the streets than what they are marked. [^22]

No evidence indicates that Partridge's suggestion was ever sent to Joseph or that Joseph ever gave it consideration. However, this document implies that even Partridge was unsure of the degree of inspiration reflected in the proposed arrangement of temples on the center blocks of the city. His document further implies that a written description was associated with the August revised plat of the city of Zion, no longer extant.

However, since no description exists, the real value of this document is in providing important measurements in regard to the proposed August plat. It confirms that the revised plat was drawn to the same scale as the earlier 25 June plat. With this information,

W. W. Phelps made an important statement in 1864 concerning the Jackson County Temple dedication: "We planted a Stone at the Southeast corner of the ten acres for the first Temple." This information is incongruent with other reminiscences of the dedication and is more reminiscent of the August 1833 revised plan for the city of Zion than of the 1831 Temple Lot dedication. Perhaps Phelps' memory became blurred because of the frequent changes to the plan. But this later remembrance does support the possibility that, despite ongoing revisions, at least one of the center ten acre blocks of the proposed August 1833 plat may have been surveyed and staked out. Though probably inaccurate, his statement does theoretically suggest that revisions may have led to an alternative method of locating the beginning point of the city. The "spot" may have become dissociated from the corner of the temple, and changed to the "Southeast" corner of the ten acre block for the temples of the Presidency. [^24] Unfortunately, in the end, not even one of the 24 temples was built in the city of Zion.

In response to the escalation of assaults attending the expulsion, members of the Independence branch gathered together upon the Temple Lot for corporate safety. Over the years a tradition has developed about a small pile of stones at the site. Perhaps they were put there by Church members in a last effort to mark the spot before they were driven from the county in 1833.

One early church member to return to Independence was Roderick May, who was affiliated with the Reorganized Church. He stated to Inez Smith Davis

that he came to Independence in 1884, and that Doctor W. E. McClellan [sic] showed him where the temple was to standÂ�. He said that McClellan [sic] told him that although he was not here at the dedication that he was here soon afterwards and that a few loose stones were gathered together on the spot at the time of the dedication and oil poured on them. [^25]

However, this alleged pile of stones was not all that was left behind. An account from the James Owens family, who were member's of the Independence Branch, states that their oldest child, 15-year-old Rebecca Cordilia, died during the expulsion from exposure, improper food, and exhaustion. Owens' short account: "Brother John, died [in 1833] and was buried on the Temple Lot. Sister Cordilia died and was buried by the side of Brother John." [^27]

For the early Church, the November 1833 expulsion was both an end of the physical process of building Zion in Jackson County, Missouri, and a beginning of Joseph's grand preoccupation with its redemption. Even though succeeding events kept the Church far from Zion, Church members hoping for a return continued to plan for a literal Zion in Jackson County. John Whitmer indicated that a council was held at Kirtland, 17 May 1835, "in which were several selections, made for particular individuals according to the dictation of the spirit of the Lord through Joseph the Revelator, for inheritances in Zion." [^28] W. W. Phelps' inheritance appears as number 16 on Whitmer's list. Right after this meeting Phelps included a rough sketch of the city of Zion on the top right corner of a letter to his wife, Sally, with the inscription "ours" on inheritance 16 [^29] ((see Fig. 9). Phelps' sketch provides a visual clue to Whitmer's list of inheritances. And it establishes a reference from which it is possible to locate some 20 or more other contemplated inheritances.

As if to end the early Independence era, some who had been among the first church members to view the Temple Lot were also the last to visit it. [^30] After the expulsion from the state, Joseph's efforts to obtain redress and a return to Jackson County proved unavailing.

As may be seen from the foregoing, the plan for the city of Zion and her temples was never really fixed, as many believe, but experienced constant adjustment in response to various circumstances. Even though early Church members were called upon to continually adjust their conceptions of Zion, only some of the significant elements which they experienced relating to the development of Zion have been remembered and emphasized.

After many years of longing, members of the movement eventually reestablished a significant presence in Independence. First came the Hedrickites, who reclaimed a portion of the Temple Lot by purchase. Soon after, members of the Reorganized Church came, followed by the Latter-day Saints.

Over the years, the Temple Lot has taken on a variety of appearances. Even though it is an important location to members of the movement, there seems to have been proportionately little

recorded concerning events disturbing its pastoral aspect through the years. For whatever reason, the temple area, until recently, appears to have largely stood as if frozen in time.

Though there has been some building construction upon the original 63 acres through the years, the pace has greatly increased of late. The decades approaching the twenty-first century appear to be heralding a renewed attention to efforts oriented at achieving a portion of the original intent and inspiration delineated at the dedication of a temple in Zion on 3 August 1831.

Whatever God's future purposes for this spot of ground, despite the differing visions of respective movements, we may be assured that they will be realized. Christ, the Great Reconciler, is most fully able to affect His larger perspective. In this writer's opinion, we have allowed religious myths, some of which are misconceptions relating to the Temple Lot, to divide us too long. As RLDS leaders complete a temple dedicated to the pursuit of peace, I would anticipate a healing, to a degree, of the separation the Reorganized Church has experienced from its close cousins of the Restoration. It is time that we understand that relationships between Restoration churches may be dynamic and changing according to circumstances, just as Joseph Smith, Jr.'s, plan for the city of Zion and its temples underwent numerous adjustments. As the RLDS temple program emerges, and RLDS and local Latter-day Saint leaders look with interest upon their respective efforts in behalf of the gospel, we may have hope for improved relations (see Fig. 10).

The RLDS Temple will be open to individuals of all faiths. Hopefully many will feel welcome and comfortable in this sacred space. It is anticipated that Latter-day Saints will manifest a certain curiosity and interest in the RLDS expression of the Jackson County temple. In his writings, Richard Cowan has called attention to Orson Pratt's observation, that the New Jerusalem temple will encompass "many thingsÂ�which we now have not." [^31] RLDS members have been told that they may not anticipate this temple providing a location for the performance of ordinances and sacraments typically performed in their home congregations. Perhaps in the same vein, Latter-day Saints may come to also appreciate this temple, while yet enjoying the significant experience of their own rich temple heritage.
