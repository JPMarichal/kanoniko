Various authors, Regional Studies in LDS History: Missouri ( 1994)

New Light on Old Difficulties: The Historical Importance of the Missouri Affidavits

Kenneth W. Godfrey

While incarcerated in Liberty Jail, Joseph Smith "suggested" that the Saints gather up "a knowledge of all the facts, and sufferings and abuses put upon them by" the people of Missouri (D&C 123:1). The Mormon leader furthermore urged that property damage and definitions of character be listed as well as personal injuries. The names of the perpetrators were to be listed and a committee appointed to receive and compile the testaments. This committee was also charged with amassing all libelous publications, including those found in encyclopedias, magazines, and history books. [^1] Such documents, Joseph declared, would then be presented to the heads of governments "in [all] their dark and hellish hue" (v 6).

In the Prophet's mind, these acts represented a last effort "before" the Saints could "completely claim that promise which shall call him [Heavenly Father] forth from His hiding place" (D&C 123:6). This last statement replicates the Prophet's expectation that God would intervene and restore them once more on their Missouri land after the Saints had done all that they could do. With the compilation of the materials described above, the Zion of the last days and the establishment of the New Jerusalem would become more than a theological dream.

The collected petitions would then leave the "whole nation without excuse," which assuredly, Joseph Smith said, will cause the unveiling of the power of Deity's "mighty arm" (D&C 123:6). The accumulation of these materials became an imperative duty owed "to God,Â�wives and children," "widows," and orphans, as well as to those "murdered under its [Missouri's] iron hand." The atrocities committed against the Mormon people, the Prophet concluded, "are enough to make hell itself shudder," and the devil "palsy" (D&C 123:7, 9, 10).

From 1839-1845, almost a thousand Church members responded affirmatively to Joseph Smith's suggestion. Some wrote rather lengthy accounts of their experiences in Missouri, while others penned only a line or two, merely providing skeletal lists of their losses without editorial comment. In total, the Mormon Missouri affidavits number almost a thousand valuable pages of primary historical material. While often overlooked by historians of the Latter-day Saint ante-bellum Missouri period, their value is multi-faceted and of considerable importance. [^2] Stephen LeSuer, for instance, argues that these petitions "are in some respects more valuable than the lengthier reminiscences of the Saints because the petitions were written only a year or two after the expulsion, and thus are more accurate and specific in their details." [^3]

For more than a decade, Brigham Young University Professor Clark V. Johnson, recognizing their importance, has been collecting and preparing these petitions for publication. Thus he alone deserves credit for fulfilling, more than 150 years later, the suggested action of Mormonism's first prophet. [^4] [See Mormon Redress Petitions: Documents of the 1833-1838 Missouri Conflict. Ed. Clark V. Johnson, Salt Lake City: Bookcraft, 1992.] I also have reviewed, studied, and pondered the historical importance of these documents, and will hazard a few observations on their historical merit.

While a plethora of books, dissertations, theses, and articles have appeared regarding the seven-year turbulent Mormon sojourn in the "show me" state, most of these studies have focused on the "battles," the "skirmishes," and the "atrocities." Few authors, save Max Parkin, have written about daily life among Missouri's Mormons. [^5] Though the petitions provide portentous insights into violent aspects of the Latter-day Saint experience, they also provide details on the size of Latter-day Saint landholdings, diet, dress, housing, and farming practices. The variety, too, of crops grown, the size of gardens and fields, and the animals used in conquering the fertile prairie soil are chronicled with detail.

Before proceeding with our analysis of these petitions of historical importance, a few cautions should be raised. The affidavits were, after all, written or dictated a year or more after the events took place while the writers still remembered with considerable anger the abuses incurred. The descriptions of losses in Jackson and Clay Counties were of events that had occurred as many as seven years earlier. Thus there may be inaccuracies. The claims should be compared with court records, deeds, and other legal documents before reliable conclusions can be reached. The petitioners, too, were attempting to gain monetary redress from an acknowledged reluctant federal government. For this reason, there may have been a predilection to exaggerate or inflate actual losses incurred. It is at least questionable that so many Mormons actually owned thoroughbred horses, registered cows, and expensive guns. Writers may have overstated their sufferings, too, in an attempt to arouse as much sympathy as possible. Perhaps they are too much like insurance claims for the historian to use them without confirming their accuracy.

Because the petitions were designed to elicit financial compensation, they do not provide a balanced history of the Mormon Missouri experience. There are few glimpses into theology, worship patterns, social activities, cultural interests, or attitudes toward death and suffering. Their chief value lies in the disclosures they make regarding crops, animals, acreage, household furnishings, and atrocities experienced or witnessed.

Land Holdings

Mormon land acquisitions in Jackson and Clay Counties tended to be much smaller than purchases made in northern Missouri's counties, especially Caldwell. A typical land purchase in Jackson County was 20 or 30 acres, the average being 34. The smallest listing was five acres; the largest privately owned farm was 80 acres. [^6] The average Caldwell County farm, in contrast, figured from a sample of more than ten percent of the total petitions, was 124 acres, while in Clay County it was 55 acres.

This reveals that in spite of the fact that many Latter-day Saints were driven from their homes as many as three times, they increased the amount of their land holdings. At least one factor that enabled Church members to enlarge the size of their homesteads was the government's policy in northern Missouri of making land available at relatively inexpensive rates: $1.25 per acre if improvements were made. However, a few Mormons paid as much as $415.00 per acre for land in Caldwell County.

In the less settled areas of northern Missouri's prairies, land was plentiful and settlers in the late 1830s comparatively few. Still, the size of a number of Mormon farms is somewhat surprising. Latter-day Saints, by my own count, owned and improved more than 10,000 acres in Caldwell County alone; this perhaps provides a significant clue as to why the non-Mormon populace pushed for their expulsion. LaMar Berrett argues that the Northern Missouri War of 1838 took place just before many Latter-day Saints were to receive clear title to their farms. [^7]

Anger, too, fueled the fire of covetousness. The feeling was that if they were not checkmated, the Mormons would soon have all the land in Missouri's northern counties, and then increase their settlements in other counties as well, thereby gaining aggregate control of the state. While alluded to in some histories, the significance of Latter-day Saint land acquisitions as a cause of conflict has perhaps not received the full attention it deserves. The Missouri Petitions provide valuable documentation into this matter that historians must consider and evaluate, if the definitive history of Missouri's Latter-day Saints is to be written.

Home Life

The Missouri affidavits detailed insights into home life among the Latter-day Saints. The most repeatedly mentioned cultivated crop was corn. An extremely large number of the petitions refer to this product and the devastating effects of its loss when destroyed by Missouri's mobs. Other crops frequently cited include wheat, hay [including Timothy], potatoes, sweet potatoes, turnips, "pease" [peas], buckwheat, oats, rye, pumpkins, peaches, and apples. Thus it is possible to reconstruct with some accuracy the nature of the Missouri Mormon's diet as well as that of his animals.

Lists of animals find as conspicuous a place in the affidavits as the crops Mormons raised. From these official documents we learn that the Saints raised hogs, one farmer having as many as 600. Most, however, possessed only one or two, probably for eating. Calves, cows, sheep, chickens, and goats are listed repeatedly in the affidavits. Moreover, most homesteads had at least one horse and some oxen (one man possessing 13 teams), and a few Mormons even raised bees.

The Missouri Latter-day Saint diet thus included ham, chicken, eggs, milk, honey, butter, boiled maize, and hot wheat bread, supplemented at times with elk, deer, and wild turkey. This fare probably would have differed little, if any, from that of the non-Mormon residents of the state. However, conspicuously absent from the Mormon petitions were alcoholic beverages and tobacco. Americans consumed large amounts of liquor during the 1830's, and while we know that Church members did some drinking, they were either able to safely stow away such commodities or were unwilling to list them among their losses.

Machinery and Tools

According to the petitions, most Mormon households possessed plows, hoes, chains, wagons, bridles, harnesses, buckskins, saws, axes, saddles, and grindstones. One Latter-day Saint, perhaps unique, declared the loss of his shoe and bootmaker, while another the detriment of his blacksmith tools. Still another listed the theft of his razor. Many individuals surrendered rifles, pistols, and swords. In fact, the number of guns owned by Mormons (some possessed as many as three weapons) is rather impressive and manifests that firearms were a necessity on the American Frontier.

Some Latter-day Saints referred to the theft of fence rails, beds, blankets, quilts, tin plates, shoe hammers, saws, chairs, and tents; only one lists the loss of books. The tally also reveals that although some Church members slept in tents, more dwelt in log cabins or one-room frame houses. Few Latter-day Saints had the means to construct larger homes.

When the history of daily life among Missouri's Mormons is written, facts found on the petitions will provide valuable information. These primary sources will also help flesh out the skeleton of Mormon life on America's frontier. In fact, if we may assume that Latter-day Saints were somewhat typical of other people in America during the 1830s, then these documents provide estimable insights into the Missourian's daily life as well.

Persecution & War

War remains perhaps the most popular study for historians. Each year hundreds of books, usually written from the viewpoint of a general or a national leader, issue from the world's publishing houses on the various conflicts. The writer may discuss the causes of the war, battle strategies, the most famous engagements, as well as the long-term results of the conflict. However, historians, too, explore the consequences of war on the populace. The Missouri petitions not only provide insights into how war and its terror affect the common people, but they give insight into the way unpopular minorities are persecuted in a land dedicated, in part at least, to protecting minority rights.

Three decades ago, Brigham Young University Professor Mark W. Cannon argued that the "Crusades against the Masons, Catholics, and Mormons could be described as separate waves of a common current." [^8] Other writers have detailed the persecutions of Quakers, Blacks, Indians, and other minority groups. From these studies we learn that the Mormons were hardly unique in suffering violence at the hands of hostile neighbors. The petitions are not only significant for the barbarous violence they vividly describe, but they are also of considerable value in providing the historian with raw data from which comparisons can be made with the sufferings of other unpopular minority groups. Fresh insights, too, are provided for certain heinous acts of violence, such as the Haun's Mill Massacre.

Order of Extermination and Expulsion from MissouriHCThe petitions, then, supply the historian with graphic examples of the failure of religious freedom in a land dedicated to its realization. Thus while the Mormon Missouri experience replicates that of Quakers, Catholics, and other new sects, the Latter-day Saint experience is somewhat different in that the crusade against Mormons persisted until it significantly altered the Church and its members. The harassment began in New York, followed the Saints to Ohio, then to Missouri and Illinois, and even 2,000 miles to the Great Basin.

Order of Extermination and Expulsion from MissouriHCThe petitions, too, are an important source for documenting violations of Latter-day Saints' constitutional rights. The Mormon William Aldredge, a veteran of the War of 1812, lamented that a "bold soldier" could be "exterminated from the state of Missouri." [^9] Joseph S. Allen, another Latter-day Saint, was taken from his home, his weapons confiscated; he was then held in custody for a week, not knowing the fate of his family. All of this was done without due process of law. [^10]

The constitutional right to respect private property and to receive a "speedy" trial was violated in the case of Alexander Badlam and Caleb Baldwin. Sick and driven from their home, the Badlams shivered together in a shack while Missourians drove away in their wagon pulled by the Badlams' team. [^11] Caleb Baldwin was later taken prisoner and for six months languished in prison with no charges ever being filed against him. His family were driven out of "the state in the Cold inclement Season to wander on the broad prairies." [^12]

In October 1838, Colonel Ned Gilliam and his mounted troops, faces painted black, confiscated the farm of Calvin Beebe. They used the Beebe property as their headquarters while they plundered Mormon homesteads and imprisoned Beebe in his own house. The troops robbed his beehive, killed and ate his chickens, and used up three acres of corn and a stack of hay. They also slaughtered and consumed his beef cattle. It was from the Beebe farm that Gilliam launched his attack on Far West. [^13] Beebe had also previously lost land and improvements in both Jackson and Clay Counties.

The tavern of John M. Burk became the operations base for General John Clark and his army. Without Burk's permission, the troops camped on his ground, used up his wood and hay, and prevented the family from leaving. They also threatened travelers who stopped at the tavern. [^14]

Commanded by General Parks to call the Mormons of Ray County together, Reynolds Cahoon did so. When assembled, they learned that they were prisoners of war. One hundred and fifty-three of their rifles were taken, plus several pistols and swords. The Latter-day Saints were given ten days to either leave the county or be killed. A company under command of Gilliam later plundered Mormon farms and took 50 horses and a large number of logs, which they used for their fires. [^15]

Davis County resident William Cahoon was imprisoned and kept under guard for eight days. His oppressors forced him to sign a deed of trust, giving his farm to the army. His house was "broken open," and the troops confiscated all his food. Not being permitted to go outside and obtain wood, they shivered from the cold. However, Cahoon and his siblings remained in Missouri until spring 1839, when they finally made their way to Illinois. [^16]

Order of Extermination and Expulsion from MissouriHCA number of wrongs perpetrated upon the Latter-day Saints in the "Mormon War of 1838" are known only because of the affidavits. We learn, for example, that early in October of 1838 Lemira Calkins, unaware that trouble was brewing, left home to bless a grandchild living some 40 miles away. On the very day the child was christened, Calkins' wagon was stolen by mobbers and she was prevented from returning home. It was the middle of December before Calkins, by sleuth, reached her homestead and found the house plundered and the corn, potatoes, and garden produce stolen. Before she could secure shelter, her feet froze. [^17]

Feeling that "it [was] out of [his] power to Relate" the suffering he and his family endured, James Carrolle reported that they were forced from their home. They remained "in an open frame in the Cold weather when the Snow fell in torrents and would Blow up on us in the Night and we with our Little ones, would have to Crawl out of our Beds while they were Covered frequently with Snow that would blow" in the frame from the North. Mobbers, described as cruel and heartless, laughed at the Carrolls while threatening all the while to put them out of existence. [^18]

Doctor Levi Richards saw the army, under command of General Clark, destroy large amounts of timber, lumber, cattle, and hogs-even shooting some of these animals for sport. Richards was forced to attend the wounds of several prisoners who had been shot, and two died. [^19] Angry that he had to pay to secure his own pistol once more, Richards finally gave one of Governor Boggs' aids 50 cents for the weapon. (Many other Latter-day Saints reported similar experiences in purchasing back their own arms.)

A non-Mormon, Thomas D. Casper, heard mobbers threaten the lives of Latter-day Saints who refused to deny their faith, and listened, as did others, while a man named Willits declared he had slain three Mormons. Several others testified of acts "too tedious to mention." [^20]

Having been a member almost from the day the Church was organized, Solomon Chamberlain, in 1838, lived almost 40 miles northwest of Far West. He saw his house, furniture, timber lot, 400 bushels of corn, a barn full of feed, farming utensils, two "eligent Mares," and a mule taken by mobbers, leaving him destitute. [^21]

Timothy Clark, working in his fields on a peaceful October day, was suddenly seized by General Lucas, who proceeded to hold captive the entire family. Soldiers later tore down fences, destroyed crops, orchards, and a nursery, then confiscated Clark's oxen and horses. [^22]

Blind and lame, Joseph Drury was driven from the land along with his wife and three children (all suffering from fever and ague in "cold weather"). They walked 25 miles before reaching shelter and security. [^23] David Frampton tells that two Mormon families, both his neighbors, became so terrified of the mobbers that they denied their faith, thus securing safety. In contrast, Frampton, threatened by a soldier named Odle, calmly turned his back to the officer and walked slowly away, daring him to pull the trigger. [^24] The mobber luckily did not fire the weapon.

William Seely, while conducting business with a Mr. Pinkham at "Bunkham's Strip," was captured by "mobbers, who both stripped him and searched him for arms," finding only a jack-knife. In anger, they thrust him out-of-doors, then dragged him to a fence, where they applied the foot "to the rear of his body" and threatened to kill him. Kept in custody overnight, he was forced the next day to stand in front of a company of soldiers and was shot in the left shoulder. Left for dead, Seely's friends carried him to his family, and after four months of "tedious confinement," he finally recovered. [^25]

After watching 30 men beat his father with guns and ramrods, Perry Keys was given "twenty-three lashes with a cowhide" just because he was a Church member. [^26] Truman Brace's wife and children were forced to watch as he was beaten with an ox gourd and then a rawhide whip. Following Brace into his home, one Missourian, continuing his abuse, kicked him in the mouth. [^27]

The examples cited above are typical and provide evidence that helps historians flesh out Mormon difficulties in northern Missouri. By making use of the affidavits, scholars can move the narrative from the realm of strategy, battles, and chronicles of the actions of leading figures to the personal pathos, fear, and injustices perpetrated on decent people. The petitions "tell a unified story of murder,Â�beating, thievery, and general lawlessness." These acts did not arise spontaneously from "drunken town rabble," but were instead the result of a deliberate plan involving cruelty, disregard of constitutional rights, and expulsion. This plan was carried out by some of Missouri's best citizens, including ministers, and was sanctioned by both state and local governments. [^28]

These documents are also of historical significance for the information they provide on Missouri's Jackson County when the Latter-day Saints were expelled in late fall 1833.

Forced to abandon their homestead and taking with them only what they could carry on their backs, the John Loveless family never again saw Jackson County. [^29] William Whiting, wounded by the Jackson County mob, lived only a few days. The wound, compounded by hardship, exposure, pain, and distress, reportedly caused his death. [^30]

Even as the Jackson County Latter-day Saints were worshipping together, a mob headed by a man named Masters burst into the meeting and told the congregation that all who were willing to forsake their religion could live peaceably among the rest of the citizenry, but those who would not forsake Mormonism would be driven from the county. During the month of November, Masters carried out his threat. [^31]

Poor and delicate health caused further suffering. After being driven from her home and coping with inclement weather, Isaac Higbee's wife delivered a son in the woods. [^32] Many times, even while sick in bed, Levi Jackman was forced to leave his home and take shelter in the woods in order to save his life. [^33]

Not all the Mormons left Jackson in the fall of 1833, as is generally supposed. For instance, the Abigail Leonard clan was still there in March 1834. However, on the first day of that month, a group of 50 men armed with whips and guns surrounded the Leonard home. When the husband refused to abandon the house, he was struck on the head with a chair then dragged from his domicile. Outside, a mobber named Lattern "jumped" on Leonard "with his heels." When Leonard stood up, he was stripped of all his clothes, save his pantaloons, and then attacked with guns and whips. Finally the bullies left, and the beaten man was carried back into the house. Being bruised, he could not lie on the bed, nor could he work. [^34]

For refusing to abandon his chosen religion, David Pettigrew saw men, their faces painted red and black, burst into his house, threaten his family, and leave only after abusing everyone. Returning home after a few days in the wilderness, Pettigrew saw that his farm and house had been plundered, his grain, stock, and farming tools stolen, and the house burnt. [^35]

Porter Rockwell, in a long, detailed, dictated petition, declared that while he and his father ferried a group of men across the Big Blue River, they were told that on 20 July 1833, the Mormon printing press would be destroyed. Again on the twentieth, as they were ferried back across the river, the Rockwells listened to what the men had done to the press as well as to Partridge and Allen, two Mormon leaders.

Bishop Partridge attested that when the ruffians came to his home, he was sitting with his "quit feeble" wife. Taken outside, he was commanded to remove his clothes. Protesting, Partridge was permitted to keep on his shirt and pantaloons while being tarred and feathered. [^36]

A few days later the Rockwells were told that unless they renounced their "doctrine and religious faith as Mormons," they would share the same fate as Partridge and Allen. Rockwell, too, related how the wife of David Whitmer was dragged from her home by the hair of her head and watched helplessly as her domicile was demolished.

On 2 November 1833, a large assemblage of ruffians came to the Rockwell home, insulted Porter's mother and sisters, then destroyed the building and threatened to cut his sister's throat. Their faces painted like Indians, these men destroyed property and frightened children in the absence of Rockwell's father. [^37]

While historians of Mormonism have detailed a number of reasons for the Latter-day Saints' expulsion from Jackson County, the Saints themselves believed that if only they had renounced their religion, they could have lived in peace in their homes. [^38]

Haun's Mill MassacreHCBy carefully reading the petitions, the historian also gleans bits and pieces of fascinating information on the events that took place at Haun's Mill (commonly referred to as a massacre). To illustrate, Joseph and Jane Young asserted that the Mormons entered into a treaty with the "Mobbers" prior to the attack on the mill. On the day the massacre took place, the banks on both sides of Shoal Creek teemed with children sporting and playing, while their mothers did household chores. Twenty-eight armed Latter-day Saint men guarded the town despite the treaty, and were described as being in constant readiness for an onslaught. So the attack was hardly a surprise, as some historians have asserted. [^39] David Fullmer declares that the Saints gathered at the mill to save it from threatened arson. In a last-ditch effort to prevent the massacre, at least two of the men took off their hats and swung them around their heads, crying, "quarters, quarters," only to be shot down by a group of mobbers other than the ones with whom the treaty had been made. [^40]

Haun's Mill MassacreHCAmanda Smith asserted that the men went into the blacksmith shop because they feared that if they "all run together" the mob "would rush upon us, and Kill the women and Children." Thus, contrary to what some historians have written, congregating in the shop was a deliberate, calculated heroic act, designed to save lives, making those who were shot therein martyrs. [^41]

Haun's Mill MassacreHCIn the blacksmith shop, a wounded Jacob Foutz pretended he was dead when a mobber placed his arm under [him], hunting for pistols. He heard the man say that it was too bad to take a dead man's guns, "but I must have them." [^42] Following the massacre, while dogs howled and cattle bellowed, the surviving Saints held a prayer meeting until told by the mob to cease or they, too, would be killed. [^43]

Haun's Mill MassacreHCToo tired and too frightened to bury the dead, the Mormons mourned throughout the night and the next day; still lacking strength to dig graves, they finally were "obliged to throw" the bodies into a dry well. Painted-faced mobbers returned for days to the scene of the crime, riding around the houses, abusing, insulting, and terrifying the survivors. [^44] Crowding into the domicile of Ruth Napier, whose husband had not survived the slaughter, the ruffians cast Napier and her children away from the warmth of the fire and bedded down. One of the men came to Ruth's bed in the night and laid his hand upon her. Frightened, she "made quite a noise, and crept over the back side" of her children, and the mobber "offered no further insult." [^45] These examples show, I believe, the rich cache of Missouri ore that awaits mining in the petitions. There are, perhaps, more eyewitness accounts of the Haun's Mill Massacre in these documents than any other one source.

It was the Missourians hatred for Joseph Smith and the church he restored that roused their deepest anger and fears. The petitions overwhelmingly attest that this was the chief cause for Boggs' extermination order.

Moreover, the Missouri Petitions provide the intricate details as well as the broad perspectives historians need in order to compose accurate chronicles of this period of the Church's development. Having the ring of truth, the petitions merely state the facts. They contrast sharply with the more sensational remembrances found in books, diaries, and journals. For example, in 1905, Benjamin F. Johnson remembered being taken prisoner when just 20-years-old by a "ruffian horde" commanded by General Clark. While Johnson was sitting on a log one day, "a brute came to him with a rifle in hand saying, `You give up Mormonism right now, or I'll shoot you.' Receiving a decisive refusal he took deliberate aim not ten feet distance and pulled the trigger. No explosion occurred, and he cursed fearfully, saying he had used the gun 20 years and it had never before missed fire." Examining the lock, he reprimed the rifle and aimed at Johnson, and again the gun refused to fire. Loading it a third time, the would-be murderer pulled the trigger, the gun exploded, killing the mobber on the spot. Johnson believed God had intervened, rewarding him for his refusal to denounce the Prophet and the church he founded. [^46]

In the more than eight hundred petitions there is rarely, if ever, documentation of the miraculous. Rather, the historian reads listings of losses incurred, abuses suffered, threats levied, and murderous deeds done by some of Missouri's leading public figures. Thus scholars are inclined to believe these petitions as written.

The petitions provide insights into the literacy of the Mormon people. As Clark V. Johnson has written, about five percent of those who filed petitions signed by making their mark. [^47] Thus one of the few ways we have of ascertaining which Latter-day Saints could read or write is by examining the affidavits. They also provide a name base from which to study Latter-day Saint membership in the 1830s.

Moreover, the petitions graphically illustrate and provide a substantial proof that the Saints indeed made a valiant, almost heroic effort to solve their difficulties in a peaceful way. As their book of Doctrine and Covenants counseled, they truly did renounce war and proclaim peace (D&C 98:16). However, the fact that the Mormons, despite their efforts to reconcile, were deprived of their civil rights caused many of them to become Federalists.

The petitions are perhaps one of the earliest sources of documentation for what would later become the writing of defensive Latter-day Saint history, which has persisted until the present time. The accumulation of these petitions may also have influenced the way Joseph Smith perceived written history. Those he charged with preserving the story of the Latter-day Saints also penned apologetic, often self-serving, narratives.

The Missouri experience catalyzed in a number of Mormons the desire to detail what had been suffered and endured, and this led to the publishing of some of our earliest histories. While no Homer emerged to tell the saga of the Latter-day Saints, there did arise a Sidney Rigdon and a Parley Parker Pratt, to name only two of the many Saints who wrote histories of the Mormon Missouri experience! [^48]

The petitions can provide a foundation from which to proceed to write a complete history of the Mormon Missouri experience. When this history is composed, the wars, the expulsion, and the atrocities will be balanced against the calm days: the farming days, the housekeeping days, the days set aside for worship, the times spent in gaining an education; as well as those days spent making bonnets, picking berries, walking in the woods, discussing theology; and the time played out planting, weeding, harvesting crops, tending animals, attending marriages, visiting friends, and reading The Evening and Morning Star. Drusilla Hendricks, living on six Caldwell County acres, described her initial days in Missouri in the following words: "I never lived happier in my life." [^49] Only months later, Drusilla's husband was wounded and crippled for life in the Battle of Crooked River. While we know much about the engagement at the river, we have spent precious little time in attempting to learn what made Drusilla's early days in Missouri the best ones in her life.

For more than 150 years we have had a somewhat distorted picture of Mormon Missouri. Historians have tended to focus on a few months of violence, or the Danite Band, while ignoring the peaceful years on one of America's frontiers.

Warner Woodworth reminds us what the German theologian and World War II martyr Dietrich Bonhoeffer once wrote: "It is an experience of incomparable value to have learned to see the great events of the history of the world from beneath; from the viewpoint of the useless, the suspect, the powerless, the oppressed, the despised-in a word, from the viewpoint of those who suffer." [^50] Using the petitions as a base, let us begin today viewing the Latter-day Saint Missouri experience from the viewpoint of the despised, the powerless, and the oppressed. The experience will, I am convinced, be richly rewarding.
