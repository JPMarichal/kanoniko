De Witt-Prelude to Expulsion

Keith W. Perkins

One of the most controversial eras in the history of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints was the northern Missouri period in the 1830s. Almost since the Mormons were driven out of Missouri, both Latter-day Saints and non-Latter-day Saints have defended their position.

Shortly after they were expelled from Missouri the Latter-day Saints began to prepare what are called the Missouri Petitions. These petitions are claims against the federal government for the loss of lives and property they incurred during the conflict. [^1] A committee headed by Joseph Smith took a group of these petitions to Washington, DC, and petitioned Congress and the president of the United States to reimburse them for their losses in Missouri.

The suggestion to petition the leaders of the nation originated in a letter Joseph Smith wrote to the Saints in Quincy, Illinois, while he was imprisoned in the Liberty Jail. This letter suggested "gathering up a knowledge of all the facts, and sufferings and abuses put upon" the Latter-day Saints by the state of Missouri. It further suggested that a committee be appointed to present these statements to the world and to the "heads of government in all their dark and hellish hue, as the last effort which is enjoined on us by our Heavenly Father" (D&C 123:1, 6).

Joseph Smith and other Church leaders in Liberty Jail considered this "an imperative duty" that they not only owed to their own families, but also to the "widows and fatherless, whose husbands and fathers have been murdered under its iron hand; Which dark and blackening deeds are enough to make hell itself shudder, and to stand aghast and pale, and the hands of the very devil to tremble and palsy. And also it is an imperative duty that we owe to all the rising generation, and to all the pure in heart" (D&C 123:9-11).

At the 6 October 1839 conference of the Church in Commerce (Nauvoo), Illinois, a committee comprised of Joseph Smith, Sidney Rigdon, and Elias Higbee was sent to Washington, DC. [^2] Their formal petition to congress is recorded in History of the Church. [^3]

Not to be outdone by the efforts of the Saints, the General Assembly of the state of Missouri published their version of the expulsion story in 1841. In his message to the Missouri legislature, Governor Lilburn W. Boggs maintained that after the

"infatuated and deluded sect" left the state of Missouri, they industriously propagated throughout the Union, the most exaggerated details of our difficulties and the foulest calumnies against our citizens. In some of our eastern cities, missionaries to their creed were employed, daily making converts to their cause by proclaiming the cruelties which they alleged they had endured at the hands of our authorities. The report of our alleged barbarities has not been confined to our Union, but even at this day in Europe they are made the ground-work of proselyting, and their orators find it to their interest to distort the acts into a persecution, which in every religious excitement that has marked the history of the earth, has always been found the most effective weapon of conversion. [^4]

The House of Representatives and the Senate of Missouri voted on 16 February 1841 to publish two thousand copies of their version of the story. [^5]

Many books and articles since that time have been published on the Mormon-Missouri conflict, most recently the book by Stephen C. LeSueur, The 1838 Mormon War in Missouri. [^6] Many of these writings have tried to prove that the violence inflicted upon the Latter-day Saints came because of their aggression against the people of the state of Missouri. However, any action taken by the Latter-day Saints in De Witt, Carroll County, Missouri, was taken only after they attempted every peaceful means to settle the issue. In brief, the Mormons were invited by the citizens of De Witt to settle in their community. After the Saints began to gather there, however, members of the surrounding communities became upset and threatened to exterminate them or drive them out. Because of these threats, the Latter-day Saints petitioned Governor Lilburn W. Boggs to protect them in their rights. Missouri military officials also pleaded with the governor on several occasions to come to the scene of conflict and protect the Mormons, who were only defending themselves. All of these petitions were ignored. Many Latter-day Saints reluctantly fought the vigilantes that came against them. Finally, when all other attempts to settle the issue failed, and to avoid serious bloodshed, the Saints in De Witt agreed to peacefully leave the land they had purchased. Generally, any action taken by the Latter-day Saints in Daviess and Caldwell Counties following this episode must be considered defensive. [^7] Truly the experience in De Witt was a prelude to the expulsion of the Mormons from Missouri.

The history of Constitutional rights in the United States is darkened by the way the Latter-day Saints were treated in Missouri. This persecution reached its peak on 27 October 1838 in the infamous "extermination order" of the governor of Missouri, Lilburn W. Boggs: "The Mormons must be treated as enemies and must be exterminated or driven from the state." [^8] Within a few weeks the Latter-day Saint population was ejected from Missouri.

This mass expulsion in November 1838 had its beginning in De Witt, [^9] Carroll County, Missouri. The same actors played on the stage both at the Mormon hub of Far West and at De Witt. Prominent Saints in this conflict included Joseph Smith, Sidney Rigdon, and George M. Hinkle. Some Missourians involved were Samuel Lucas, Samuel Bogart, Alexander Doniphan, David Atchison, and of course, Lilburn W. Boggs.

On 12 January 1838 Joseph Smith and others fled Kirtland, Ohio, arriving in Far West, Missouri on 14 March. There they hoped to live in peace, something enemies of the Church had not afforded them from the time that Joseph Smith announced he had received new revelation from heaven. The Saints began to gather to Missouri as early as 1831. After Joseph Smith's arrival in western Missouri, he announced that the Lord had identified Independence, Jackson County, Missouri, as the site for the City of Zion (D&C 57:1-3). The few Latter-day Saints that had gathered to Jackson County were driven out in 1833. However, the great gathering of the Saints to Missouri did not occur until 1838.

The Saints settled to two primary locations-Far West and Adam-ondi-Ahman-but soon they began to search out other places of settlement for the thousands that were heeding the counsel to gather. New locations were needed to facilitate the movement of members and supplies to Missouri. An ideal place would have an excellent steamboat landing, where goods and people could be easily transported and then travel by land to Far West, the headquarters of the Church. This consideration drew the attention of Church leaders to the town of De Witt.

Originally called Elderport, [^10] De Witt had originally been purchased and platted by Reverend Eli Guthrie. On 23 January 1837, Henry Root of Brantford, Ontario, Canada, purchased the town of De Witt for $3000 from Eli Guthrie and his wife Eliza. [^11] Though today the Missouri River has moved east and cannot be seen from De Witt, when the Saints arrived the town was located on a high bluff overlooking the river. This made it an ideal location for a town of river commerce. For many years there was a ferry in operation, which proved a great blessing to the local farmers as well as the citizens of surrounding communities. [^12]

Henry Root and David Thomas of De Witt sent word to Church leaders that there was available for purchase excellent land and a fine steamboat landing near the confluence of the Missouri and Grand Rivers. Undoubtedly the first time the Latter-day Saints became aware the town of De Witt was open to immigration was when this letter was delivered to Joseph Smith:

Pleasant Park, Missouri, March 31st 1838

Respected Sir,

Permit me to introduce to your acquaintance Mr. Henry Root of Dewit near this place on the Misso(u)ri River His business I am unacquainted with, Though any thing he may say to you, you may put the most implicit confidence in, as I have allways found him to be a man of truth & honor, neither have I ever known him to give a misrepresentation of any part. He is a merchant and I suppose doing a moderate business his place is now, onley laid out a year since, a beautifull sight to the river, and a first rate landing And Sir permit me to Say to you, if you could make it convenient or for your advantage to settle in this County, I would let you have part of my land There is yet to enter adjoining my land, as good land [as] is in the world. I have no doubt you can do as well here in forming a settlement and probably better than any place in the state The facilities of the river will be of great servise in settling this upper country besides some of the knowing ones have arrived to uproot you, but here you can break them down in turn. [^13]

In April 1838, Sidney Rigdon and his family were on their way to Far West. They stopped for the night at a home in Carroll County, located on Turkey Creek. A young man rode up and when he heard Sidney's name mentioned, he identified himself as Henry Root. He asked if Rigdon would intervene in his behalf to get the Saints to settle on his property in De Witt. He promised Rigdon a liberal share in all the profits that would result in the sale of this property. Some weeks after Sidney arrived in Far West, Henry Root and David Thomas visited him there. "After much solicitation on their part, it was agreed" that the Saints would make a settlement in De Witt, which is about 65 miles east and a little south of Far West. [^14]

George M. Hinkle and John Murdock, members of the High Council, were sent to purchase land in De Witt on 23 June 1838. They paid $500 for the undivided half of the De Witt town plot, for which they gave a note that Bishop Edward Partridge had endorsed (See Figures 1 and 2). They immediately set out with their families to settle their newly purchased land. [^15] By July 1838 a number of families were moving to De Witt. Soon De Witt was made a stake of Zion and designated as a port of landing on the Missouri River. [^16] George Hinkle was called as branch president.

With continuing arrivals of Latter-day Saints in De Witt, the Carroll County citizens became concerned that the Saints would become an overwhelming majority. According to Judge A. C. Blackwell, an active participant in activities against the Saints, the residents of Carroll County became convinced that they had only two choices: to give up the county to the Latter-day Saint settlers or to drive them out. Written handbills began to circulate calling for a meeting in the county clerk's office on 14 July 1838. A large number of citizens showed up and were addressed by Dr. William W. Austin, Sr., a Presbyterian minister. Dr. Austin was chosen to chair the meeting, and Blackwell was chosen as the secretary. At this gathering many expressed their opinions on what action should be taken regarding the Mormons. [^17] The discussion centered on the dangers and responsibilities of driving the Mormons from the county. Another meeting was called for the following Saturday. This time so many attended the assembly that they could not gain access to the building, so they gathered around the doors and the windows to hear the proceedings. "Both in and out of doors great enthusiasm prevailed, the popular feeling setting strongly for an immediate advance on De Witt." However, they realized that they did not have the numbers to make the attack on the Mormons. So Dr. Austin and Hiram Wilcoxson were asked to correspond with the citizens of Howard, Saline, Chariton, Ray, and Clay counties to ask for their assistance in driving the Mormons out of the county. In addition, it was decided to call for a vote supporting or opposing the Mormons at the forthcoming general election. The meeting then adjourned until after the election. [^18]

The committee appointed to inform Latter-day Saints at De Witt consisted of Reverends Abbot Hancock and Sashel Woods, and John Smart. According to John Murdock, this committee came to them about the last of July and "ordered [them] to leave the county by the 7th August." At this he drew his sword, vowing to "fight to the last" rather than be expelled for their religious differences. [^21] Woods replied, "Colonel, put up your sword. According to John Murdock, Woods remained with the mob so he could assault Colonel Hinkle's family during his absence. [^25]

Conditions continued to deteriorate in Carroll County, but a brief reprieve came on 13 September. At this time the Carroll vigilantes postponed an assault on De Witt so they could march to Daviess County to assist the settlers there against the Latter-day Saints. They joined with groups from other counties at Millport, Daviess County, under the pretext of defending the citizens of Daviess County against the Latter-day Saints, and acted under the orders of a Dr. Austin from Carroll county. [^26] This group of nearly 300 men was met by state militia general Alexander W. Doniphan, who read to them General David R. Atchison's order to disperse. They professed that their object for arming and collecting was solely for defense, but they were "marching and counter-marching." [^27] Finally, the vigilantes obeyed this order, and those from Carroll County returned home. Upon their return they again threatened the Latter-day Saints there.

The first major attack came upon the town of De Witt on Friday, 21 September 1838, by a mob of 150 men, "all anxious to try their hand in a brush with the Mormons." The force took up a position just west of De Witt, in heavy timber, and planned to march down the road to the main part of town. Shortly after their arrival, Mr. A. Clinkscales went down the hill to get water from a nearby spring. This action alerted the Latter-day Saints, who ordered him to halt. When he failed to do so he was fired upon. Firing continued on both sides with no injuries on either side, except the following incident.

A Mr. Williams, "a member of the Saline County company," had just fired his gun and was reloading. "He was an inveterate tobacco chewer, and was in the habit of constantly keeping a quid between his under lip and teeth. A small ball from the enemy entered the right side of his lip, and coming out at the left, carried with it the unfortunate chew of tobacco without breaking the skin in front of the mouth." [^28] George Averett, a Latter-day Saint, added his own version of the story. He stated that this Mr. Williams approached the night guard, Alexander Williams. The Saline Williams fired at and missed the Mormon Williams, then broke and ran. The Mormon Williams shot the Saline Williams and "made the mobber call on his God he thought for the first time in his whole lifetime, and it was stated that Williams shot his chew of tobacco out of his mouth." [^29]

The next day, 22 September 1838, the Mormons petitioned Governor Boggs in desperation for relief. They explained to the governor that on 20 September 100 to 150 armed men came and threatened to drive them out of the county, but after deliberation conceded to let them stay until 1 October 1838. If the Mormons were not gone by this time the mob would destroy them. The Latter-day Saints plead with Governor Boggs to "take such steps, as shall put a stop to all lawless proceedings." Fifty men signed this petition, of which at least one was not a member of the Church. [^30] The governor not only ignored this request but, according to Joseph Smith, Boggs later told them "`the quarrel was between the Mormons and the mob,' and that `we might fight it out.'" [^31]

Most of the generals commenting on the conditions in De Witt affirmed that the Latter-day Saints were acting defensively. Two statements to this point are found in the words of Generals Atchison and Parks, respectively: "I have found that there is no cause of alarm on account of the Mormons; they are not to be feared; they are very much alarmed." [^32] "As yet they [Mormons] have acted on the defensive as far as I can learn." [^33] This was also the evaluation of the Chariton County committee sent to investigate the situation at De Witt. "We found them in the act of defence, begging for peace, and wishing for the civil authorities to repair there as early as possible, to settle the difficulties between the parties." [^34]

The actions of the Carroll County mob became more desperate when a large group of Latter-day Saints emigrating from Canada arrived in De Witt, under the leadership of John E. Page. The mob considered these people aliens and unworthy of living among them. In their documents they used some of the following terms:

They said that there was a large portion of people called Mormons, embodied in De Witt from different parts of the world. They are unwilling for them to remain there, which is the cause for their waging war against themÂ�. We will anticipate you immediately, and shall expect your co-operation and assistance in expelling the fanatics, who are mostly aliens by birth, and aliens in principle from the countryÂ�. These facts are made known to you, sir, hoping that your authority will be used to stop the course of this banditti of Canadian refugees. [^35]

The Canadian Saints arrived in De Witt the first week in October, in a company consisting of about 30 wagons. They were very pleased with the appearance of De Witt, calling it "a very beautiful place with broad acres of rich soil covered with grass and plenty of timber nearby, pleasantly located on the bank of the Missouri River, which at that place was about a half mile wide." [^36]

They decided to stop there and they began to settle, build, and prepare for the coming winter by cutting wild hay for feed and cutting logs to build some cabins.

With the arrival of the Canadian Mormons, the Carroll County men became increasingly concerned. But after their initial failure in routing the Mormons, they sent for more firepower and recruits from the surrounding counties. Men from Ray, Howard, and Clay Counties soon arrived. Hiram Wilcoxson went to Jackson County for a cannon.

With more reinforcements coming to the Carroll County army, the Latter-day Saints sent word to Far West for reinforcements also. The humor over the first armed incident was quickly fading; it became soberingly apparent that a major battle was about to take place which could result in the loss of life.

What the Canadian Saints thought at first to be a beautiful place soon turned into a nightmare. It was all they could do to guard their stock and homes from the attacking troops. Zadoc Judd, a Mormon, felt that a showdown was inevitable. This was especially a trial of the faith of these new converts from Canada. They had not yet experienced the persecutions and mobbings that some of the other Saints had. As Judd put it, "This state of affairs was very trying to some of our sober, serious Christians that had been taught that it was wicked to fight; it almost rocked their faith in the gospel; to take up arms and try and kill their fellow mortals was a new doctrine that some could hardly endure." [^37] Some of them were so upset over the whole affair that they feigned sickness and stayed in their wagons. One such was Christopher Merkley. At first he refused to fight, feeling that a religious man could not take such action. However, after a week of mob attacks, he changed his mind. [^38]

John Taylor had a similar reaction: "This was the first mob I had ever seenÂ�and the whole affair was new to me, especially when I considered the kind of officers they had. I had heretofore looked upon gospel ministers as messengers of peace; here they came not only in a war-like capacity, but as the leaders of an armed mob-a gang of marauders and free-booters, with the avowed object of driving peaceful citizens-men, women, and children-from their homes." [^39] Taylor did not believe in fighting for slight encroachments of his rights, even though he believed in self-defense, but like the other Canadian converts, he now knew he had to act. "I had no armsÂ�and heretofore considered that I needed none in a Christian civilized land; but I found I had been laboring under a mistake. The civilization here was of a very low order, and the Christianity of a very questionable character. I therefore threw off the sling and bandages from my lame arm, suppressing my repugnance to fighting, borrowed a gun, bought a brace of pistols, and prepared myself at least for defensive measures." [^40]

The attack on De Witt began in earnest on 1 October 1838. Led by Hyrum Standly and reinforced by men from the surrounding counties, the Carroll County vigilantes attacked the home of Smith Humphrey, who lived one and a half miles outside of the city. They seized Humphrey's household goods, and Standly burned two blocks of houses. The Humphreys were then compelled to move into De Witt, although Humphrey's wife was sick with the ague, and they stayed there until the Saints were driven out. [^41]

After these early skirmishes, both sides delayed further action for a few days. A local history stated that "The Mormons evacuated their works and fled to some log houses, where they could more successfully resist the Gentiles, who had in the meantime returned to their camp to await reinforcements. Troops from Saline, Ray and other counties came to their assistance, and increased their number to five hundred men." [^42]

These engagements brought to the attention of the militia officers the critical nature of events in De Witt. General Samuel D. Lucas, who commanded the state militia, reported the conditions to Governor Boggs as early as 25 September 1838. He erroneously reported that several persons had been killed, and worried, "If a fight has actually taken place, of which I have no doubt, it will create excitement in the whole upper Missouri, and those base and degraded beings will be exterminated from the face of the earth." He was confident that if only one of the citizens from Carroll County was killed, 4,000 or 5,000 volunteers would come to De Witt, and "nothing but [the Mormons'] blood will satisfy them." [^43]

General David R. Atchison, another commander in the state militia, was also concerned. He had just received word from Henry Root, who had sold the land to the Latter-day Saints, that on 2 October an armed force of 30 to 50 persons had come into De Witt and fired on the civilians. General Atchison ordered out two companies under Captains Bogart and Houston to help settle the affair. [^44] Unfortunately, many in these troops, especially those under Captain Samuel Bogart, were threatening to join with the vigilantes. Atchison consequently had to return to Ray County.

General Hiram G. Parks of the Missouri militia wrote to General Atchison and was also concerned. When he approached De Witt he was surprised to find a mob of 200 or 300 well-armed men ready to attack De Witt. The assault proceeded with firing on both sides; one of the Saline men was wounded in the hip. The general felt the Latter-day Saints were too strong for the mob to attack De Witt again until 10 or 11 October. However, by that time Reverend Austin hoped to have 500 men with their cannon and small arms ready. Parks worried that without more men he could do nothing but try to negotiate between the two parties. He therefore requested additional troops, saying, "Should these troops arrive in time, I hope to be able to prevent bloodshed. Nothing seems so much in demand here [to hear Carroll county men talk,] as Mormon scalps-as they are scarce. I believe Hinkle, with his present force and position, will beat Austin with five hundred of his troops. The Mormons say they will die before they will be driven out." [^45]

He pleaded with Atchison to get word to Governor Boggs and ask him to come. "You know a word from his Excellency would have more power to quell this affair than a regiment," he wrote. On 9 October, in the midst of the battle, Atchison again asked the governor to come: "I have no doubt but your Excellency, if you should deem it your duty to proceed to Carroll county, could restore peace." He further stated that he had suggested General Parks urge the Latter-day Saints in Carroll County to sell out and move somewhere else, and had also urged the oldtime residents to buy out the Saints. Finally, he again suggested the importance of Governor Boggs' coming soon. "If you deem it necessary to proceed to Carroll county, I would respectfully suggest that it should be done as quick as possible." [^46] But the governor would not come, despite the many requests that were made to him by both the Mormons and the militia. [^47]

Now both sides began to request reinforcements for the predicted battle. Under the leadership of Congrave Jackson from Howard County, 11 men wrote a letter asking for help in the war:

This county is the theatre of a civil war, and will soon be one of desolation, unless the citizens of the adjoining counties lend immediate assistance. The infatuated Mormons have assembled in large numbers in De Witt, prepared for war, and are continually pouring in from all quarters where these detestable fanatics reside.

The war is commenced! Blood has been[shed-[the Mormons] shed it; they waylaid and fired upon a body of citizens of Carroll county and wounded some. They are the aggressors-they have been guilty of high treason; they have violated the laws and shed blood of our citizens; and we think this one of the cases of emergency in which the people ought to take the execution of justice in their own hands. Speedy action is necessary; the progress of their imposition, insult and oppression ought to be checked in the beginning. [^48]

The Latter-day Saints also requested aid from Far West. Joseph Smith was informed by messenger on Thursday, 4 October, that the Saints in De Witt desperately needed help. The next morning Captain Seymour Brunson left for De Witt with 42 mounted and well armed men. The same afternoon Joseph Smith left with another company. Even though Captain Brunson's company met the mob at De Witt, it passed by them unnoticed and arrived at De Witt Saturday, 6 October 1838. [^49] Joseph Smith's company found it difficult to penetrate the defense of the mob, since the main roads were "strongly guarded." But with much difficulty, and by keeping to the less traveled roads, they arrived in De Witt. [^50]

[^51] He was also deeply disturbed by the lack of provisions, which became a real burden for the Mormons. The Saints living in De Witt were in desperate need of supplies, for when they tried to leave De Witt to get food "of which they were destitute," they were shot at; acquiring new provisions became impossible. [^52] Things became so bad that "Some of the brethren died, for want of the common necessaries of life, and perished from starvation." [^53] Joseph Smith said, "For once in my life, I had the pain of beholding some of my fellow creatures fall victims to the spirit of persecution, which did then, and has since prevailed to such an extent in Upper Missouri-men too, who were virtuous, and against whom, no legal process could for one moment, be sustained; but who, in consequence of their love to God-attachment to his cause-and their determination to keep the faith, were thus brought to untimely grave." [^54]

Although Judge Earickson felt the terms were stringent, it was the best he could do. Colonel Hinkle stated they would not submit to such demands; he would "die on the hill rather than accede to such propositions." [^59] Again the judge reminded Hinkle that he could do no better under the circumstances-in fact, the majority of the mob opposed the terms, since they felt they were too liberal. He also told Hinkle that if the Mormons were determined to stand and fight, he must move the women and the children out of town for their protection, as the battle would begin the next morning. Another member of the "peace commission," William F. Dunnica, feared that they could not convince the Mormons to sell out. His reasons were published in an 11 October 1838 Missouri newspaper: "The citizens proposed that if the Mormons would leave the country and not return again, they would pay them back the amount of their property cost, with 10 per cent interest thereon, and return them the amount of their expenses in coming and going out of the county. The Mormons replied that ever since they had been a people they had been driven from place to place, and they had determined they should be driven no more and that they had determined, every one of them to die on the ground." [^60]

The next morning Judge Earickson made another appeal to Colonel Hinkle, telling him frankly how the situation stood: "If one citizen of the county should be killed, a hundred would rush to avenge the blood of the slain. [Earickson] would not be responsible for the safety of the women and children. The sentiment of the people of the surrounding counties was against the Mormons, and they would be driven from the state by the power of the enraged people." [^61]

The local citizens were making final plans for the next assault on De Witt when Judge Earickson came into their camp to inform them that the Mormons had accepted the terms of the surrender. Immediately the Latter-day Saints made preparations to leave the next morning. Two committees formed to settle up the final affairs of the Mormons: William W. Austin, Sr., Abbot Hancock, and A. C. Blackwell as commissioners for Carroll County; and Judge Earickson, William D. Swinney, and W. F. Dunnica, all of Howard County, as commissioners to represent the Latter-day Saints. When the commissioners entered De Witt, they were shocked by the terrible conditions when they "saw the distress of the women and children, their sympathies were aroused, and it is said that both Austin and Hancock, both of whom had been firmly determined to drive away the Mormons at all hazards, shed tears at the evidence of suffering." [^62]

On the morning of 12 October 1838, the Latter-day Saints, with their 70 loaded wagons, left De Witt and journeyed to Caldwell County and the headquarters of the Church, hoping they might be able to live in peace. The History of Carroll County relates, "The Mormons loaded their property on wagons, and the long procession filed out of town, men, women, and children casting sorrowful looks behind them as they left forever the spot on which they had hoped to build a prosperous and thriving city." [^63]

However, their suffering was not over. According to Joseph Smith and many others who left with them, they were harassed, threatened, and even shot at several times as they made their journey to Far West. Several died from fatigue. One of these was a lady who a short time earlier had given birth to a child. It was a very cold October, and as a result of this exposure she died and was buried without a coffin in a small grove of trees. John E. Page later buried his wife and two children "as martyrs to our holy religion, who died through extreme suffering for the want of the common comforts of life, which I was not allowed to provide even with my money." [^64]

This ended the battle of De Witt, but the war was not over. Many of the same players who took part in the dramatic struggle for De Witt contended again at Far West, with the same tragic results. Instead of resolving the conflict, the De Witt encounter had only set the pattern for later persecutions, culminating in the forcible expulsion of the Latter-day Saints from Missouri.
