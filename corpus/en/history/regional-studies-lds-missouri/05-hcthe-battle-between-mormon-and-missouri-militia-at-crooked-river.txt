Battle of Crooked RiverHCThe Battle Between Mormon and Missouri Militia at Crooked River

Battle of Crooked RiverHCAlexander L. Baugh

Battle of Crooked RiverHCDuring the hours just before dawn on Thursday, 25 October 1838, a contingency of Mormon Caldwell County militia engaged in armed conflict on the Crooked River, situated in northern Ray County, with the Ray militia under the command of Samuel Bogart, a Methodist minister. This skirmish, later known as the Battle of Crooked River, resulted in a dozen wounded and the deaths of three members of the Caldwell company-including the Mormon commander Apostle David W. Patten-and one member of the Ray company. Although casualties were limited, a broader examination of the conflict indicates the battle fueled the civil strife between the Mormons and the Missourians during the fall of 1838, and consequently was a leading factor in bringing about the forced expulsion of the Latter-day Saints from the state.

Battle of Crooked RiverHCThe Battle of Crooked River followed soon after the forced removal of several hundred Latter-day Saints from De Witt in Carroll County, which occurred during the first part of October 1838. This expulsion marked essentially the third time a group of Latter-day Saints had been forced from their homes and lands in the state. The first forced exodus was from Jackson County in 1833, and the second was the politically negotiated removal from Clay and Ray counties to the newly created "Mormon" Caldwell County in 1836. Following the dislocation of the De Witt Saints, Missouri assailants continued to extend their threats against Latter-day Saints residing in Daviess County. But on this occasion, Church leaders decided to take decisive action to disperse their antagonists by removing the remaining handful of non-Mormons who continued to reside in Mormon-dominated Daviess County. They justified such aggressive actions because they clearly felt they had been pushed around long enough, and if they were forced to leave Carroll County, they should be entitled to occupy both Caldwell and Daviess counties exclusively.

The immediate events surrounding the Battle of Crooked River essentially began on 23 October, when Reverend Samuel Bogart wrote a letter to Major General David R. Atchison, supreme commander of the state militia in northwestern Missouri. That letter informed Atchison that Bogart and his Ray County troops would patrol the line between Ray and Caldwell counties because "The Mormons have burnt Gallatin, and Mill Port, and have ravaged Daviess countyÂ�and they have threatened to burn Buncombe and Elk Horn and have been seen near and on the line between Ray and Caldwell." Reverend Bogart then stated that his main objective was "to prevent, if possible, any outrage on the county of Ray." [^1] Later that same day, Atchison sent the following reply:

Sir:-Your communication by express has been received. You are hereby ordered to range the line between Caldwell and Ray counties with your company of volunteers, and prevent, if possible, any invasion of Ray county by persons in arms whatever. You will also take care to inquire into the state of things in Daviess county and make report thereof to me, from time to time. [^2]

Bogart's fear of a Mormon invasion into Ray County may have been more pretended than real. However, since the Mormons had recently expelled the non-Mormons from Daviess, Bogart justified doing the same thing to the Mormons who resided in his county. His actions while patrolling the Ray County line clearly demonstrate that his main objective was to threaten, harass, and expel all of the Mormons residing in the region.

Samuel Bogart's intentions may have even extended beyond expelling the Mormons from Ray. Ephraim Owen, a Latter-day Saint, believed Bogart's actions were part of a scheme to "legally" force the Mormons from the state. Owen further believed the captain's plan was "to have the militia lying on the Mormon frontier and make them believe they were a mob; and thus draw them to an attack, that they might have a lawful pretext to call out the forces of the State to expel the Mormons, as they knew the Mormons would not willingly come in contact with the militia." [^3]

On 24 October 1838 Bogart and his militia appeared in the southern portion of Caldwell County where he threatened Latter-day Saints living near Log Creek and Long Creek. The fact that he patrolled outside the boundaries of Ray County indicates either that he believed his orders included extending his reconnaissance into Caldwell County, or that he paid little regard to whether or not he policed only the line separating Ray from Caldwell. Thorit Parsons, one of several Mormons living on the east branch of Log Creek, was harassed by the captain and his men. He recalled that sometime between 1:00 and 3:00 pm on the afternoon of 24 October, Bogart and over 20 armed men ordered him to leave his home and property by the next day. The company also searched the premises for guns, which they hoped to confiscate, but Parsons reported he had none. [^4] Another Latter-day Saint, Arza Judd, Jr., was at Parson's home when Bogart made his appearance, and his testimony substantiates Parson's. Judd also stated that this same company came to his house, ordered him to leave, and said that he would see "thunder and lightning" at Far West before the next day. [^5] Throughout the remainder of the day, the Ray County militiamen roamed the territory, intimidating other Mormon families. And although it is difficult to ascertain, some Latter-day Saints testified Bogart and his men also destroyed a considerable amount of Mormon property. [^6]

The company apparently continued their reconnaissance southward back into Ray. Two Mormons, Joseph Holbrook and David Juda (also Judah or Judith), while patrolling the southern part of Caldwell, learned that Bogart and his men had stopped at the home of Nathan Pinkham, Sr., a Mormon residing in the extreme northern part of Ray County. Here the captain's harassment was more severe. He and several of his men physically threatened Pinkham, took his weapons, shot one of his cows, and told him and his family to move to Far West before the next morning or they would be killed. He then took as prisoners Pinkham's son, Nathan Pinkham, Jr., and William Seely, who was visiting at the time. [^7] Seely was "stripped and searched" to determine if he had any weapons, thrown out of the house by his collar, and dragged and kicked over a fence "so vehemently as[toÂ�[cause] bodily injury." [^8] [^9] When Holbrook and Juda learned about Bogart's treatment of Nathan Pinkham, Sr., and his taking of the three prisoners, they rode to Far West to inform Church leaders of the actions. [^10]

Immediately after taking the prisoners, Bogart retired to a carefully selected position on Crooked River. Peter Burnett recalled that the captain "encamped in a narrow bottom on the banks of a creek [Crooked River], among the large scattering oak timber, and behind a slough-bank some four feet high. He apprehended [anticipated] an attack, and had well selected his[groundÂ�[since] no one could be seen approaching the encampment until arriving with a short distance of it." [^11] Bogart later reported that his forces numbered 35 men. [^12]

Although Church officials were concerned about the general security of the Latter-day Saints who had been threatened by Captain Bogart's men, their main concern was the safety of the three men taken prisoner by the Ray militia. Mormons had dealt with collective persecution, harassment, and abuse at the hands of their Missouri neighbors, but to have some of their numbers taken hostage was a much more serious matter. Bogart's actions must have indicated two things to the Mormons: (1) The group was a mob rather than a legal civil or military body, and (2) Pinkham, Seely, and Green might very well be dead. Lorenzo D. Young later wrote that it was reported that "the brethren [who] had been taken prisoners by the mob on Crooked River, [were to be] tried by a court martial that day, and condemned to be shot the coming morning at eight o'clock." [^13] Reports such as this indicate why the Mormon leaders believed they needed to act immediately to save the lives of the three men. Bogart may have had no intentions of executing the prisoners; however, if he and his men had limited their activities to harassing the Mormons-something which the Saints were somewhat accustomed to-and not taken prisoners, the Mormon militia might not have been so quick to respond.

Within a short time (around midnight), a decision was made by Mormon leaders to liberate the prisoners and disperse the mob. Because of the events of the preceding weeks, Far West had been in a high state of alert; and in case of an emergency, adult males residing in the community were expected to respond in a moment's notice. However, in order to be a legal military body, it was necessary for the judge of the county to issue the official commission. Therefore, "Elias Higbee, the first judge of the county now commanded the militia officers to go out and re-take the prisoners; and Capt. David W. Patten, with about 60 men obeyed the order." [^14] Charles C. Rich noted that after receiving confirmation of the reports about the depredations of the Saints in southern Caldwell County and the taking of prisoners "Judge Higbee issued an order to raise a force to disperse the mob." [^15] The leading Mormon military officer in Caldwell County was Colonel George M. Hinkle, but it was Captain David W. Patten who was placed in command of the company. Rich also recalled that a force of only 40 men could be raised at Far West. Believing a battle was inevitable, Rich felt this number was insufficient and "proposed to go and raise some more men and meet Captain Patten about six miles from Far West." This proposition was agreed to, and Rich reported that he "rode through the settlements on Goose and Log Creeks, and rallied the brethren as I went along." [^16]

The company proceeded south along the main road between Far West and Richmond. Rich and the additional men he was able to round up from the surrounding settlements (bringing the total number in the company to about 60) joined the main company approximately six miles south of Far West near the home of Benjamin Bragg, where they divided into companies of ten. [^17] They continued on the main road for approximately another four miles, until they arrived at the home of Randolph McDonald, a non-Mormon. McDonald's property was situated in Ray County directly on the line bordering the two counties. Here they tied up their horses and proceeded the rest of the distance on foot, leaving a few men to watch the animals. [^18] They also organized themselves into three main groups commanded by David Patten, Charles Rich, and James Durphee. [^19] The militia leaders apparently believed Bogart's troops were camped on the property owned by John Field, situated a short distance south of McDonald's property, so they split up in order to make an attack from three separate directions. However, when the three detachments arrived at the Field property, the Ray militia was not camped there. [^20] Unable to locate the enemy, the leaders met to discuss the next plan of attack. Patten, assuming Bogart's men had situated themselves closer to the river, gave his troops a short speech, exhorting the men to victory, and the company moved on. [^21]

Crooked River is well-named. It winds through parts of Clinton, Caldwell, and Ray counties before emptying into the mighty Missouri in the extreme southeast corner of Ray County. In 1838, the main road from Far West into Ray County ran north and south, crossing the river at a point approximately two miles south of the line between the two counties. On the evening of 24 October the Ray militia camp was situated adjacent to the road on the east side of the river just above the river bottoms (the river runs somewhat north and south at this location). As a precautionary measure, Captain Bogart positioned a picket guard on the road on a hill situated several hundred yards north of the camp. It was at this location the confrontation began as the Mormon troops came in contact with the guard. John Lockhart, one of the picket guards (he does not tell how many of them there were), reported the following:

Myself and the other guards were standing at the same tree, near the road, about a quarter of a mile from the camp; and about day-break we discovered men approaching us in the road. When the front of them got within about 15 or 20 steps of us, the other guard raised his gun. I told him not to shoot, but to hail them. He hailed them, and asked, "Who comes there?" They replied, "a friend;" but still moved on. I hailed the second time, and bade them to stand. I asked who was there; they answered, a friend. I asked them if they had any arms, and go off, and leave them; they told me to come and get them. I again told them to lay them down, and leave them; they made a noise with their guns, as if they were laying them down, and again called to us to come and get them. I could see clear enough to perceive that they had not laid down their guns, as they said they had done; but had them on their shoulders. At that time I discovered one of the men strike the ground with his sword, and immediately I heard a percussion cap burst without the gun's firing. I told the other guard to shoot; that they had bursted a cap at us; and immediately I raised my gun and fired-the other did not shoot. We then ran to camp, where, in a few moments, the Mormons arrived, and the action commenced. [^22]

Lockhart's salient points are that he and another guard hailed the Mormons, made inquiry as to who they were, and requested them to lay down their arms, which request was not observed. He then observed that someone made some sort of motion with a sword, which he interpreted as a signal to fire, at which time a firing cap exploded, indicating to Lockhart they were being shot at, he returned the fire. The fact that several Mormons recalled being hailed by the picket guard substantiates Lockhart's testimony and indicates Bogart's men did not shoot without first giving the Mormons some sort of warning. Lockhart's shot wounded Patrick Obanion, who had been persuaded to act as a guide for the Caldwell militia and was the only identifiable non-Mormon in the company. Lorenzo Young and John Greene carried him to the side of the road, asked the Lord to preserve his life, made him as comfortable as possible, and then returned to their places in the company. [^23]

At this point, the conflict escalated, and following the shooting, the Missouri guards quickly withdrew and fled back to the main encampment to alert their comrades that the Mormons were in the immediate area and would likely attack their position. Being forewarned, the Missourians positioned themselves "under the bank of the creek, below their tents" and waited for the Mormon force to appear. [^24] Perhaps as a tactical maneuver to restrain the Mormons from firing at his troops, Bogart placed at least one of the three prisoners, William Seely, a short distance from the front line, positioning him between the two armies and in the direct line of fire. [^25]

The Mormon company approached the camp of Ray militia as a body and formed a battle line. The Mormon leaders again decided to attack from three directions. Charles Rich stated the men first formed a line about 50 yards from the clump [bank] with "Captain Patten's company at the right, my company next, which brought me in the road, brother Patten's company was partly shielded by a club of trees, and brother Durfee's by a thicket of Hazel brush." Rich further indicated that soon after they were in position, the Ray militia "fired upon us with all their guns." [^26] Parley P. Pratt later wrote, "The fire [soon] became general on both sides, and the whole wilderness seemed one continued echo of the report of the deadly rifle"; and "The whole wilderness seemed for a few moments as if wrapped in a blaze of lightning." [^27] The crack of powder and the volley of bullets lasted only about two minutes. [^28]

The Ray militia clearly held a strategic advantage over the Latter-day Saints. The river bank served as a breastwork and also made it difficult for them to be seen. On the other hand, the Mormons were more exposed to fire, for they had minimal ground cover and no breastwork to protect them. The timing of the battle also proved to be advantageous to Bogart's army. Hosea Stout wrote that since the Missourians were "situated behind the bank of the river and had a fair view of the brethren, who were approaching from the east, and it being at the dawn of day [Bogart's company] could distinctly see them whereas they [Bogart's company] were almost entirely out of sight." [^29]

With his troops almost entirely exposed to enemy fire, Patten perceived his only course for victory would be to charge the enemy in hopes of routing them from their position. [^30] The decision to charge may have been the reason the Mormons were ultimately successful in dispersing Bogart and his men. But Patten's decision to charge cost him his life. Quite naturally, it would take great courage to command as well as respond to an order to charge the enemy in the midst of heavy military fire, but courage was part of Patten's nature. One Latter-day Saint believed this was why the Mormon apostle died. "He was brave to a fault," wrote Ebenezer Robinson. "So much so, that he was styled and called, 'Captain Fearnought.' He seemed reckless of his life, as though it was scarce worth preserving. He had said to us, before there was any indication of a mob, or difficulty with the people of Missouri, 'If I dare to do it, I could wish myself dead.'" [^31] Charles C. Rich was near Patten when the captain received his fatal wound. He recalled that when "Patten ordered us to charge-the enemy fired a few shots and fled, two lingered behind, brother Patten pursued one, and I the other; the man that he pursued wheeled and shot him." The Missourian who shot the Mormon apostle was also aided in that Elder Patten "wore a white blanket coat which made him a conspicuous mark." [^32]

With the Ray militia positioned behind the bank of the river and the Mormons charging from both sides as well as from the front, the only course of escape for the minister's troops was by retreating through the stream to the opposite side of Crooked River. During the retreat, the Mormons continued their fire. It was at this time a member of the Ray militia, Moses Rowland, was hit in the back. He was apparently killed instantly, and his body tumbled down the bank into the river. [^33] After making their way across the stream, Bogart's men scattered in all directions, and the Mormons became the victors. Since the mission of the Mormon troops was to rescue the prisoners and disperse the mob, they did not pursue the enemy any further. What remained was to count their losses, attend to the wounded, and gather up the spoils of their victory.

At first, there appeared to have been no immediate Mormon fatalities, but later this proved not to be the case. Apparently, upon their return to Caldwell County, they discovered one of their number was missing. During the encounter, Parley P. Pratt had seen a man fall who had apparently received some serious injuries to his head. "His face was so marred and disfigured with wounds and blood," he wrote, "that I did not recognize him then, but learned afterwards that we had mistaken him for one of the enemy, and left him on the ground in mistake." [^34] The man was Gideon Carter. Since Carter was assumed to be one of Bogart's men, it is understandable why they left him, assuming the Ray militia would return later to get the body.

Besides Carter's death, nine brethren were wounded. As already noted, Patrick Obanion was shot by John Lockhart, the guard stationed a short distance up the road on a rise above the river crossing. Later, when the actual firing commenced near the river, the three Mormon prisoners made a break for the Mormon lines. But William Seely, whom Bogart had positioned out in front of the line and was exposed to fire from both sides, was shot in the left shoulder and experienced some internal injuries because blood was running out of his mouth when he was found. [^35] James Hendricks, shot in the neck, was found prostrate, his head downhill. He was unable to move any limbs, but could speak. [^36] Patten received a ball in the lower part of his body, which eventually proved fatal. [^37] Joseph Holbrook related that he received a wound "in my left elbow with a sword after cutting through five thicknesses of cloth. [It] so fractured the bone that after the doctor had placed back the bones, it was very lame for some four months and so stiff that I could not feed myself with that hand." [^38] Curtis Hodges was shot in the hip and Eli Chase in the knee. [^39] Hosea Stout indicated Norman Shearer and Arthur Milliken were also wounded, but he made no mention of the type or extent of their injuries. [^40] Thus immediately following the battle, there was one Mormon dead and nine wounded. However, Patten's and Obanion's injuries eventually proved fatal. All the remaining wounded, with the exception of James Hendricks, fully recovered from their injuries.

The only casualty among the Ray militia was Moses Rowland, but at least five of their number were wounded. [^41] One whose injuries were reported was Samuel Tarwater. According to local history, "Two Mormons attacked Tarwater with corn knives and nearly cut him to pieces. He received a terrible gash in the skull, through which his brain was plainly visible, one terrible blow across the face severed the jaw bone and destroyed all the upper teeth, and there was an ugly gash made in his neck. He kept his bed six months and his wounds considerably affected his speech and memory." [^42]

The Mormons helped themselves to the belongings, equipment, and animals left behind by the Ray militia. [^43] Since they had proceeded from the McDonald property on foot, the property of the Ray militia came to be a godsend. "Their baggage wagon was immediately harnessed to a couple of horses," Pratt wrote, "and the wounded were picked up and laid in it upon blankets, while every man saddled and mounted a horse, and we commenced our retreat to the place where we had left our horses and guard, a distance of more than a mile." [^44]

Using the confiscated Ray militia equipment for transport, the Mormons returned to the McDonald property. Here, the wounded were made more comfortable. The jostling of the wagon apparently caused a few of the most seriously injured to experience tremendous discomfort. Captain Patten was in so much distress that he asked to be left behind. To ease his and William Seely's pain, the two men were placed on litters and carried by several men in the company. [^45]

Messengers rode ahead of the main company to report the conflict to Church leaders in Far West, as well as to inform concerned families and parties en route. James Emmett was dispatched by Patten to personally inform Joseph Smith about the incident. [^46] Upon being briefly appraised of the incident, Joseph Smith left the city in a carriage to meet the returning men. Hyrum Smith, Lyman Wight, Heber C. Kimball, and perhaps several others accompanied Joseph. [^47] [Kimball apparently rode ahead of the party and was one of the first to intercept the returning Mormon army. He remembered Patten was glad to see him, but it was evident to Kimball that the captain was in great pain and failing fast. "During his removal his sufferings were so excruciating," the fellow apostle wrote, "that he frequently desired us to lay him down that he might die. But being desirous to get him out of the reach of the mob, and among friends, we prevailed upon him to let us [continue to] convey him." [^48] The Prophet's party and the militia subsequently met near Log Creek, about six miles south of Far West, where the Prophet, assisted by others, "went to the wounded and pronounced blessings on them, and prayed for them to be healed." [^49] From Log Creek, the entire group hoped to convey the wounded the rest of the way to Far West. But because of the severity of Patten's and Obanion's injuries, the group was only able to proceed northward for about two more miles before stopping at the home of Stephen Winchester to let the injured recuperate for a short time. [^50] Heber C. Kimball informs us that Patten lived for about an hour after their arrival at the Winchester's. He noted that some people there offered him medical assistance, "yet his wound was such, that there was no hope entertained of his recovery; this he was perfectly aware of." The wounded apostle, perhaps thinking about the recent disaffection of Thomas B. Marsh (the senior member and President of the Twelve), also affirmed to those present his faith and testimony in the gospel restored through the instrumentality of Joseph Smith as well as his hope for eternal life. Patten spoke to his wife, Ann, counseling her, "Whatever you do else, O, do not deny the faith!" Kimball further recorded Patten's last moments. "A few minutes before he died he prayed as follows: 'Father I ask thee, in the name of Jesus Christ, that thou wouldst release my spirit and receive it unto thyself:' and then said to those who surrounded his dying bed, 'Brethren, you have held me by your faith, but do give me up and let me go I beseech you.' We then committed him to God, and he soon breathed his last, and slept in Jesus without a groan." [^51] Patten died at about 4:00 pm. [^52] Some years prior, Wilford Woodruff reported David Patten had made it known to Joseph Smith that he had asked the Lord to let him die as a martyr. Joseph, deeply saddened, responded, "When a man of your faith asks the Lord for anything, he generally gets it." [^53]

There are discrepancies in the records concerning the death of Obanion. Several Latter-day Saints recalled the young man died on 25 October about the same time as Patten, or within a few hours afterwards. [^54] However, Amasa Lyman reported that on the morning of 26 October he called upon Sidney Rigdon at his home, where he "saw Brother O'Banion who was dying of his wound, received at Crooked River." [^55] [Lyman's testimony agrees with that of John W. Rigdon, who remembered the slain young man was "brought to my Father's house where [he lay] in agony for two days and there he died and his parents came and took the body away." [^56]

The battle was also accompanied by several after shocks. Immediately following the conflict, spurious and exaggerated reports about the contest increased the already hostile attitude many Missouri citizens had against the Latter-day Saints. "The attack upon Bogart, and the mob under his command at Crooked River, added wonderfully to the excitement already existing in upper Missouri," wrote Ebenezer Robinson, "and created wide spread alarm, on account of the exaggerated statements made with regard to it. The report went abroad, and circulated like wildfire, 'that Bogart, and all his company, amounting to between fifty and sixty men, were massacred by the Mormons, except three,' whereas only one of his men was killed." [^57]

On 27 October 1838, two days after the battle, Governor Lilburn W. Boggs received word of the conflict from Amos Rees and Wiley C. Williams. The report he received led Boggs to perceive that the Mormons not only perpetrated the conflict, but that they were coming out in open rebellion against the state of Missouri by deliberately attacking state militia. Boggs reasoned that such antagonism was not only unlawful, but also treasonous. This fact emerges clearly in the governor's letter (known in Mormon historical circles as the "exterminating order"), calling for General Clark to raise troops and march against the Mormons. Note the following passage from Boggs' letter: "The Mormons [are] in the attitude of open and avowed defiance of the laws, and of having made open war upon the people of this state." This is why the Missouri governor felt justified in "exterminating" the Latter-day Saints, or at least driving them from the state, and why his official order stated, "The Mormons must be treated as enemies." [^58]

Another major consequence resulting from the conflict was the forced escape from Far West of many of the men who participated in the battle. This escape occurred because, after learning of the encounter against Missouri militia, Mormon leaders and military officers feared that those men who had taken part in the Battle of Crooked River would likely be arrested and killed for their involvement. According to Lorenzo D. Young, several Church leaders-including Joseph Smith, Hyrum Smith, and Lorenzo's brother, Brigham Young-believed the men "would be tried by a court martial and[shot,Â�[So] after counseling over the matter, it was decided that I, and others in the same situation, should start that night into the wilderness north, for the Des Moines River, in Iowa Territory." [^59] Not all those who participated in the battle fled, but at least two known groups made their escape first into Iowa and then into Illinois. The largest group left about midnight on 31 October, the evening before the fall and surrender of Far West. Hosea Stout reported there were some 27 men in this group. [^60] When General Clark learned of their escape, he sent a contingency of 60 men to overtake them, but they were unsuccessful. Young reported that although a heavy snowstorm had fallen, their pursuers were unable to track them down because a wild wind covered up their trail soon after it was made. [^61] A second group left on 1 November, the day of the surrender. It was a much smaller group, consisting of only six or seven men. [^62] Both groups escaped from the authorities. Some of these men waited in Iowa and Illinois until their families joined them after agreeing to leave the state. Others possibly returned to assist their families in evacuating the state when they no longer feared the possibility of arrest and prosecution.

Following the Mormon surrender at Far West, Missouri authorities sought out and arrested men who participated in the battle who had not escaped. Again, it was Samuel Bogart who conducted many of these searches and did the arresting. Charles C. Rich's wife, Sarah, recorded that Bogart knew her husband was one of the leaders of the Mormon company, and "this is why he wished to kill him, and my husband and some others were compelled to flee for their lives." [^63]

Another Mormon whom Bogart was intent on capturing was Joseph Holbrook, who had assailed and seriously wounded Samuel Tarwater of the Ray militia. In order to escape arrest, Holbrook hid out in the hayloft at the home of Levi Hancock. Levi's son, Mosiah, remembered the refugee had to remain in the loft until he was able to get out of the state. Mosiah also recalled that Bogart visited their home in search of Holbrook. "The men were heavily armed, and they searched the premises around before they came up to the house late at night," Mosiah wrote. The captain then came to the door and said, "I have a search warrant for Joseph Holbreck [sic]." Levi Hancock asked them to come in, but then Bogart refused and left, believing Holbrook was not there. [^64] On another occasion, Brother Holbrook was nearly discovered by a search party but successfully avoided capture by dressing up as a sick woman confined to bed. He played the part so nicely, wrote Nancy Tracy, that "he was not detected although the house was searched well." [^65]

There were nevertheless a few arrests. By the time the Richmond Court of Inquiry was held on 12 November, a total of 53 Latter-day Saint men had been arrested and charged with treason, murder, burglary, arson, robbery, and larceny. [^66] Among those 53 defendants, five individuals-Parley P. Pratt, Norman Shearer, Darwin Chase, Luman Gibbs, and Morris Phelps-were charged with murder in connection with the death of Moses Rowland in the Battle of Crooked River and ordered to the Richmond Jail (where they had been confined) to await trial. The sources also indicate these five men were the only ones ever arrested for their participation in the conflict. Although they subsequently avoided further prosecution, it was several months before they were able to secure their freedom. On 24 April 1839, Judge King released Darwin Chase and Norman B. Shearer after an imprisonment of over five months. [^67] [^68] Phelps and Pratt's imprisonment in Columbia lasted until 4 July, when they jumped the jail guard and escaped, eventually making their way to Illinois. [^69] Following this successful escape, Gibbs was released by the Missouri authorities. [^70]

In this paper I have attempted to provide a more complete and detailed account of the Battle of Crooked River than has previously been available. An examination of contemporary Mormon and non-Mormon sources reveals that the most significant preliminary event leading to the battle was the expulsion of the Mormons residing in De Witt in Carroll County. They reciprocated by dispersing the mob element in Daviess County and expelling any remaining non-Mormons residing in the county. In turn, the Ray militia, under the leadership of Samuel Bogart, responded by harassing Latter-day Saints living near the Ray and Caldwell county lines and by taking three Mormon prisoners. The Mormons reacted quickly to take their men back and the result was armed conflict. Following the battle, misunderstandings and false reports resulted in heightened suspicion by Missourians about Mormon intentions. Nowhere is this more evident than in the case of Governor Lilburn W. Boggs, who, upon learning of the conflict, believed the Mormons were treasonous and should be driven from the state. In addition, the legal ramifications for those Mormons involved in the encounter caused a large number of them to seek an early refuge outside the state. Those who were unable to avoid arrest were apprehended and experienced a lengthy imprisonment, though they were never fully prosecuted for their involvement.
