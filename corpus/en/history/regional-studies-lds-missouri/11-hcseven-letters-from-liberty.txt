H. Dean Garrett

Incarceration at Liberty JailHCPersecution, loneliness, and suffering were the lot of the Mormon prisoners in Liberty Jail. Joseph Smith, Hyrum Smith, Lyman Wight, Caleb Baldwin, Alexander McRae, and Sidney Rigdon were incarcerated in Liberty Jail from 1 December 1838 to 6 April 1839, awaiting trial on false charges stemming from the difficulties between the Mormons and the old settlers of Missouri. While these Church leaders were imprisoned, the activities of the mobs and the governor's Extermination Order forced the Saints to flee from Missouri to Illinois. Even though imprisoned, Joseph Smith still remained their leader. The letters he wrote from Liberty Jail to different individuals and to the Church reflect that knowledge and also reveal the Prophet's reaction to persecution, loneliness, and suffering he and the Saints experienced.

The four months Joseph Smith and his companions spent in Liberty Jail provided unique opportunities for spiritual growth and development. "Herein we see Joseph testifying while suffering, learning while teaching, giving direction to the work while he was being tutored, giving blessings while being cursed." [^1] The letters Joseph Smith wrote from Liberty Jail to the Saints and to his family reflect this growth. These letters provide insights on the Prophet's views of the events and sufferings of the prisoners and the Saints. The major themes of his letters are the prisoners' innocence, trials and tribulations, personal and family feelings, and counsel to the Saints. "The eyes of the Saints were turned to it [Liberty Jail] as the place whence would come encouragement, counsel-the word of the Lord. It was more temple than prison, so long as the prophet was there." [^2]

Seven Letters

Seven letters written by Joseph Smith from Liberty Jail have been documented. [^3] The first one, dated 1 December 1838, informed his wife, Emma, that the prisoners had arrived safely in Liberty. This letter was followed by one written on 16 December to the Saints in Caldwell County. The third letter of the seven was written on 15 March to Presendia Huntington Buell, who visited the prisoners once in February and tried to visit again but was turned away by the jailers. This letter thanked her for her friendship and interest in the welfare of the prisoners.

The fourth letter was written to the Church at Quincy, Illinois, and to all Saints scattered abroad after they had been driven out of Missouri. Written on 20 March 1839, it encouraged and instructed the Saints as they regrouped in Illinois. Some of its contents eventually comprised D&C 121, 122, 123 of the Doctrine and Covenants. The fifth letter, written on 21 March to his wife, Emma, was in response to a letter from her. One day later, Joseph wrote to Isaac Galland, a land owner in Iowa Territory and in the Commerce, Illinois, area. It provided Galland a better understanding of the Latter-day Saints and their beliefs. It expressed a desire to purchase land from Galland. [^4] The seventh and last letter written before the prisoners escaped was dated 4 April 1839. Addressed to Emma, it expressed endearment and love, giving her encouragement and advice on training the family in his absence.

Suffering for the Testimony of Jesus

Although there are many theories of social, economic, and political reasons for the conflict in Missouri, [^5] the Prophet's letters written from Liberty carry a theme that they were in prison for "the testimony of Jesus." [^6] Joseph wanted the Saints to view him as the Old Testament character, Mordecai, who was accused by Haman of false doings-the Saints were to remember that it was Haman who was guilty and hanged, not Mordecai. Joseph pointed out that the brethren were in their current predicament "because we are honest men and were determined to defend the lives of the saints at the expense of our own." [^7] Charges were filed against them, but they had "no retraction to make, we have reproved in the gate and men have laid snares for us we have spoken words and men have made us offenders, and notwithstanding all this our minds are not darkened but feel strong in the Lord." [^8] Although imprisoned under charges that could have cost them their lives, they were never found guilty of those charges.

Apostates falsely accused Joseph for his testimony of Jesus. He reacted to their accusations in his letters by referring to Mr. Hinkle, one of the apostates, as "a wolf in sheep's clothing." [^9] Joseph compared John Corrill and Reed Peck with those who led the Savior into camp "prepared for the slaughter," and W. W. Phelps was described as one who "comes up before us as one of Job's comforters." Joseph further added:

God suffered such kind of beings to afflict Job, but it never entered into their hearts that Job would get out of it all. This poor man who professes to be much of a prophet has no other dumb ass to ride but David Whitmer to forbid his madness when he goes up to curse Israel, and this ass not being of the same kind of Balaams therefore the angel notwithstanding appeared unto him yet he could not penetrate his understanding sufficiently so but what he brays out cursings instead of blessings. [^10]

Of the mobocrats who abhorred his testimony of Jesus, Joseph wrote: "The soldiers and officers of every kind hated us, and the most profane blasphemers and drunkards & whoremongers hated us, they all hated us most cordially. And now what did they hate us for, purely because of the testimony of Jesus Christ." [^11] [^12]

Among the most vicious rumors calculated to harm the Prophet Joseph and annul his testimony of Jesus were fabrications of the community of wives. The rumors of the community of wives grew from the practice of the Law of Consecration. Joseph responded: "We have heard that it has been reported by some that some of us should have said that we not only dedicated our property but our families also to the Lord, and Satan taking advantage of this has transfigured it into lasciviousness such as a community of wives." [^13] He added that the practice of consecration of property assisted in the caring of the poor and the needy. When one dedicates his wife and children to the Lord, he "does not give them to his brother or to his neighbor for there is no such law for the law of God is thou shalt not commit adultery thou shalt not covet thy neighbor's wife. He that looketh upon a woman to lust after her has committed adultery already in his heart." [^14] (This charge stemmed from the public discussion concerning the practice of plurality of wives. As early as 1831, Joseph Smith received the revelation concerning the plurality of wives (D&C 132, see Preface). The Prophet instructed Oliver Cowdery and others that the time had not come for the living of the law, but that the time would come.

During the early 1830 rumors of clandestine polygamy spread among both the Saints and their detractors. Official pronouncements denied its existence, since at that time the Prophet did not intend to make the principle a matter of Church doctrine. Some members, told by the Prophet that a new marriage system eventually would be introduced, assumed the prerogative of practicing it without authorization. [^15]

The rumors led to charges of adultery being leveled against the Prophet and other leaders. Although historical facts are hard to come by on the subject of plural marriage, evidence from the letter to the Saints in Caldwell County suggests that it had become a public issue, and Joseph wanted the Saints to understand that it was a false charge.

Enemies also sought to discredit Joseph's testimony by citing his relationship with Dr. Sampson Avard and the Danite organization. The Danites, led by Avard, acted against the dissenters from the Church and eventually against the mobs of Missouri who arose against the Saints. Under Avard's leadership, they went beyond legal means in their efforts to defend the Saints. When Avard and others were arrested and brought before the court, he testified that everything he had done with the Danites was under the direction of Joseph Smith. He also taught many things in secret that he attributed to the First Presidency of the Church, even though Joseph was unaware these things were being taught. The Prophet wrote:

We have learned also since we have been in prison that many false pernicious things which were calculated to lead the saints far astray and to do great injury have been taught by Dr. Avard as coming from the Presidency and we have reason to fear that many other designing and corrupt characters like unto himself have been teaching many things which the presidency never knew of being taught in the church by any body untill after they were made prisoners, which if they would have known of, they would have spurned. [^16]

The Prophet hoped the Saints would not believe everything they heard.

The apostates were in serious spiritual trouble as far as Joseph was concerned. He felt the reason for the open attack by former friends was due to "ignorance, yea, the very depth of ignorance is the cause of it, and not only ignorance but gross wickedness on the part of some and hypocrisy." [^17] The Saints were told that those who charged transgression did so because "they are the servants of sins and are the children of disobediance themselvs." [^18] Because of their wickedness, they would lose the rights of the priesthood, and "their basket shall not be full their houses and their barns shall famish and they themselvs shall be despised by those that flattered them." [^19] He observed, "Such characters God hates we cannot love them the world hates them and we sometimes think the devil ought to be ashamed of them." [^20]

Joseph Smith's personal experience and the vantage of his months in jail, however, helped him understand that some of the problems the Saints had experienced developed from their own hands or misdeeds. There was an "aspiring spirit which spirit has oftentimes urged men[fo[r]wards to make foul speaches and influaance the church to reject milder councils and has eventually been the means of bringing much death and sorrow upon the church." [^21] The Prophet was concerned that the Saints would make unwise choices as to whom they followed. "Again outward appearance is not always a Criterean for us to Judge our fellow man but the lips betray the haughty and over baring[imm[a]ginations of the heart." [^22] He encouraged the Saints to remain stable and keep focused on the leadership that God had called and placed at the head. An imprisoned Joseph continued to give counsel and advice to his Saints.

Some of the apostates were allowed to run their course "because their harts are corrupt and the thing which they are willing to bring upon others and love to have others suffer may come upon them selves to the verry utmost that they may be disappointed also and their hopes may be cut off." He assured her that "trials will only give us that knowledge to understand the minds of the Ancients." [^25]

Incarceration at Liberty JailHCJoseph personally understood that the trials of imprisonment had provided spiritual benefit: "For my part I think I never could have felt as I now do if I had not suffered the wrongs that I have suffered all things shall work together for good to them that love God." [^26] One week after he wrote the letter to Sister Buell, he wrote to the Church at Quincy, Illinois, expressing his sorrow for their suffering and explaining that he, having been falsely imprisoned, could understand their suffering. He felt that those experiences had a purifying effect on him.

One token of friendship from any sorce whatever a wakens and calles into action evry simpathetick feeling it brings up in an instant evry thing that is pased it sesses [seizes] the presant with a vivasity of lighting it grasps after the future with the fear sness of a tiger it rhetrogrades from one thing to an other untill finally all enmity malice and hatred and past diferances misunderstanding and mismanagements be slain victoms at the feet of hope and when the hart is sufficiently contrite then the voice of inspiration steals along and whispers my son peace be unto thy soal. [^27]

God had previously revealed that he was going to have a tried and proven people (see D&C 98:13-15). "This time he has chosen his own crusible." [^28] The Prophet promised that if the Saints could survive the test and keep the faith "that it will be a sign to this generation alltogether sufficient to leave them without excuse." [^29] [The tests the Saints were experiencing were "equal to that of Abraham" and if the Saints could survive it, the ancients "will not have were off [whereof] to bost over us in the day of judgment as being called to pass through heavier afflictions that we may hold an even waight in the balances with them." [^30] [^31] for them so that they might be delivered. Joseph had great faith that deliverance would come. He wrote to Sister Buell that he felt "our bondage is not of long duration." [^32]

The Saints needed to learn how to respond to the improper use of authority. Joseph wrote on 20 March 1839 that it was the "disposition of almost all men as soon as they get a little authority as they suppose they will imediately begin to exercise unritious dominion." [^33] The Saints were to make sure their behavior was consistent with the principles of the gospel. It was the Prophet's hope that the Saints would "becum as little Children without mallice guile or Hypokrisy." [^34] Under no circumstance should the Saints seek revenge for the sufferings that they had experienced. Joseph gave strong counsel that God "must do his own work or it must fall to the ground we must not take it in our hands to avenge our wrongs." [^35]

Counsel to the Saints

Although the Saints had been denied the right to exercise their own religious beliefs in peace, Joseph counseled them to allow others to have that privilege. The Saints should always

be awair of such things. and we ought always to be awair of those prejudices which somtimes so strongly presented themselves and are so congenial to human nature against our neighbors friends and bretheren of the world who choose to differ with us in opinion and in matters of faith. Our religeon is betwean us and our God their religeon is betwean them and their God. [^36]

A relationship should be "exercised to wards those of our faith who walk uprightly which is peculiar." [^37] However, it must be practiced without prejudice. This type of behavior would allow the Saints to conduct themselves "with grater liberality to word all others that are not of our faith than what they exercise to wards one another these[principal[s] approximate nearer to the mind of God because it is like God or God like." [^38]

The Saints were to care for each other. Joseph counseled the Saints not to "aspire after their own aggrandisement and seek their own oppulance" while many of their brethren were in poverty and "are under sore trials and tempations." [^39] It was important for the Saints to remember that when their brethren were in poverty they could not "be benefeited by the intersesions of the holy spirit." [^40] The Prophet counseled: "We ought at all times to be verry carefull that such highmindedness never have place in our harts but condesend to men of low estate and with all long suffering bare the infermities of the weak. Behold there are many called but few are chosen. And why are they not chosen? Because their hearts are set so much upon the things of this world and aspire to the honors of men." [^41]

He counseled the Saints to settle in an area that would be safe but advised that they institute "no organizations of large bodies upon common stock princepals in property or of large companies of firms untill the Lord shall signify it in a proper maner as it opens such a dredfull field for the avericious and the indolent and corrupt hearted to pray upon the inocent." [^42] It was not that these practices were wrong, but rather "many things were introduced among the saints before God had signified the times." [^43] Many things were still to be revealed to the Church, but "the times have not yet come but will come as fast as there can be found place and reception for them." [^44]

Personal and Family Life

Some of the greatest insights gained from the Liberty letters pertain to the personality and feelings of the Prophet himself and his concern for his family. His first letters from Liberty exhibited his strength and determination to overcome all trials placed before him. His later letters were mild and loving, containing expressions of concern and compassion for the Saints and his family. Liberty Jail was a purifying time for Joseph Smith. He was able and perhaps even forced to sit back and view the events of Missouri, and especially his life, in a different light. Watching the suffering of the Saints, as well as enduring the trials and pains that he himself suffered, did not harden Joseph, but rather deepened his love for God and commitment to his family and the Saints. This is best illustrated in his letters to Emma and his children. [^45]

The final two letters written to Emma disclosed strong feelings which the separation had developed in Joseph. "As to yourself if you want to know how much I want to see you, examine your feelings, how much you want to see me, and Judge for[you[r]self, I would gladly walk from here to you barefoot, and bareheaded, and half naked, to see you and think it great pleasure, and never count it toil." [^46] Joseph desired to care for Emma and the children, but could not provide for them at this time of great distress. He wrote on 21 March: "Dear Emma I very well know your toils and simpathise with you if God will spare my life once more to have the privelege of takeing care of you I will ease your care and indeavour to cumfort your heart." [^47]

Being separated from his wife and family during such difficult times was trying for Joseph. Perhaps his children might view his absence as an abandonment and a loss of his love for them. In the letters that Joseph wrote to Emma in March [^48] and April, he insisted that she teach the children why he was in prison. "Do you tell them I am in prison that their lives might be saved." [^49] [He desired that Emma "not let those little fellows, to forgit me, tell them Father loves them with a perfect love, and he is doing all he can to git away from the mob to come to them." He looked forward to the time when he could "see little Frederick, Joseph, Julia, and Alexander, Joana, and old major [his dog]." [^50]

Because of Joseph's absence, it was Emma's responsibility to properly train the children. He counseled her to "teach them all you can, that they may have good minds, be tender and kind to them dont be fractious to them but listen to their wants, tell them Father says they must be good children and mind their mother." [^51] He requested that Emma write a "long letter and tell me all you can and even if old major is alive yet and what those little pratlers say that cling around[you[r] neck." [^52]

A danger also existed that Emma in her moments of discouragement and despair might doubt her husband and his prophetic role. Rumors were rampant concerning Joseph; he was concerned that Emma not lose faith. He admonished her "by way of my duty, do not [be] self willed, neither harber a spirit of revevenge: and again remember that he who is my enemy, is yours also, and never give up an old tried friend who has waded through all manner of toil, for your sake, and throw him away[becau[se] fools may tell you he has some faults." [^53]

Conclusion

The letters written during the confinement in Liberty Jail reveal the spirit and character of Joseph Smith. His letter to the Church at the beginning of his stay in jail discloses compassion and love but is strong in resolve and intent. His last letters, written in March and April, are more sensitive and deeper in spiritual strength. His absence from his wife, family, and friends, coupled with his suffering and trials as a prisoner, focused his feelings and attention on those things that were most important and allowed him to develop greater faith in God's hand in his life. He learned that his bounds were set, his days known, and his years would "not be numbered less." [^54] Although he suffered much and was deprived of many things for no fault of his own, he learned that "all these things shall give thee experiance and shall be for thy good." [^55] Not only did he come to a better understanding of himself, but he also "sought God in this rude prison, and found him." [^56]

Liberty Jail was a spiritual watershed for Joseph Smith. He left jail a different man than when he entered it. His own spiritual depths were plumbed, and deeper understanding gained. Perhaps Liberty Jail was a preparation for Carthage.
