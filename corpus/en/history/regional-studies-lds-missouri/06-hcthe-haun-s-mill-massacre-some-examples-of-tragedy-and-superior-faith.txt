Haun's Mill MassacreHCIntroduction-Background of Haun's Mill

Haun's Mill MassacreHCHaun's Mill once stood about 16 miles due east of Far West, Missouri, on the north bank of Shoal Creek. Jacob Haun built the mill between 1835 and 1836 after moving there from Green Bay, Wisconsin. It was the second mill built in Caldwell County. Between 1836 and 1837, some members of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints made settlements along Shoal Creek and perhaps some thirty families of Saints had settled near Haun's Mill by October 1838. This little settlement consisted of the mill, a blacksmith shop, and six to eight houses. [^1]

Haun's Mill MassacreHCFueled by a skirmish between some Missourians and Mormons at the elections in Gallatin on 6 August 1838, misunderstandings and bitterness grew rapidly between the two groups. Distorted reports and rumors circulated on both sides. Many Missourians, stirred up by numerous false accusations against the Mormons, congregated together in illegal groups, armed themselves with weapons, and vowed to drive the Mormons from Missouri. [^2]

Haun's Mill MassacreHCMounting prejudice, misunderstandings, and false reports grew out of proportion on both sides, and in the fall of 1838, Missouri group violence became increasingly bold. Many Mormons were threatened, their houses raided, and animals and possessions taken. On 11 October 1838, the Mormons were forced to leave De Witt, and all the Saints in outlying settlements received counsel from Joseph Smith to move into Far West. Many heeded the counsel, but some, failing to sense the precarious conditions, didn't obey. [^3]

Haun's Mill MassacreHCIn late October, upon hearing rumors that the Mormons were beginning a war to conquer Missouri, Samuel Bogart organized his own militia and began driving any remaining Mormons out of southern Caldwell County. This group was reported to be holding three Mormon prisoners. Based upon this information, David W. Patten led the Mormon Caldwell militia to search for Bogart and free the prisoners. The ensuing conflict became known as the Battle of Crooked River. Three Mormons, including Patten, were killed, one Missourian was killed, and a number were wounded on both sides. False reports circulated that the Mormons had massacred the Missouri company. Distorted reports reached Governor Lilburn W. Boggs, and without further investigation, on 27 October 1838, he issued his infamous Exterminating Order. [^4]

According to an account of the History of Caldwell County published in 1886 by the National Historical Company, conditions at the mill in late October were as follows:

News that the militia of the State had been ordered to expel them had reached the Mormons, and following these tidings word was brought that a considerable number of men living in Livingston county, together with some from Daviess, had organized near Spring Hill, in Livingston county, and were preparing to attack them. A company of about thirty men, indifferently armed with shot guns and squirrel rifles, was organized, and David Evans, a Danite, was chosen captain. It was determined to defend the place.

Learning that the force organizing against them numbered some hundreds, some of the older men among the Mormons urged that no resistance should be made, but that all should retreat to Far West. It seems that the Prophet had advised this, but nevertheless had given them permission to remain if they thought they could protect themselves. [^5]

According to John D. Lee, this same historical account reports that the following conversation took place between the Prophet Joseph Smith and Jacob Haun on 26 October 1838:

John D. Lee says that the morning after the fight on Crooked River, Haun himself came to Far West to consult with the Prophet concerning the removal of the Mormons on Lower Shoal Creek to Far West. The Prophet said, "Move in, by all means, if you wish to save your lives." Haun replied that if the settlers left their homes all of their property would be lost and the Gentiles would burn their houses and other buildings. Joseph Smith said, "You had better lose your property than your lives, but there is no danger of losing either if you will do as you are commanded." Haun thought he and his neighbors could protect and defend themselves, and Smith finally gave them permission to remain, saying they would consider him a tyrant if he forced them to leave and abandon their property and come to Far West. [^6]

On 8 June 1867, John D. Lee recorded in his diary that "Jos. permited Haun to geather the Brethern and defend their Mill but stated at the same time that they would be massacred & sure enough it was done." [^7]

Although some wanted to move to Far West immediately, others opposed retreating and abandoning their property to a mob of ruffians. They felt that the Lord would protect them when the day of battle came. Furthermore, as recorded in his redress petition, Isaac Leany (or Laney), a survivor of Haun's Mill, notes that "some of the neighbours wanted to leave their homes and run off but haveing only about seven waggons to twenty three or four families we had to stay and defend our selves." [^8]

Haun's Mill MassacreHCHaun's Mill Massacre

Haun's Mill MassacreHCMissourians had threatened the Saints around Haun's Mill for some time, and they even threatened to burn down the mill because it ground corn for the Mormons. All the other mills in that part of the country were controlled by Missourians, who refused to grind corn for the Saints, hoping to starve them out. Because of the many threats, a few of the brethren guarded the mill. The threats continued for several days. [^9]

Haun's Mill MassacreHCOn 28 October 1838, one of the militia leaders, Colonel William O. Jennings of Livingston County, sent one of his men to the little settlement to negotiate peace. The Saints readily accepted this idea, and both sides decided to control themselves and prevent any further hostilities. However, other groups of militia in this area, such as those commanded by Captain Nehemiah Comstock, Thomas R. Bryan, and William Mann, were not affected by this agreement, and so the Saints remained under arms.

Haun's Mill MassacreHCAccording to some of the residents of Haun's Mill, 30 October 1838 was a beautiful autumn day with a gentle breeze rustling through the corn and stirring the colored leaves of the trees. The merry laughter of the children rang out as they played along the banks of Shoal Creek, and many mothers could be heard singing as they prepared meals, washed clothes, and attended to other household duties. The men gathered crops and guarded the mill. All seemed beautiful and peaceful. [^10]

However, the "Mormon leader, David Evans, had become apprehensive of an attack, and was about sending out scouts and pickets. It was arranged to use the blacksmith shop as a fort or block-house. This structure was of logs, with wide cracks between them, and had a large door. The greater portion of the Mormons were unsuspicious of imminent danger, and the women and children were scattered about. Nearly every house contained two or more families. There were two or three small houses on the south bank of the creek thus occupied. It was now about four o'clock in the afternoon." [^11]

Elder Joseph Young, First President of the Seventies, had arrived with his family at Haun's Mill just two days earlier. In the following affidavit, he records what he saw of the Haun's Mill Massacre:

It was about four o'clock, while sitting in my cabin with my babe in my arms, and my wife standing by my side, the door being open, I cast my eyes on the opposite bank of Shoal creek and saw a large company of armed men, on horses, directing their course towards the mills with all possible speed. As they advanced through the scattering trees that stood on the edge of the prairie they seemed to form themselves into a three square position, forming a vanguard in front.

At this moment, David Evans, seeing the superiority of their numbers, (there being two hundred and forty of them, according to their own account), swung his hat, and cried for peace. This not being heeded, they continued to advance, and their leader, Mr. Nehemiah Comstock, fired a gun, which was followed by a solemn pause of ten or twelve seconds, when, all at once, they discharged about one hundred rifles, aiming at a blacksmith shop into which our friends had fled for safety; and charged up to the shop, the cracks of which between the logs were sufficiently large to enable them to aim directly at the bodies of those who had there fled for refuge from the fire of their murderers. [^12]

Warren and Amanda Smith and their family arrived at Haun's Mill that afternoon. Amanda describes the ensuing attack:

Our men called for the women and children to run for the woods while they ran for the blacksmith shop, for they feared if we all ran together they would rush upon us and kill men, women, and childrenÂ�. I took my little girls, my boys I could not find, and ran for the woods, the mobs encircled us in all sides excepting the bank of the creek, as I ran down the bank and crossed the mill pond on a plank, ran up the hill on the other side into the bushes. The bullets whistled by me like hailstones and cut down the bushes on all sides of usÂ�. I sat down to witness the scene; when they had done firing they began to howl and one would have thought all the infernos had come up from the lower regions. [^13]

In 1854, David Lewis, one of the few men to escape, wrote his account of the massacre: "The first man that fell was Simon Cox, he was standing close by my side when he received the fatal blow, he was shot threw the kidneys, and all the pain and misery I ever witnessed a poor soul in his seemed to excell, it seems as though I could now hear him screamÂ�. Sutch groans of the dying, sutch strugeling in blood, I hope that none that reads this account may ever have to witness." [^14] Lewis described how the mob eventually entered the blacksmith shop and put a stop to most of the suffering and groaning by shooting the wounded with closeup shots in the head.

In April 1839, Isaac Leany recorded his experience at the Haun's Mill Massacre: "The mob still advancing came within about four or five rods when I made my escape by flight being shot four times through the bodyÂ�. I cannot tell how many bullet holes was in my clothing thare was twentyseven in my shirt but to my story haveing made my own escape and hid my self I listened at them shooting the wounded which could not escapeÂ�. Jacob Fouts and Wm Champlin feined their selves dead and lay still until their pockets were robed and after they supposed the wounded all were all dead." [^15] While lying in these conditions, Fouts (or Foutz) and Champlin heard two little boys, Sardius Smith and Charles Merrick, beg for their lives. But William Reynolds and Mr. Glaze put their guns to the boys' heads and blew them off. Reynolds said, "Nits will make lice, and if he had lived he would have become a Mormon." [^16] Being wounded in numerous places, Leany suffered excruciating pain, lying entirely helpless for the next few days, unable to move a limb of his body. [^17]

Nathan K. Knight witnessed one of the mobbers, Mr. Rogers, cut Thomas McBride, a soldier in the American Revolution, to pieces with an old corn cutter. Knight records that he shot Rogers in the hip, and Rogers became a cripple afterwards. Shortly thereafter, Knight was wounded in the right hand, the left leg, and the lower back. Through much exertion he managed to escape and hide in the timber and brush. After an hour or so, Sister Polly Wood found him, but he was so weak from bleeding that he couldn't speak or get to his feet. Knight later stated that Polly "kneeled down and placed her hands on my wounds and prayed the Lord to strengthen and heal me. I never heard a more powerful prayer. The Lord answered her prayer, and I received strength and walked back to Haun's house by resting three or four times." [^18]

John Hammer, a nine-year-old boy at the time, later recorded his views of some of the events that occurred on 30 October 1838, including the horrible death of his father, Austin Hammer:

Several bullets were shot into his [my father's] body, breaking both thigh bones. Some of the brethren thus shot down were dragged out into the yard that their murderers might have a better chance and more room to strip them of their clothing. All who had boots and coats were rifted of the articles. My father had on a new pair of boots that fit him tightly and in an effort to get them off he was dragged in his mangled condition. This cruel treatment must have caused him excruciating pain.

The brethren seeing that the mob party was so numerous and blood thirsty were useless to make any defence. Their only safety was to make their escape as best they could, which they did by fleeing into the woods or brush or where ever they could conceal themselves. When the mob had murdered all they found and robbed them of their clothing they retreated.

After darkness had come on, the brethren who were hiding began to make search for those who had been killed and wounded. My father was found and carried into Haun's house, where he died about twelve o'clock that night. [^19]

Warren Smith was likewise stripped of his coat, hat, and boots and dragged around the mill yard until he was dead. Warren Smith's son, Willard, saw the ruthless mobocrat William Mann drag Smith across the floor of the blacksmith shop in order to pull his boots off. Warren called out, "Oh! You hurt me!" And then Mann dragged him again to get the other boot off. At that moment, Warren died. Later, William Mann showed off these boots in Far West, saying "Here is a pair of boots that I pulled off before the d—d Mormon was done kicking." [^20]

In his affidavit, Joseph Young records the names of those who were killed and wounded in the slaughter:

The number killed and mortally wounded in the wanton slaughter was eighteen or nineteen, whose names as far as I recollect were as follows: Thomas McBride, Levy N. Merrick, Elias Benner, Josiah Fuller, Benjamin Lewis, Alexander Campbell, Warren Smith, Sardius Smith, George S. Richards, Mr. William Napier, Augustine Harmer, Simon Cox, Mr. [Hiram] Abbott, John York, Charles Merrick, (a boy eight or nine years old), [John Lee, John Byers,] and three or four others, whose names I do not recollect, as they were strangers to me. Among the wounded who recovered were Isaac Laney, Nathan K. Knight, Mr. [William] Yokum, two brothers by the name of [Jacob and George] Myers, Tarlton Lewis, Mr. [Jacob] Haun, and several others, [Jacob Foutz, Jacob Potts, Charles Jimison, John Walker, Alma Smith, aged about nine years]. Miss Mary Stedwell, while fleeing, was shot through the hand and fainting, fell over a log, into which they shot upwards of twenty balls. [^21]

When the Missourians finally left, Nathan Knight reports that they "had taken with them all our horses, wagons, cows and all of our property of every description." [^22] Concerning the final deeds of the Missourians on 30 October 1838, Joseph Young states:

To finish their work of destruction, this band of murderers, composed of men from Daviess, Livingston, Ray, Carroll, and Charlton counties, led by some of the principal men of that section of the upper country, (among whom I am informed were Mr. Ashby, of Charlton, a member of the state legislature; Colonel Jennings, of Livingston county; Thomas O. Byron, clerk of Livingston county; Mr. Whitney, Dr. Randall, and many others), proceeded to rob the houses, wagons, and tents, of bedding and clothing, drove off horses and wagons, leaving widows and orphans destitute of the necessaries of life; and even stripped the clothing from the bodies of the slain. [^23]

Warren Smith's widow, Amanda, vividly describes her inner feelings and the conditions at Haun's Mill that evening:

After they had gone I came down to witness the awful scene and Oh, Oh, horrible! What a sight!Â�My husband and one son ten years old lay lifeless upon the ground and one son six years old wounded very bad, his hip all shot off and to pieces, the ground all covered with the dead and dying. There were three little boys crept under the blacksmith's bellows, one of them received three wounds, he lived three weeks and died, he was not mine, the other two were and one of them had his brains all shot out and the other his hip shot to pieces, Realize, my readers, for a moment the scene. Nothing but horror and distress; it was sunset, the dogs were filled with rage, howling over their dead master, the cattle caught the scent of innocent blood and bellowed, a dozen helpless widows, thirty or forty orphaned or fatherless children screaming and grieving for the loss of their husbands and fathers, the groans of the dying and wounded; all this put together was enough to melt the heart of anything but a Missouri mob. [^24]

As the night passed, Mrs. Haun and many of the sisters dressed wounds and tried to make the wounded and dying as comfortable as possible. The women were the only ones available to administer comfort during this night of desolation and suffering. Abner Blackburn recorded the following in his diary:

mr. Eames escaped the masacre and came to our house about dark and said the missourians wear a going to kill all the mormons on shoal creek that night. every one grabbed their clothes and ran for safety to the woods. the mob satiated their thirst for blood retierd from their glut of gore my father was sick with fever and ague. but went to the mill to assist in burying the dead and caring for the wounded the ground was frosen so they couldnot dig graves and they put eighteen dead bodies in a well. they did not know but the mob would return and kill them allso. Our house was full of wounded and dessolate families. [^25]

When daylight arrived on 31 October the able brethren moved about very cautiously. As soon as possible, many of them made their way to the mill to learn the condition of their friends. In the blacksmith shop the sight was totally appalling. Thirteen brethren lay on the floor which was covered with blood. In places the blood was pooled two to three inches deep. For a variety of reasons, including (1) some bodies starting to decompose, (2) wild hogs roaming the woods, (3) few men available to dig graves, (4) frozen ground, and (5) Missourians still lurking about nearby, the dead had to be hastily buried. According to the History of Caldwell County (1886), there "was a large unfinished well at the place, and the bodies were gathered up, the women assisting, and borne, one at a time, all gory and ghastly, to this well and slid in from a large plank. All of the corpses were disposed of in this way; then some hay or straw was strewn over the ghastly piles and then a thin layer of dirt thrown on the hay." [^26]

Concerning this "grave,"Amanda Smith records, "Over that rude grave-that well-where the nineteen martyrs slept, where my murdered husband and boy were entombed, the mobbers of Missouri, with an exquisite fiendishness, which no savages could have conceived, had constructed a rude privy. This they constantly used, with a delight which demons might have envied, if demons are more wicked and horribly beastly than were they." [^27]

What a great change one day had brought into the lives of numerous families. All the men, except two or three who had narrowly escaped, were dead or wounded. It hadn't been a battle, but a massacre, indeed! The survivors knew not at what moment they might be attacked again. For the next ten days mobbers with blackened faces came back every day, cursing and swearing and declaring that they would "kill that d—d old Mormon preacher." [^28]

Haun's Mill MassacreHCAmanda and Alma Smith—Examples of Great Faith and Courage

Haun's Mill MassacreHCAfter the firing was over and the Missourians had left, Amanda Smith returned to the mill and saw her eldest son, Willard, emerging from the blacksmith shop, carrying his little brother Alma. Amanda cried, "Oh, my Alma is dead.""No, Mother, I think Alma is not dead, but Father and brother Sardius are dead." [^29] What an appalling answer! But there was no time for tears now. Alma's entire hip bone was shot away. Amanda records:

Haun's Mill MassacreHCFlesh, hip bone, joint and all had been ploughed out from the muzzle of the gun, which the ruffian placed to the child's hip through the logs of the shop and deliberately fired.

Haun's Mill MassacreHCWe laid little Alma on a bed in our tent and I examined the wound. It was a ghastly sight. I knew not what to do. It was night nowÂ�.

Haun's Mill MassacreHCThe women were sobbing in the greatest anguish of spirit; the children were crying loudly with fear and grief at the loss of fathers and brothers; the dogs howled over their dead masters and the cattle were terrified with the scent of the blood of the murdered.

Haun's Mill MassacreHCYet was I there, all that long, dreadful night, with my dead and my wounded, and none but God as our physician and help.

Haun's Mill MassacreHCOh my Heavenly Father, I cried, what shall I do? Thou seest my poor wounded boy and knowest my inexperience. Oh, Heavenly Father, direct me what to do!

Haun's Mill MassacreHCAnd then I was directed as by a voice speaking to me.

Haun's Mill MassacreHCThe ashes of our fire was still smoldering. We had been burning the bark of the shag-bark hickory, I was directed to take those ashes and make a lye and put a cloth saturated with it right into the wound. It hurt, but little Alma was too near dead to heed it much. Again and again I saturated the cloth and put it into the hole from which the hip joint had been ploughed, and each time mashed flesh and splinters of bone came away with the cloth; and the wound became as white as chicken's flesh.

Haun's Mill MassacreHCHaving done as directed I again prayed to the Lord and was again instructed as distinctly as though a physician had been standing by speaking to me.

Haun's Mill MassacreHCNear by was a slippery-elm tree. From this I was told to make a slippery-elm poultice and fill the wound with it.

My eldest boy was sent to get the slippery-elm from the roots, the poultice was made, and the wound, which took fully a quarter of a yard of linen to cover, so large was it, was properly dressedÂ�.

I removed the wounded boy to a house, some distance off, the next day, and dressed his hip; the Lord directing me as before. I was reminded that in my husband's trunk there was a bottle of balsam. This I poured into the wound, greatly soothing Alma's pain.

"Alma, my child," I said, "you believe that the Lord made your hip?"

"Yes, mother."

"Well, the Lord can make something there in the place of your hip, don't you believe he can, Alma?"

"Do you think that the Lord can, mother?" inquired the child, in his simplicity.

"Yes, my son," I replied, "he has showed it all to me in a vision."

Then I laid him comfortably on his face, and said: "Now you lay like that, and don't move, and the Lord will make you another hip."

So Alma laid on his face for five weeks, until he was entirely recovered-a flexible gristle having grown in place of the missing joint and socket, which remains to this day a marvel to physicians.

On the day that he walked again I was out of the house fetching a bucket of water, when I heard screams from the children. Running back, in affright, I entered, and there was Alma on the floor, dancing around, and the children screaming in astonishment and joy.

It is now nearly forty years ago, but Alma has never been the least crippled during his life, and he has traveled quite a long period of the time as a missionary of the gospel and a living miracle of the power of God. [^30]

Amanda Smith was a great wife and mother, a woman with superior faith in God. After the massacre, in the depths of winter, she was forced to leave Missouri and travel hundreds of miles through snow, frost, mud, and storms. She recorded in her journal:

I started the first of February for the State of Illinois without money, mobbed all the way, I drove my own team slept out of doors. I had four small children, we suffered much with cold, hunger and fatigue, for what? For our religion, where in a "bossed" land of liberty-deny your faith or die, was the cryÂ�.

I felt the loss of my husband but not as I should as he had apostatized, he died in the faith and in hopes of a glorious resurrection. As for myself, I felt an unshaken confidence in God through it all. I had been personally acquainted with the Prophet Joseph for many years, and seen his walks and knew him to be a Prophet of God, that boyed me up under every trial and privation. [^31]

Amanda Smith was a noble woman, full of good works and Christ-like qualities. She stood firm in her faith in the gospel of Jesus Christ and rejoiced to see her children do likewise.

Haun's Mill MassacreHCNancy Jane Hammer—Example of Great Faith and Perseverance

Haun's Mill MassacreHC John Hammer, whose father Austin was murdered at Haun's Mill, records the plight of his family and the great faith and perseverance of his mother, Nancy Jane, after the Haun's Mill Massacre:

The death of my father left our family in a very helpless condition. It would have been an event sufficiently melancholy had he died at home of sickness where his family could have administered to his needs. But to have him cut down in his prime, and be torn from his family, ruthlessly and cruelly, so intensified the gloom which rested down upon our bereaved circle, that for a time it seemed that no ray of joy would ever be able to penetrate our bosoms again. Could we have been left unmolested to pass our season of grief, would have been a boon which we did not have the privilege of enjoyingÂ�. All the male membersÂ�had to be kept concealed. The moment the mob caught sight of them they were shot at. The women were not quite so closely hunted and they, by being extremely cautious, managed to convey food and water to their husbands and brothers.

My cousin and myself had to sleep in shocks of corn, or in bushes for two or three weeks, not daring to enter the house. The nights were cold and frosty which added to our affliction.

About three weeks after the massacre, the mob sent word to our people that we were all to leave the country within ten days, or we would be killedÂ�.

Our family had one wagon and one blind horse, and that blind horse had to transport our effects to the state of Illinois. Into this wagon we placed our clothing, bedding, some cornmeal and what scanty provisions we could muster, and started out into the cold on foot, to eat and sleep by the wayside with the canopy of Heaven for a coveringÂ�. Mothers and sisters were the only ones who had shoesÂ�. We all walked the entire distance, except Austin and Julia, for our horse could not carry a greater loadÂ�. There was scarcely a day while we were on the road, that it did not either snow or rain. The nights and mornings were very coldÂ�. It is a marvel to me to this day how we endured such fatigue without being disabled by sickness, if not death. But that merciful Being who sheltered us, and gave us courage took us to our destination.

My mother seemed to be endowed with fortitude and resolution and appeared to be inspired to devise ways and means whereby she could administer comforts to her suffering children, and keep them in good spirits. Her faith and confidence had ever been great in the Lord, but now when all this weight and responsibility had fallen upon her, with no husband to lean upon, she felt indeed that God was her greatest and best friend. She realized that He, and He alone must be the deliverer of herself and her family and conduct them to a people possessing the sympathies of humanity. [^32]

Nancy Jane Hammer loved the Lord, and the gospel of Jesus Christ was the center of her life. She lived and died full of faith in Christ and the doctrines of God. Her name should be included on the long list of other noble women in the Church who have been called on to play a similar role.

Conclusion

The Haun's Mill Massacre stands as "an evidence of the brutality and savagery of those who gathered themselves into armed mobs, under the guise of justice, to inflict upon a righteous people hardships, confiscation of property, suffering, and death; who sought for peace desiring to worship God and to live in accordance with the gospel of Jesus Christ that had been restored to earth again, through the instrumentality of the Prophet Joseph Smith." [^33] As expressed by Andrew Jenson, "Since the organization of the Church a large number of Saints suffered martyrdom for the truth's sake, but on no other occasion has so many faithful at one time been called to lay down their lives as at the Haun's Mill massacre, on the 30th of October, 1838." [^34]

Many lessons of great importance can be learned from this tragedy:

Reflection on the Haun's Mill Massacre gives us a glimpse of the struggles, afflictions, and pain which some of our ancestors experienced in building up The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter- day Saints in Missouri. Likewise, it gives us a view of the great faith in God, the devotion to the truth, and the tremendous courage and perseverance of many who had been inadvertently trapped in the Haun's Mill disaster. They were average, ordinary people striving to keep God's commandments. This helps build and solidify our testimonies and renew our courage to work harder, to keep our standards high, and to battle for the truth on all fronts.

Some of our faithful, devoted ancestors planted and some pruned that we may harvest. We must not fail!

[^28] Austin A. King was born in Tennessee. He came to Richmond in 1837 from Columbia, Missouri, where he had previously practiced law. Between 1837 and 1848, King served as judge if Missouri's fifth judicial circuit, consisting of the counties of Clinton, Ray, Caldwell, Clay, Platte, and Buchanan. He served as governor of Missouri from 1848 to 1852. In 1862 he was elected to the US Congress. See History of Ray County, Missouri (St. Louis: Missouri Historical Co., 1881), 259-61. Encyclopedia of the History of Missouri 3:537.
[^29] Alexander William Doniphan was a prominent Clay County lawyer beginning in 1833. Three times he represented Clay County in the Missouri general assembly. During the Mormon difficulties in 1838, Doniphan commanded the first brigade of David Atchison's third division of the Missouri militia. He later achieved distinction as an officer in the Mexican War. History of Ray County, Missouri, 498-502.
[^30] Amos Rees was the Clay County prosecuting attorney from 1831 to 1834 and in 1835 was one of the county's five practicing lawyers. From 1831 to 1837 Rees was attorney for Missouri's fifth judicial circuit. He later moved to Platte City, Missouri, and after that, to Fort Leavenworth, Kansas, where he was a prominent lawyer. He played a key role in the founding of Leavenworth as one of three trustees of the Town Company that parceled the land for settlement following the treaty with the Delaware Indians. H. Miles Moore, arly History of Leavenworth City and County ((Kansas: Sam'l Dodsworth, 1906), 23; W. H. Woodson, istory of Clay County Missouri ((Topeka: Historical Publishing, 1920), 330.
