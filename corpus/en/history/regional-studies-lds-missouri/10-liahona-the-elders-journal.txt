Various authors, Regional Studies in LDS History: Missouri ( 1994)

Liahona The Elders' Journal

Arnold K. Garr

When we consider the significance of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints in the state of Missouri, our thoughts naturally turn either to the historic occurrences that took place in the 1830s, or to the glorious events that are prophesied for the future. Yet some important episodes in the history of the Church in Missouri since the 1830s have gone largely unnoticed. One of these is the story of the Liahona the Elders' Journal.

The Liahona, as it was usually called, was the official publication for all the North American missions of the Church from 1907 to 1945. It arose from a merger of The Elders' Journal, published by the Southern States Mission from 1903 to 1907, and The Liahona, a multimission publication begun by the Central States Mission in Independence, Missouri on 6 April 1907.

When the journal was founded, it filled a publishing void which had existed in the LDS missions of America for almost a half-century. The Church had not published a periodical of significant tenure and circulation in the American missions since the Utah War in 1857. By the time The Liahona ceased publication in 1945, it had been in existence longer than any periodical ever published in the Church's American missions. In its prime it had a circulation of about 20,000 readers, and from 1907 to 1917 it was the only mission periodical the Church published in America. After 1917, even though several missions began publishing their own distinct bulletins, The Liahona remained the only mission publication to serve all the American missions until 1945. The journal will be "a great help to missionaries," the First Presidency declared in 1907, "and will add to the value of any man's library." [^1] In fact, the periodical assisted in the conversion process on many occasions, and in some cases it was even the primary instrument of conversion. The purpose of this paper is to recover the memory of The Liahona's outstanding contributions by reviewing the history of its publication.

At the beginning of the twentieth century, when The Liahona first appeared, Protestants, Catholics, Jews, and Latter-day Saints were all attempting to adjust to important trends in society that affected their respective histories. The Protestants were divided over fundamentalism, the Catholics were struggling with Americanizing their church, and the Jews were quibbling over their position on zionism. In the meantime, the LDS Church was redefining its position on such issues as plural marriage and its role in politics. At the same time, the Church was striving diligently to pay off the debt which the severe antipolygamy crusade had created.

Altogether these religious denominations published almost a thousand different periodicals in America. However, "less than ten percent had circulations of over ten thousand." [^2] Churches used these magazines to promote their religious and political views and to attack the tenets of other denominations. Frank Mott, in A History of American Magazines, observes that because "religious editors themselves were not inclined to be gentle with each other, their lot was not, on the whole, a quiet one." [^3] (Speaking of Protestant periodicals, Martin Marty adds, "For the most part they were polemic and proselytizing in character, tending to downgrade other Christian denominations. Long battles over religious viewpoints (that is, baptism of infants, immersion) were waged between them." [^4] As time passed, some periodicals became more moderate, but the religious press was "still a convenient tool for hate mongers." [^5] It was in such a theologically charged setting that he Liahona ade its appearance.

The time was ripe for the success of this type of periodical because the LDS missions of North America had finally stabilized to the point where they could support their own publication. Before 1875, the Church had established three formal missions in the United States, but these were loosely structured and often did not function. However, during the last quarter of the nineteenth century, the Church founded or reopened seven missions in America. This development, coupled with the Church's deemphasis on migration to Utah, helped give its American missions the strength and stability they needed to support their own periodical. Additionally, important changes were occurring in Church publishing. With the death of George Q. Cannon in 1901, the Church acquired his publishing company in Salt Lake City and renamed it the Deseret Book Company. Also after the 1880s, Liverpool, England began to decline as the main publishing location for LDS literature; for example, the prestigious LDS Millennial Star gradually decreased in influence to become a local mission publication for England. Thus the Church was entering a period of decentralization in publishing.

The Founding

The missions of the United States needed a publication because they covered vast geographic areas at a time when other means of communication, such as the telephone, were a rarity, especially in rural areas. For example, the Southern States Mission comprised ten states, "extending from the Great Lakes on the north to the Gulf [of Mexico] on the South, and from the Mississippi on the west to the Atlantic Ocean on the east." [^6] Ben E. Rich, president of that mission, claimed there was probably not another mission in the Church that required "so much travel and such lengthy trips" from its presiding authority. Proselyting in the South at that time was done mostly in the country, and the members often lived so far from railroads and each other that it was "barely possible to get a sufficient number of saints together to formulate a good, healthy branch." Rich further claimed that his scant 200 missionaries could barely keep in touch with the 8,729 members scattered throughout the South. He felt the mission was "well nigh ungovernable without some effective means of communicating." [^7]

In response to this need, President Rich published the first number of The Elders' Journal (a sort of missionary newsletter for the Southern States Mission) in August of 1903. The following statement outlined its purpose:

The "Elders' Journal" will contain a synopsis of the work done each month by the different conferences; the names of all Elders arriving from Zion, and their assignments to their fields of labor; notice of releases and appointments; instructions to the Elders from the Authorities in Zion and from the Presidency of the mission; and all items of interest connected with the work that will be beneficial to the missionaries, and that will have a tendency to draw us all closer together. [^8]

It also contained brief articles on doctrine written by leaders of the Church. During its first year, the periodical was small in size (4 1/2" by 7 3/4"), and issued monthly. By the end of the first volume, the Journal claimed to have 1200 subscribers [^9] yet the mission only had about 200 missionaries. [^10] Many members as well as missionaries purchased the periodical. Members and missionaries alike felt isolated and were starving for some kind of communication within the mission. And the magazine experienced considerable growing pains as it attempted to respond to these various needs.

The president reacted to the positive response of the members by announcing that the second volume of the Journal would be larger, something like the format of The LDS Millennial Star, in order to include more articles that might be of interest to the members. [^11] This volume, which was six by nine inches in size, was issued bimonthly and had a more professional appearance.

By the beginning of its second year, the Journal had changed its format and frequency, and it had already expanded from a small circular intended primarily for the use of missionaries to a much larger periodical geared also to members. Later, when it merged with The Liahona in 1907, it would also reach out to investigators.

The first home of the Journal was at the headquarters of the Southern States Mission in Atlanta, Georgia. The mission office had moved to Atlanta in 1902, the year the Middle States Mission was created. [^12] Before that, mission headquarters had been in Chattanooga, Tennessee, which became part of the Middle States Mission as a result of the division. The Church probably moved the office to Atlanta because it was centrally located within the newly formed boundaries of the Southern States Mission.

The home of the mission and the Journal was not in Atlanta very long, however, because President Rich was planning to relocate. Soon after the missions were reunited, Rich bought some property in Chattanooga for a new mission home, [^13] and in August 1904 he moved the headquarters of both the mission and the Journal back to Tennessee. President Rich made the move because "he longed to return to Tennessee and the friendship and loyalty of the Chattanooga businessmen which he had won" [^14] when he lived there before. Also, with the expansion of the mission, Chattanooga once again became the geographic center of the mission.

Consequently, the first volume of the Journal was published in Atlanta, but volumes two, three, and four were published in Chattanooga. The magazine moved to Independence, Missouri, its third and final city, when it merged with The Liahona in 1907.

In examining the four years of the Journal before its merger, it seems evident that Ben E. Rich had accomplished something that no one else had ever done. He established a mission magazine in the United States that would not only endure, but flourish. Before President Rich, the success rate of mission publications in the United States was not very impressive. Several very capable people, including John Taylor, George Q. Cannon, Parley and Orson Pratt, and Erastus Snow, attempted to establish periodicals in the American missions, but none of those publications remained in existence more than two years. [^15] However, by the time the Journal consolidated with The Liahona, it had already existed for four years and was thriving. Altogether, the magazine was issued for 42 years, longer than any other Mormon mission periodical published in the American missions. Of all the Church's English speaking periodicals published in the United States, only five had a longer tenure than The Liahona, and they were all published at Church headquarters in Salt Lake City: The Deseret News, The Instructor, The Improvement Era, The Children's Friend, and the Relief Society Magazine.

The Merger and a New Name

The year 1907 was a critical one for the Journal. In that year it lost its identity as a distinct publication of the Southern States Mission and merged with a new magazine, published in Independence, Missouri, called The Liahona. The amalgamated magazine, Liahona The Elders' Journal, became the organ for all the LDS missions in America.

When the Journal began its fourth year of publication in the summer of 1906, few would have thought that it would merge with another magazine at the conclusion of that year's volume. In 1907 it was the only LDS mission magazine published in the United States, and it was approaching the peak of its popularity and prestige as a distinct publication of the Southern States Mission. During its first four years, circulation had grown from 200 to 6,000 subscribers. [^16]

The total number of subscribers was impressive, but the ratio of subscribers living outside the Southern States Mission was also significant. In 1907, the Journal had over 2,500 subscribers in the Southern States Mission, but nearly 3,200 outside the mission. [^17] This remarkable response indicated that there were obviously many missionaries and members in other American missions, not just the Southern States, who were thirsting for a periodical published in the mission field for those who lived and served there.

Inasmuch as the Journal was the only LDS mission magazine in America and since its popularity was increasing so rapidly, one might expect that it would simply expand to become the official organ for all the American missions. Ben E. Rich certainly had this aspiration, but the course of events turned in a different direction.

The success of the Journal impressed other mission presidents, [^18] and soon they began making plans for a magazine to be published in behalf of all the other missions of America.

In November of 1906, President Joseph A. McRae of the Colorado Mission (later named the Western States Mission), and President German E. Ellsworth of the Northern States Mission met in Kansas City with President Samuel O. Bennion of the Central States Mission and discussed the proposition that the various missions join together to print a paper. They wrote a formal proposal to the First Presidency of the Church, and President McRae "was authorized to take the matter up with the brethren in Zion." [^19]

In December the same three presidents met again, this time in Chicago, to discuss further plans for the paper. They organized an advisory board for the magazine composed of all the mission presidents in America, except Ben E. Rich of the Southern States Mission. The members of the board, in addition to Presidents Bennion, McRae, and Ellsworth, were President Joseph E. Robinson of the California Mission, President Nephi Pratt of the Northwestern States Mission, and President John G. McQuarrie of the Eastern States Mission. They selected Bennion as president of the board and Ellsworth as secretary and treasurer. In addition, the First Presidency called Benjamin F. Cummings of Salt Lake City to serve a two year mission as its editor. [^20]

Cummings and members of the advisory board suggested that the new paper be called The Liahona. When Cummings presented the idea to the First Presidency of the Church, President Joseph F. Smith remarked "it was a singular name for a paper." Cummings explained that the title was chosen because they thought "it would impel a person to whom an Elder might hand a copy to ask what it meant." This they hoped would lead to a discussion of the Book of Mormon, from which the name was taken. President Smith said he liked the name and believed it a very good one for their purpose. [^21] The advisory board also determined that the place of publication would be Independence, Missouri, the new headquarters of the Central States Mission.

Unfortunately, those involved in the decision to launch The Liahona did so without consulting Ben E. Rich-a situation that led to a certain amount of tension for a time. When President Rich first learned of a competing mission publication, he expressed his disappointment and frustration to John McQuarrie, president of the Eastern States Mission:

If I were not interested personally I would ask the questions: Why is another missionary paper needed?Â�

I begin to think that someone should tell me of the great mistake I am making in trying to publish a missionary paper, or else it should be made the official missionary paper of the ChurchÂ�. Why is it not better to get behind the paper we now have, instead of trying to make something new? [^22]

Perhaps Rich dreaded most the possibility of consolidation, especially under a new name. In a fit of pique he resorted to hyperbole:

If we will change its name, its residence, its shape, and fill it with advertisements, and make it like the "Salvation Army War Cry," why then I am perfectly willing, upon the advice of the Authorities, to execute the little memory we have erected to the Prophet Joseph Smith, roll up my sleeves and help to build a monument to the memory of General Booth of the Salvation Army? [^23]

In spite of this outburst, Rich would have to yield to consolidation in less than two months. But before the Brethren officially asked him to do so, he carried out a brief, rather impressive, yet ineffectual campaign in behalf of his Journal. In the 1 March 1907 issue of the magazine, he published endorsements (solicited almost a year before) together with a handsome picture of literally every living general authority in the Church. He buttressed this with the endorsements of many mission and stake presidents.

Notwithstanding President Rich's promotional campaign in behalf of The Elders Journal, on 6 April 1907 The Liahona rolled off the press in Independence, Missouri, with a hearty endorsement from the First Presidency of the Church.

As circumstances developed, however, two separate mission magazines did not last for long. As the first issue of The Liahona appeared in Independence, the Saints had gathered in Salt Lake City for April conference. On the agenda for a conference of the mission presidents in the United States and Mexico, the First Presidency and Council of the Twelve planned to discuss the advisability of consolidating the two magazines. After several meetings, the Brethren had decided that the Church realistically could not support both magazines, and "that it would be for the best good of all the interests involved for [consolidation] to take place at an early date." [^24]

During the conference, they also decided upon a new name for the periodical. Ben E. Rich, of course, wanted the presidents to retain the name The Elders' Journal. On the other hand, the presidents who founded The Liahona wanted to retain that name. [^25] The First Presidency asked the Council of the Twelve and mission presidents to make the decision. In an effort to compromise, they suggested the consolidated and somewhat unwieldy name of Liahona The Elders' Journal. Though Joseph F. Smith would later express a desire to see the paper named The Elders' Journal, when he was reminded that the Twelve and presidents had decided the matter, he said that "if it had already been settled he would not interfere." [^26]

In retrospect, it appears that the decision to merge the two magazines was, in fact, "for the best good of all the interests involved." [^27] The General Authorities and mission presidents chose to consolidate because the Church simply could not support two mission periodicals. Ben E. Rich, as much as he originally dreaded the idea of consolidation, even admitted that his Journal would have died slowly through years of competition.

From the time of the merger, in 1907, until 1919, Liahona The Elders' Journal (usually shortened to The Liahona) was issued weekly at a dollar per year. Thereafter it was issued bi-weekly at the same price. The subscription fees, however, never fully paid the cost of printing, and therefore the Church was obliged to give financial assistance to the magazine.

Inasmuch as The Liahona had a multi-mission concept, it had a unique organizational structure. The editors answered to an advisory board composed of the mission presidents in North America. The advisory board, in turn, answered to the General Authorities of the Church, particularly the First Presidency. People at each level made decisions which had an impact on the magazine, but the First Presidency wielded the most influence over the general policies of the publication.

The Contents

Throughout its history, The Liahona attempted at least thirteen different regular features in an effort to meet the needs of its readers. Four of these features endured for decades, regardless of who the editors were. They were the editorial column, "News from the Missions," discourses by general authorities, and poetry. The rest of the regular columns lived and died with the editors who created them, usually lasting two years or less.

The editorials, more than any other regular column, served as a barometer for the opinions, attitudes, and interests of The Liahona's editors and publishers. Ben E. Rich began writing editorial comments in the first volume of the periodical, and by the second volume he established the practice of writing a distinct editorial column for every issue. This practice continued throughout the history of the magazine. Altogether, The Liahona published over 1,670 editorials, many of which fell into four broad categories: first, those which responded to anti-Mormon activities; second, those concerned with national and world events; third, editorials about missionary work; and fourth, editorials dealing with doctrine.

During the early years of the magazine's history, the editors wrote numerous editorials that responded to specific anti-Mormon activities. This was a dominant theme for editors Ben E. Rich, Benjamin F. Cummings, and to a lesser extent, Nephi Anderson. From 1904 to 1910, while these three men served as editors, ten percent of all editorials were responses to anti-Mormon activities. Ben E. Rich was the most active in this regard. In volumes two, three, and four, 18 percent of Rich's editorials were replies to anti-Mormons. However, in 1910, during a time when the Church was making an effort to improve its public image, The Liahona ceased publishing rebuttals to anti-Mormons.

The magazine's editorials pertaining to national and world events favored zionism, total prohibition, and Roosevelt's New Deal. However, they were not sympathetic to the theory of evolution. This theory was an explosive issue in the early twentieth century that literally split American Protestantism into two broad camps-Fundamentalists and Modernists. Fundamentalists were against evolution because they felt it was a threat to the biblical account of the creation, and a basic doctrine of fundamentalism was inerrancy of the Bible.

It is quite evident that The Liahona was not sympathetic to evolution. At the death of William Jennings Bryan, an outspoken critic of evolution, Hugh Ireland wrote an editorial in the 11 August 1925 edition of The Liahona entitled "The Passing of Mr. Bryan." Ireland wrote that Bryan deserved "to be held in honor by all who accept the Bible as the revealed word of God." [^28] [^29] He then quoted ten different scholars who oppose the theory. Altogether Ireland published at least six different editorials, from 1925 to 1934, that dealt with evolution, with none of them sympathetic to the theory.

In spite of the fact that The Liahona had little sympathy for evolution, it would be a mistake to classify the magazine with traditional fundamentalist periodicals. For one thing, the LDS Church's eighth article of faith states that the Bible is the word of God only "as far as it is translated correctly." The Liahona published this article of faith verbatim along with the other Articles of Faith in nearly every issue of the magazine, for a two-year period, as part of a regular column by the same name. This doctrine is, of course, in direct opposition to the fundamentalist belief of biblical inerrancy. For this reason, among others, neither the protestant fundamentalists nor the Latter-day Saints wanted The Liahona to be classified as a traditional fundamentalist publication.

In many ways The Liahona serves as a valuable source for LDS mission history. For example, in the September 1903 issue, editor Ben E. Rich published the "Rules and Regulations" for the Southern States Mission. Rule six, on finances, stated that all money received by the elders should go through the mission office so that "their record may show how much money is being spent by them." [^30] Rule eight, on clothing, said that straw hats could be worn in the summer but derby hats were recommended for winter. [^31] Rule ten, on temple work, reminded the missionaries that the President of the Church was the only one who could "give a recommend to the Temple, outside of the regularly organized wards." [^32] Later in the same issue, Rich explained that "recommends for saints immigrating to the west must be signed by conference presidents and countersigned by the mission president." [^33] These are just a few examples of how the magazine can help the historian better understand the policies and procedures of the missions during the early part of the twentieth century.

Perhaps the most unique, long-running feature of The Liahona was the "News from the Missions." Other Church periodicals printed editorial columns, reprinted discourses by General Authorities, and often published poetry. The Liahona, however, was the only Church publication that regularly provided a significant amount of space, over a long period of time, for news from the American missions.

Benjamin F. Cummings established the "News from the Missions" in 1907 when The Liahona and The Elders' Journal merged. Its purpose was to publish narrative reports from all the missions in America. Cummings initially named the column "Mission News," but during volume six he changed the name to "The Missions." Then on 14 April 1914, Joseph Cardon renamed the column "News from the Missions," by which it would be known until 1939, when Ezra Marler discontinued the general heading "News from the Missions" and created a distinct column for each contributing mission in America. The Liahona, therefore, published the column continuously from 1907 to 1939. Thus it had the distinction of being the magazine's longest running regular department, with the exception of the editorial column.

From a strictly literary point of view "News from the Missions" would not get very high marks because it was written by amateurs. From a social standpoint, however, the column helped fill an important need, especially in the early part of the century. Before World War I, members and missionaries in the American missions were relatively few and far between. Furthermore, some of the important means of transportation and communication, that we take for granted today, were in their infancy or non-existent. During this same time the individual missions did not publish their own bulletins, and Church periodicals published in Salt Lake City gave little attention to the missions. Under these conditions the people in the mission field often hungered for association with their brothers and sisters living in similar circumstances. The "News from the Missions" helped satisfy this hunger in a small way. Through this column the missionaries and members could share conversion stories, incidents of being healed by priesthood blessings, and other faith promoting experiences. To take liberties with a famous expression, this was the column that was "of the missions, by the missions, and for the missions." More than any other regular feature, the "News from the Missions" gave members in the mission field a feeling of community.

Perhaps the magazine which The Liahona is most comparable to is The LDS Millennial Star, published by the British Mission in Liverpool, England, from 1840-1970. In fact, in the first volume of The Elders' Journal (1903-1904) the editor stated that he intended to pattern the new American mission periodical after The LDS Millennial Star. [^34] This was only natural inasmuch as the Star had been such a successful magazine and was the only LDS mission periodical in the English language in existence at the time. Ultimately The Liahona would serve the North American missions in the same manner The LDS Millennial Star was serving the British Mission, with a narrower, geographical focus. Each carried many of the same kinds of regular features, including an editorial column, reprinted discourses by General Authorities, poetry, and a column that published news from the mission field. While the Star published news from the various districts in the British Mission, The Liahona published news from the several missions in America. Actually The Liahona might be considered the American counterpart to The LDS Millennial Star with an additional multi-mission dimension. Furthermore, Independence, Missouri (the home of The Liahona as well as Zion's Printing and Publishing Company) would also become a significant center for Church publishing in America during the twentieth century, just as Liverpool, England (the home of the Star), was so important to Mormon publishing in the nineteenth century. [^35]

The Influence of The Liahona

The Liahona enjoyed considerable influence during its 42 year lifetime. It reached a circulation of approximately 20,000 and was a worthwhile aid to missionary work as well as serving as a source of edification and comfort to both missionaries and members.

In 1907 Elder Charles Penrose, a member of the Quorum of Twelve, said the magazine met "one of the needs of the Church" by filling "a place heretofore vacant, at least for many years." [^36] [^37] Elder Orson F. Whitney believed the publication was "in the front rank of periodicals issued in the interest of the Church." He further commented that he "always read it with pleasure and profit" and that it had his "unqualified endorsement." [^38] Patriarch John Smith considered it "a paper of extraordinary value," [^39] and Charles H. Hart, of the First Council of the Seventy, believed the magazine was "of inestimable value to the Saints in general." [^40] Nephi Pratt, president of the Northwestern States Mission, wrote in 1907, "In all my reading, extending over a period of fifty years, I do not remember a periodical of greater value to the missionary Elder than your excellent little JOURNALÂ�. This little JOURNAL deserves almost first place in all our mission fields." [^41]

On one occasion, editor Benjamin F. Cummings stated, "Conference presidents have expressed the opinion that the aid of this magazine has doubled the results of the labors of their elders." [^42] [^43] Then in 1912, editor Joseph A. McRae affirmed, "Scores of letters have been received from investigators telling us that their conversion to the Truth was the result of their reading iahona The Elders' Journal." [^44] A good example of this was a letter sent by Elizabeth P. Aikele in which she declared, "I never heard an Elder preach, nor attended a Mormon meeting, nor heard a Mormon sermon until after I had been baptized. I was converted by reading The Liahona." [^45]

The magazine was also a lifeline of communication to the scattered Saints who lived in the American mission field, especially before World War I. In 1912, Editor Joseph A. McRae noted that the periodical went "to the homes of thousands of Saints who [were] isolated from the body of the church, and who [needed] spiritual food." [^46] A subscriber from Georgia wrote, "I had rather be without bread than THE JOURNAL, if there was a way to sustain life, for the dear little JOURNAL is food for the soul." [^47] E. B. Rucker of Windy, Virginia once sent in money for a new subscriber and said, "I have got the promise of others. After once reading it they would not be without it for anything. It is the next thing to a visit from the Elders." [^48] [^49] A sister from Windsor, South Carolina once said of the magazine, "There are no other Saints here so I value its worth more than I can tell you." [^50] Finally, Sister N. C. Stanfield of Adamsville, Tennessee, declared, "I cannot do without THE JOURNAL. We look for its coming as a welcome and glorious visitor." [^51]

However, with the passing of time, as conditions changed, The Liahona's influence gradually decreased. Duplication with new periodicals such as the Church News and the individual mission bulletins, coupled with financial problems, eventually led to its demise in 1945. Yet in its day it served the missions of North America well and played an important part in the history of Mormon periodicals. Even after its discontinuance, the magazine remains a valuable source of information for those interested in the history of the Church in the United States and Canada during the first half of the twentieth century.

But I hope there are better days to come to us yet" (Emma Smith to Joseph Smith, 7 March 1839, Original in LDS Historical Department, Salt Lake City). Joseph's two letters to Emma reflected his concern for her condition both physically and spiritually. He encouraged, praised, and challenged her to endure until he could be with her.
