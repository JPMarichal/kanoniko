Various authors, Regional Studies in LDS History: Missouri ( 1994)

Missouri and the Redemption of Zion: A Setting for Conflict

John L. Fowles

The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints has had a longer association with Missouri, or the land of Zion, than many people realize. Early in the restoration movement Joseph Smith received specific information regarding a location for Zion, and a condition of righteousness among mankind which all previous prophets had tried to achieve (D&C 45:12). In December of 1830, Joseph Smith received a revelation concerning Enoch which was not found in the Bible (Moses 7). It notes that Enoch had built a city that was called the "City of Holiness, even Zion" (Moses 7:19). The Prophet recorded Enoch's preaching and prophesying regarding a future time in the earth's history when Zion would again stand upon the earth. The Lord was to gather the elect from the four quarters of the earth to a place which he would prepare, a place where people would be looking for His coming, and it would be called "Zion, a New Jerusalem" (Moses 7:62).

When the Book of Mormon was published, it also contained information concerning the New Jerusalem. Ether saw that Zion would be built upon the American continent (Ether 13:8; see also Article of Faith, No. 10). It would be the place for the gathering of Joseph's descendants, a land of inheritance which would be a holy city unto the Lord, separated from the Old Jerusalem (Ether 13:8). Jesus Christ, during his ministry on the American continent, also prophesied that this land would be given to the remnants of Jacob for their inheritance, including the place for the building of the city called the New Jerusalem (3 Ne. 21:22-23).

In September of 1830, Hiram Page predicted where the city of Zion would be located. However, the Lord revealed to Joseph Smith that no one knew where it would be built at this time, but that "it shall be on the borders of the Lamanites" (D&C 28:9). In February of 1831, the Prophet received another revelation that said that the New Jerusalem would be prepared, and the designated spot would thereafter be revealed in the Lord's own due time (D&C 42:9, 35-36, 62). Later in February, a revelation announced that the Saints needed to repent, and become one in mind, to gather up their riches to purchase an inheritance "which shall hereafter be appointed unto you" (D&C 45:65). In beautiful language the Lord said:

And it shall be called the New Jerusalem, a land of peace, a city of refuge, a place of safety for the saints of the Most High GodÂ�. And now I say unto you, keep these things from going abroad unto the world until it is expedient in me, that ye may accomplish this work in the eyes of the people, and in the eyes of your enemies, that they may not know your works until ye have accomplished the thing which I have commanded you. (D&C 45:66, 72)

During 1831-1834 the Lord continued to reveal the necessity of gathering to Zion, purchasing land, and beginning to build the kingdom of God, including the city of New Jerusalem. Echoing the February 1831 revelation, the Lord four months later called Missouri a land of the enemy (D&C 52:42). Thus, a setting of conflict was foreseen by the Lord. Still he said he would "hasten the city in its time" and "crown the faithful with joy and with rejoicing" (D&C 52:43). These revelations motivated the Latter-day Saints to migrate to Missouri to purchase a land of inheritance, even though the real potential for tension and conflict with the Missourians lingered and eventually ensued.

First, to explain the Latter-day Saints' stay in Missouri from 1831 to 1838 and the future redemption of Zion more fully, this paper will briefly consider the major historical events prior to Missouri becoming a state in 1821 and the first Mormon mission in 1831. Second, it will note early descriptions of the physical landscape of Missouri. Third, it will discuss the larger historical setting of Jacksonian America in which the Mormons found themselves. Fourth, it will analyze the cultural and religious differences between the rapidly gathering Latter-day Saints and the early Missourians on the western frontier. Finally, this report will briefly present other "historical" revelations given to the Prophet Joseph Smith during this period which influenced the Saint's ideologies concerning Zion and spoke of the future redemption of the New Jerusalem.

Historical Overview

In 1673 the French explorers, Marquette and Joliet, discovered the mouth of the Missouri River. The Osage and Fox Indians inhabited Missouri during this time, controlling the land until 1682. In that same year Sieur de La Salle, another French explorer, claimed the whole Mississippi Valley for France as he floated the Mississippi, naming it "Louisiana." French traders and trappers began to establish trading posts all along the river. [^1]

In 1700, Jesuit missionaries established the first white settlement in Missouri, the Mission of St. Francis Xavier, near present day St. Louis. In 1762, France, in a secret deal with its ally Spain, gave up all its territory east of the Mississippi River during what was called the Seven Year War. Shortly thereafter, in 1763, England gained control of this early frontier at the conclusion of the French-Indian War, except for St. Louis, which was still controlled by Spain during the Revolutionary War. In 1800, Daniel Boone, the famous frontiersman, was granted land in Missouri by the Spanish. However, Missouri shifted hands again when Napoleon forced Spain to relinquish the area west of the Mississippi the same year. [^2]

By the early 1800s, Missouri's population had grown to 6,915. The United States obtained Missouri when Jefferson purchased the entire area of Louisiana in 1803 just two years prior to Joseph Smith's birth. Twenty-five years later, the Prophet Joseph Smith would play a major role in attempting to build Zion on Missouri's soil. Jefferson expected the Louisiana Territory to be an outlet for surplus Americans for many decades. Missouri was a perfect starting place for pack trains moving westward on the Santa Fe Trail. The population continued to grow rapidly to approximately 10,000 in 1804, comprising about 15% slaves, 43% French and 56% Americans. [^3]

In 1810, the population had nearly doubled to 19,783. [^4] Congress granted territorial status to Missouri in 1812. In 1818, Missouri applied for statehood, and a controversy arose over slavery. If Missouri were allowed into the Union as a slave state, it would create an imbalance in favor of slavery, as the Union then had exactly the same number of free and slave states at the time. Later, the way was created for Missouri to become a state when Maine also applied for admission as a free state in 1820. This provided the opportunity for the so-called Missouri Compromise, and on 10 August 1821, Missouri became the 24th state of the union.

Emigrants continued to pour into Missouri even before statehood. By 1820 the population was estimated at 66,586 with about a 15% slave population. [^5] The western-most outpost, called Independence, was settled in 1827, just six years following Becknell's first Santa Fe trading experiment. Independence was selected because there was a permanent place for a landing on the Missouri River that steamboats could reach. Traders and farmers came rapidly as the town began to grow. [^6]

By the end of 1830, Missouri had been controlled by a variety of nations, including the original French inhabitants, the Spanish, and other foreigners from England, Ireland, Scotland, and Wales. Finally, Americans came to the western frontier to start a new life. In 1830 the population had grown to 140,455. [^7] As will be discussed below, the Missouri settlers in the early 1800s came mainly from the southern states of Tennessee and Kentucky, and their culture differed widely from that of the first Mormons to arrive. These were some of the demographics of Missouri when the Mormon missionaries first arrived in Independence in 1831.

Land of Zion

The western counties of Missouri have been described by early visitors to the area. Charles Latrobe told of the farms and houses he visited in 1832. According to John Francis McDermott, Latrobe reported in specific terms the physical conditions at the time he visited Independence. Dwelling houses had a wide open space in the middle covered by a common roof. Some farms had guest quarters with three to four beds. Like other southern states, slave quarters were separate, including kitchen quarters in the back. Wildlife was plentiful in this new area. According to Latrobe, women had plenty of ham, chicken, butter, and eggs which they could fix in a half hour. [^8] Latrobe's observations are helpful in understanding the environmental characteristics, local customs, and other social conditions of early Missouri life.

During the summer of 1831, the Prophet Joseph Smith's party journeyed from Kirtland to St. Louis and walked to Independence, arriving in the middle of July. [^9] W. W. Phelps, one of the members of the group, who had recently joined the Church and had been the editor of the "Ontario Phoenix" in New York, gave an account of the trip to Jackson County including his own cultural analysis of the Missourians. [^10] According to Phelps, Missouri was about 2/3 prairie and 1/3 timber. A variety of trees existed including oak, hickory, walnut, bass wood, cotton wood, locust, and bean. The soil was rich black mould about three to eight feet deep. Prairie hens, turkeys, rabbits, deer, rattlesnakes, and wolves inhabited the land. Phelps said, "The people are proverbially idle or lazy, and mostly ignorant; reckoning nobody equal to themselves in many respects." [^11] Levi Hancock also gave a detailed description of this western frontier in his missionary journal. He said the houses were scattered as they preached by the firesides. The Missourians had dogs, horses, cows, pigs, and chickens in abundance around their homes. Children were ragged and dirty and lived on corn pudding, bacon, and sassafras tea. [^12]

Joseph Smith received a revelation directing Sidney Rigdon to write a "description of the land of Zion" (D&C 58:50). John Whitmer's history contains a first attempt by Rigdon to follow that directive:

This land being situated in the center of the continent on which we dwell, with an exceeding fertile soil and ready cleared for the hand of the cultivator, bespeaks the goodness of our God in providing so goodly a heritage,Â�Indeed I may say that the whole properties of the country invite the Saints to come and partake their blessings. But what more need I say about a country which our heavenly Father holds in his own hands, for if it were unhealthy he could make it healthy, and if barren he could make it fruitful. [^13]

Later, Joseph Smith, in the official account published in the Times and Seasons, remembered that they had received a commandment for the eloquent Sidney Rigdon to write a description of the land of Zion from the best information they had at the time. Richard Anderson has observed that inasmuch as Joseph Smith would publish this description at a time when Rigdon's influence in the Church was waning demonstrates that the Prophet fully approved of the wording of the document. [^14] This description of the land of Zion, along with the many revelations Joseph Smith received concerning the inheritances waiting for the Saints in Missouri, motivated them to sell all and come to Zion. Western Missouri was also a place where other Americans were gathering in the spirit of Jacksonian America.

Jacksonian America

Andrew Jackson, president of the United States from 1829-1837, for whom Jackson County, Missouri, was named, symbolized the rise of the new west and the triumph of the common man, otherwise known as Jacksonian democracy. The importance of this larger contextual influence in America at the time, especially in the new frontier, has been increasingly acknowledged by historians. For example, R. J. Robertson, Jr. stated: "Even the most cursory examination of the Mormon experience in Missouri reveals wide differences between the Mormon and non-Mormon forms of social organization in the 1830s. An intensive investigation reveals that the two societies were in opposition, and that some form of conflict between the two was virtually inevitable, especially when considered in the larger context of Jacksonian America." [^15] [^16]

Another tradition in America closely associated with Jacksonian democracy has been defined as "extralegal violence." Stephen C. LeSueur described the existing social tension and confrontation between the Mormons and the Missourians during the Mormon War of 1838:

The confrontations between the Mormons and their Missouri neighbors vividly illustrate the powerful cultural forces that have fostered a tradition of extralegal violence in America. Since colonial times, when impassioned citizens tarred and feathered tax collectors, dumped English tea into Boston harbor, and declared their independence from Great Britain, Americans have claimed the right to take the law into their own hands to enforce justice. Such violence has generally been conservative in purpose, and thus supported or tolerated by a large portion of the population. Vigilante organizations, often led by members of the local elite, acted to preserve established customs and practices against persons and groups that were perceived as a threat to society. [^17]

LeSueur reports "extralegal violence" on both sides during the Mormon War. [^18]

Another Mormon scholar has described the Mormon story as a flight from American religious pluralism or institutional religious diversity, which was actually a sub-theme of Jacksonian America. Marvin Hill states his thesis as follows:

Thus the prophet spoke of the deep Mormon aversion to religious institutional diversity, which I term here religious pluralism. But in 1834 Oliver Cowdery, Smith's close friend and co-worker in the Mormon kingdom, remembered in the young church's "Messenger and Advocate" that this antipathy extended beyond sectarian rivalry to all voluntary associations in America and predicted their impending doom: `Certain the Gentile world, with all its parties, sects, denominations, reformations, revivals of religion, societies and associations, are devoted to destruction'Â�The Latter-day Saints saw the maelstrom of competing faiths and social institutions as evidence of social upheaval and found confirmation in the rioting and violence that characterized Jacksonian America. [^19]

Hill also argues that the Book of Mormon itself was anti-pluralistic as the Nephites tried to sustain and support a theocratic government which eventually succumbed to pluralism, materialism, and lack of belief in God by the greater Nephite-Lamanite society.

The events of the Latter-day Saints in Missouri can be viewed against the larger backdrop of Jacksonian America. Many of the Mormon cultural and religious beliefs came into conflict with the mores of sectarian groups and other religious denominational beliefs which aligned themselves with the spirit of Jacksonian democracy. These factors help explain why the Mormons met with so much resistance as they moved west to build the city of Zion.

Cultural and Religious Differences

Most Missourians who had settled in western Missouri in the early 1800s were from the South, especially from Kentucky and Tennessee. Their behavior reflected their hero president, Andrew Jackson. Hattie M. Anderson described the emigrants to western Missouri as tough and aggressive:

These were the type of people who followed Boone into Kentucky and who possessed the usual characteristics of the pioneer which enabled them to adapt themselves to a frontier environment. They were independent, liberty-loving, democratic, shrewd, courageous, enthusiastic, aggressive, frugal, contentious, unceremonious in religion, honest, versatile, and had a high faith in themselves. [^20]

These settlers had a firm belief in the importance of individualism and their ability to seek their own way. However, the Mormon emigrants viewed themselves as God's chosen people. God had set aside the western boundaries of Missouri for a Mormon Zion. The Missouri settlers saw the Saints as a cohesive, anti-individualistic society with a closed economic system. The Latter-day Saints were not typical of Jacksonian Americans as were their Missouri counterparts; they were extremely loyal to one another and their leaders. In addition, most of the Mormons came from the North or Canada, bringing radically different cultural mores.

The Missourians also viewed the coming of the Mormons as a political threat because of their purchases of large tracts of land in the area. By 1830, some 1,200 Mormons had come to Jackson County, comprising about 30% of the population. Richard Bushman, after listing the differences between the Missourians and the Mormons, concluded that the major source of conflict was the political fears of the Missourians:

There was real substance in the settlers' fears of Mormon domination. The Latter-day Saints themselves believed Mormon and gentile societies were incompatible. The revelations to Joseph Smith identified Jackson as Zion, and Zion was to be the abode of the righteous only. If the gentiles did not leave, the Saints believed, the Lord would destroy them. [^21]

The Missouri Intelligencer published articles which confirmed the political threat the Mormons posed to the Missourians. On 20 July 1833, an article appeared which spoke of Mormon settlers coming to Independence in swarms. "It requires no gift of prophecy to tell that the day is not far distant when the government of the county will be in their hands." [^22] The article continued by saying it would not be long before there were Mormon sheriffs and judges.

There were also differences in religious beliefs between the Missouri settlers and the gathering Latter-day Saints. Before 1804 Missouri was predominantly Catholic. As Jacksonian democracy began to prevail in the early 1800s, Protestantism superseded Catholicism in the new frontier. Nearly all of the "Jackson men" were Protestant. The Catholic Church could no longer hold its dominance, as it did as late as 1797 when Protestant ministers were outlawed. The two major Protestant churches represented among early Missouri settlers were the Baptists and the Methodists. These denominations fit well into the Missouri society with their emphasis on individuality, free will, and free grace. Presbyterians also came but were less successful. Their Calvinistic attitudes of election and predestination came in direct contrast to the free spirit of Jacksonian America. Presbyterianism, similar to the LDS concept of a special group chosen by God, did not ring true to those who believed that anyone could succeed with hard work and initiative. Hattie M. Anderson's research showed that in religion

Missourians manifested some of their most obvious traits, for there was the same crudeness, quarrelsomeness, individualism, aggressiveness, and competitivenessÂ�. Their worship was crude; they wrangled over matters of doctrine and form; the extreme individualism made it difficult to maintain permanent religious organizations, and religious sects competed to increase their membership. [^23]

Even though there were differences in religious idealogies between the Missourians and the Mormons, it appears that the real conflict came between religion versus irreligion. For example, Joseph Smith received a revelation in 1831 concerning keeping the Sabbath day holy. He was told by the Lord that he and the Saints of God should keep themselves unspotted from the world, pay their devotions to the Most High, confess their sins to their brethren, prepare food with singleness of heart, and preserve Sunday as a day totally for worship (D&C 59:9-16). This came in stark contrast to many of the Missourians way of worshipping on Sunday:

In the years before 1820, in St. Louis, Sunday was a day for the conducting of business and amusement rather than one of worship, and these days carried records of fighting and riot of negro slaves. St. Charles had horse races on Sunday. In 1819, white men in the frontier town of Franklin paraded the streets on Sunday, played cards, and fought. Everywhere on the frontier the Sabbath was much neglected and profaned, even by professors of religion. [^24]

Sabbath breaking and other irreligious activities of the Missourians were contrary to the teachings of the Latter-day Saints. Joseph Smith later said, "We could not associate with our neighbors who were many of them of the basest of men and had fled from the face of civilized society, to the frontier country to escape the hand of justice, in their midnight revels, their Sabbath breaking, horse racing, and gambling, they commenced at first to ridicule, then to persecute." [^25] T. Edgar Lyon found the same elements in his research on life in the Independence outpost. Non-Mormon missionaries to the area reported:

There are a few so-called ministers of the Gospel hereabouts, but they are a sad lot of churchmen, untrained, uncouth, given to imbibing spirituous liquors, and indulging, as participants, in the gambling which accompanies horse-racing and cock fightingÂ�. Christian Sabbath observance here appears to be unknown. It is a day for merchandising, jollity, drinking, gambling, and general anti-Christian conduct. When the Santa Fe wagon trains return here, or pass through on their way eastward, there is a multiplication of sin beyond the usual amount. There appears to be an over abundance of females here practicing the world's oldest profession. [^26]

Another problem arose over the Mormons' friendship to the Indians. The Saints were predisposed to being friendly to the Indians from the very beginning as is shown in the first missionary journey to Missouri during the winter of 1831. The missionaries had arrived in Independence after a long trek west to the "borders of the Lamanites." Parley P. Pratt described their missionary journey through New York and Ohio including their extended adventure on foot from St. Louis to Independence. [^27] Pratt related their early success of preaching among the Delaware Indians, and the excitement, jealousy, and envy of the Indian agents and other sectarian missionaries in the area. [^28] This envy and antagonism for the Mormon missionaries grew to the point of their being ordered out of the Indian country.

Meanwhile at Kirtland, the Lord had commanded the "Colesville Saints" living in Thompson, Ohio, to journey westward to Missouri (D&C 54:8). He reminded the Saints to be patient in tribulations as he foresaw the clash in Jackson County. After the arrival of the Colesville Branch, as well as other Latter-day Saints to Missouri, articles began to appear in the Evening and Morning Star describing the Indians as members of the lost ten tribes, who would assist in the building of the temple. The December 1832 edition of the paper stated:

It is not only gratifying, but almost marvelous, to witness the gathering of the Indians. The work has been going on for some time, and these remnants of Joseph gather by hundreds and settle west of the Missouri and Arkansas. And is not this scripture fulfilling: Give ear, O Shepherd of Israel, thou that leadest Joseph like a flock, through the instrumentality of the government of the United States? For it is written, Behold I will lift up my hand to the Gentiles, and set up my standard to the people: and they shall bring thy sons in their arms, and thy daughters shall be carried upon their shoulders. [^29]

One of the purposes for gathering to Zion was to build the temple in Jackson County. In an earlier revelation the Lord had said a temple would be reared in that generation upon the consecrated spot (D&C 84:1-4). In August of 1833, the Lord revealed his will that a house should be built in the land of Zion (D&C 97:10). Parley P. Pratt, a key leader in Zion, remarked, "This revelation [D&C 97] was not complied with by the leaders of the Church of Missouri, as a whole; not withstanding many were humble and faithful. Therefore, the threatened judgment was poured out to the uttermost as the history of the five following years will show." [^30]

Another problem of conflicting cultures between the early Missourians and the Mormons came over the issue of slavery. Missouri was a slave state, while the Mormons were anti-slavery and sympathized with the abolitionist movement. Shortly before mob violence broke out in Jackson County, an article entitled "Free People of Color" appeared in the July 1833 issue of the Evening and Morning Star which simply quoted Missouri laws on the subject. The article widened the differences between the two groups. A disclaimer in an extra was printed on 16 July 1833 which denied any actual abolitionist intentions, but the disclaimer went unheeded. [^31]

In summary, many factors contributed to the cultural and religious differences between the Missourians and the Mormons. It was a setting prone for conflict, tension, and animosity. Some of the major differences were divergent attitudes about Christian living, friendship toward the Indians, the slavery issue, Sabbath breaking, and a growing threat of political power. Later revelations to Joseph Smith stated that the Saints had experienced many of the hardships in Missouri in consequence of their own transgressions (D&C 101, 105), including their failure to build the temple (D&C 97). Other factors which contributed to the "jarrings, envyings, and strife" were Mormon control of grazing lands, conversations with "gentiles" freely voicing eschatological expectation of inheritance and dominion, the opposition of gentile merchants to the Mormon economic policies of consecration, squatters' fears of Mormons obtaining their land, and offensive articles in Latter-day Saint publications that were distasteful to other denominations. [^32]

Redemption of Zion

Previous to July of 1831, Joseph Smith had received seven revelations concerning the Saints' activities in Missouri (D&C 28, 42, 45, 48, 49, 52, 55). All of these divine communications motivated the Saints to migrate to Missouri to receive an everlasting inheritance in the land of Zion preparatory to the second coming of Jesus Christ. Millennial expectations were high among the Mormons, as with other denominations. When the Prophet arrived in Independence, the Lord again stressed that the land of Missouri had been appointed for the gathering of the Saints, and that they should purchase the land for an everlasting inheritance (D&C 57:1-5). However, in August of 1831, the Lord said that his law should be kept on the land, and promised that after much tribulation would come blessings. The Saints were to still purchase the whole region of country, but only after many years would the inheritance actually be realized through their faith (D&C 58:19, 44, 49, 51, 52, 56).

Expulsion from Jackson CountyHCAfter Joseph Smith returned to Kirtland, the Lord cautioned the Saints not to gather to Missouri in haste, lest there would be confusion (D&C 63:24). The Lord then referred to the coming tension and conflict when he referred to the inhabitants of Missouri as enemies (D&C 63:26-31). Elder Bruce R. McConkie, commenting on this scripture, queried, "Is the Lord warning them that all will not go well with their efforts to build the New Jerusalem? Are they being told that tribulation and trials and persecutions and mobbings lie ahead?" [^33] So although the message and goal of obtaining the land through legal means was mentioned, the Lord also knew the troubles that lay ahead for his people.

Expulsion from Jackson CountyHCAfter the expulsion of the Saints from Jackson County, another revelation was given to the Prophet concerning the persecution. The Lord specifically said he allowed the conflict to happen because of the Saints' own transgressions (D&C 101:2). They were told that they needed to have an Abrahamic experience, and these problems had arisen because of jarrings, contentions, envyings, strifes, lustful and covetous desires among the Saints (D&C 101:4-6). Even before this communication from the Lord in December of 1833, Oliver Cowdery and Joseph Smith wrote a letter to the brethren in Jackson County concerning their initial feelings about the expulsion of the Saints in August of that same year. In this letter, the Saints were told that the hardships and persecutions were necessary to cleanse the Church at large. [^34] The epistle further declared:

Expulsion from Jackson CountyHCThis affliction is sent upon us not for your sins but for the sins of the church and that all the ends of the Earth may know that you are not speculiting with them for Lucre but you are willing to die for the cause you have espoused you know that the church have treted lightly the commandments of the Lord and for this cause they are not worthy to receive them yet God has suffered it not for your sins but that he might preprare you for a grateer work that you might be prepared for the endowment from on high. [^35]

Expulsion from Jackson CountyHCIn other words, the Church at large was partially responsible for the conflict in Jackson County. The Saints had treated lightly the things they had received, which included a previous revelation announcing their neglect of the Book of Mormon (D&C 84:54-58) and failure to build the temple (D&C 97, 101).

Zion's CampHCIn addition, the Lord had given an earlier parable regarding the future redemption of Zion saying that the gathering should not be in haste and that they should still try to buy land in the area (D&C 101:68-71). On 24 February 1834 the Prophet Joseph Smith received a revelation concerning the organization of Zion's camp to help the Saints regain possession of their inheritance (D&C 103). At the end of this expedition, the Lord again revealed that the Saints had transgressed the laws necessary to redeem Zion. They had not learned to be obedient to the Law of Consecration (D&C 105). The Saints were counseled at that time, which evidently remains in force in the Church today, to give freely of their money to the cause, to be taught more perfectly, to have experience, and to purchase lands in Jackson County (D&C 105:8-10). The Lord further said:

Zion's CampHCFirst let my army become very great, and let it be sanctified before me, that it may become fair as the sun, and clear as the moon, and that her banners may be terrible unto all nations; That the kingdoms of this world may be constrained to acknowledge that the kingdom of Zion is in very deed the kingdom of our God and his Christ; therefore, let us become subject unto her laws. (D&C 105:31-32)

Zion's CampHCThe Lord also told the Saints not to talk of judgments, neither boast of faith, or of mighty works, as they gathered in Missouri (D&C 105:24). As this revelation suggests, some members in Jackson County were not following this command; their behavior understandably caused the Missourians concern. But the fault was not that of the Saints alone; the Lord in his mercy eventually revoked the divine command to build the New Jerusalem in that generation because the Saints had been hindered by their enemies while in Missouri (D&C 124:51).

Zion's CampHCThe City of Zion, including the temple of the New Jerusalem, has not graced Missouri's soil over a century and a half later. However, there will be a future day when chosen, qualified Latter-day Saints will redeem Zion. Elder Bruce R. McConkie paraphrasing and interpreting D&C 105 said:

Zion's CampHCAfter many days, a designated period in which we still live, those who are called, chosen, selected, appointed, and sent forth by the voice of the Spirit, as it speaks to the President of the Church, shall build the New Jerusalem and the holy templeÂ�. In the meantime, our work as a people is to keep the commandments and sanctify ourselves so that if the call comes in our day, we shall be worthy to respond. [^36]

Zion's CampHCThe Latter-day Saints from 1831 to 1838 were not able to redeem Zion. Their personal sins and weaknesses temporarily prevented them from obtaining the land of promise. The Church is still under many of the same edicts given in the scriptures about the building of the New Jerusalem. The Saints need to follow Elder McConkie's advice of sanctifying themselves so they will be ready when that day comes to fully establish the New Jerusalem under prophetic guidance.

Conclusion

The existing social, cultural, and physical environment of Missouri in 1831 was a setting prone for conflict. The Missourians were an aggressive, independent, irreligious people from Kentucky and Tennessee. Their Sunday activities of fighting, gambling, and horse-racing came in conflict with the Mormon concept of Sabbath worship. The Latter-day Saints befriended the Indians whom the Missourians saw as a roadblock to trade. In addition, the Missourians believed in and practiced slavery, while the Mormons sympathized with the abolitionist movement. These conditions comprised the primary differences between the groups and created the setting for tension and conflict. Probably the foremost cause of the conflict in Jackson County was the existing settlers' fear of political domination by the Mormons who were rapidly migrating to the land of their inheritance.

From 1831-1838, the Latter-day Saints gathered to Missouri to obtain their land of inheritance. The Lord had directed them to purchase lands and to prepare to live his law in the land of promise. However, the Lord also knew the tribulations which would follow, especially if the Saints were not obedient. The contentions, envyings, strifes, and covetous desires which developed among them prevented the redemption of Zion, which included a failure to build the temple (D&C 97, 101). Still, the Lord promised the Saints that, after many years, the realization of the promised city would come through obedience and exercising great faith. The Saints were to learn from this experience. During Joseph Smith's imprisonment in the Liberty Jail in March of 1839, the Prophet cried to the Lord as to why he and the Saints had experienced so much persecution and hardship. The answer was "all these things shall give thee experience, and shall be for thy good" (D&C 122:7). The Saints needed then, as they do now, to learn to live the laws of the celestial kingdom, to be willing to sacrifice their means for the building of Zion, and to follow the Lord's commands in all things in preparation for the establishment of the New Jerusalem.
