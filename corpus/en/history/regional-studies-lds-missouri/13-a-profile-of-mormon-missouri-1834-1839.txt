A Profile of Mormon Missouri, 1834-1839

Clark V. Johnson

In May 1839, a few months after he was forced to leave Missouri, Asahel Lathrop wrote that James Weldon, a neighbor, and W. P. Thompson, a justice of the peace, had warned him "to leave his home immediately or suffer Death." At this time, Asahel's wife was apparently recovering from a recent childbirth and was still weak. Shortly after he left, his home "was thronged" by 14 or 15 "armed men" who abused his family. These men moved into the house, took what they wanted, and forced Asahel's wife to cook, and wait on them. While he was away from home, he camped on the prairie out of sight but near his farm so he could watch over his family, and he appealed to local government leaders for help. During his absence his baby died and was "buried by the Mob" and his wife was not even["all[owed] to pay the last respects to her child."

After being gone nearly two weeks, he finally dared to return home. When he entered his house, he found his family "in a soriful situation not one of the remaining ones able to wait uppon the other." Once again he was warned to take his family and leave. Asahel moved his family about 60 miles where his wife died and his two remaining children died a "short time" later. [^1] When Asahel crossed the Mississippi River into Illinois, he had buried all of his family along the way.

Asahel Lathrop along with 12,000 to 15,000 other Mormons fled from Missouri after Governor Lilburn W. Boggs issued the now infamous Extermination Order. [^2] Lathrop's affidavit is representative of at least 800 documents. Most of these are petitions by Latter-day Saints sworn before government officials illustrating some of the most violent stories ever told concerning religious persecution on the US frontier. [^3] These recorded documents provide a detailed account of Mormon persecution in Missouri. In addition they also give detailed information about land ownership, wealth, personal property, and migration of the Saints who were present on the Missouri frontier in the 1830s.

The Saints Settle in Missouri

Under the Prophet Joseph Smith's direction, the Saints began settling Jackson County in western Missouri in 1831. Joseph dedicated several sites in Jackson County for the future use of Church members (D&C 52:2-5). [^4] As early as April 1832, troubles arose between the Mormons and their Missouri neighbors and in 1833 mobs drove the Saints from Jackson County. Most of the exiles settled in Clay County, but some moved north and east into the counties of Ray, Clinton, LaFayette, Carroll, Chariton, Randolph, and Monroe and into areas that later came to be known as Daviess and Caldwell Counties. [^5] Here the Mormons settled long enough to build homes and plant crops. This brief respite lasted only a few months, and then in 1836, mobs again gathered in response to continual Mormon migration from the eastern states and to agitation by Jackson County residents. [^6]

During this time (1836-38), the citizens of Clay County undertook action to relocate the Mormons in some unsettled parts of Missouri. [^7] By 1836 the Latter-day Saints had moved to an area known as Caldwell County, and within two years over 4,900 Saints had settled there. [^8] [^9] However, they knew little rest, for during the summer and fall of 1838 mobs once more came against them.

The Missourians felt threatened by the continual influx of Mormons and saw, in the presence of their new neighbors, a threat to their society. LDS religious customs, which united Church members socially, economically, and politically, irritated the Missourians. These differences made the mobbers more vindictive, and the violence escalated into the so-called "Mormon War," which culminated in the expulsion of the Saints from the state.

Origin and Purpose of the Petitions

As an outgrowth of the Mormon War, Joseph Smith spent the winter of 1838-39 confined in jail at Liberty, Missouri. While incarcerated the Prophet received a revelation wherein the Lord counseled the Saints to prepare statements describing their recent persecutions and deprivations in Missouri. He instructed his Prophet to gather this information, and to present it to the federal government to obtain redress for the losses they had suffered in Missouri (D&C 123:1-4).

Beginning in May 1839, Church members started writing their Missouri experiences. These affidavits were sworn before civil authorities in Illinois and Iowa. These officials included justices of the peace, county clerks, clerks of circuit courts, and notary publics in two counties in Iowa and ten counties in Illinois. [^10] During the ensuing years (1839-43), the Latter-day Saints presented these documents to the federal government on at least three different occasions in a concerted attempt to obtain reparation from the federal government for their sufferings in Missouri. [^11]

Appeals to Congress

At this time Joseph Smith made every effort to keep the Mormon cause before Congress by a personal visit to Washington, DC and by continued correspondence with the Illinois congressional delegation, who tried to use their influence to help the Saints. Even after Joseph Smith's death in 1844, Church leaders apparently intended to make another appeal to the federal government since 13 of the petitions found in the Salt Lake collection are dated 1845. [^12]

Order of Extermination and Expulsion from MissouriHCNot all of the documents in these collections are petitions. Letters to Senator Felix Grundy seeking his help, and a letter from Governor Robert Lucas of Iowa supporting the Saints serve as such examples. The earliest petitions portray the mobbings in Jackson County and the latest end with the persecution resulting from Governor Boggs' Extermination Order. These documents give insights about the petitioners themselves. Among the petitioners who filed personal affidavits were 70 women. Twenty-five men and 11 women were illiterate. Twenty-five men and women had witnessed the Haun's Mill Massacre or arrived shortly after the event; eight claimed personal knowledge of the events and sufferings at De Witt; five were at the Gallatin election; three described the events at Crooked River; and 23 were at Adam-ondi-Ahman. One hundred six men and three women claimed that they were taken prisoner by the Missouri militia or mobs, and at least 21 Mormons were killed.

Some of the Saints documented their losses before a county officer and forwarded their documents to Church leaders in Illinois or in Washington, DC. Simons Curtis claimed the smallest amount, $.63, while Edmund Nelson claimed $5,000 for the loss of property and $500,000 for loss of liberty. [^13] Some might question Nelson's motives for his obviously exaggerated figure, but it may have been his way of showing the frustration and contempt he felt concerning the violation of human rights that he suffered in Missouri. The brief petitions of Daniel Cathcart and Steven Blackman serve as examples of the information found in their statements concerning the losses they suffered:

State of Missouri Dr to Daniel Cathcart For damages Sustained by loss of property in Davis Co by being driven from the state $200 sickness by exposure $500 [signed] Daniel Cathcart Sworn to before me the sixth day of May AD 1839 C. M. Woods Clerk Circuit Court Adams County Ill. [^14]

State of Missouri Dr. to Stephen Blackman For damage and loss of property by burning and being driven from the State $150 For damage by loss of Son there is no earthly consideration can compensate [Signed] Stephen Blackman I certefy the within to be correct May 6th 1839 [Signed] Stephen Blackman Sworn to before me this sixth day of May A. D. 1839 C M Woods Clerk Circuit Court Adams County State of Illinois. [^15]

Many bills contain only two or three lines, and yet others are more extensive, such as the one sworn by Nahum Curtiss, who gave an elaborate account of the land and personal property he lost in Missouri:

May 13th 1839 An account that I Nahum Curtiss have against the State of Missouri in Consequence of Mobocracy To expenses moveing from the State of Michigan to the state of Missouri $50.00

To Eight weeks that it took me to go with my family from Michigan to Missouri at $1 per day 48.00

To Eight weeks time each for my Two boys 96.00

To Loss on Land which I sustained in consequence of being driven from Missouri 1,520.00

To Loss of time for myself and son in time of waring against the Mob six months each at one dollar per Day each $312.00

To Loss on Corn potatoes & oates and hay 150.00

To Loss of ploughs 5.00

To loss on Cattle and Hogs 55.00

To one horse Taken by the Militia 50.00

To Loss of wagon in Consequence of mob Stealing it from me 20.00

To Expenses Moving from Missouri to Illinois 25.00

To waggon & team Moveing me to Illinois 40.00

To Money & property given to help the poor 50.00

To teams to help the poor out of the state of Missouri to Keep them from being killed by mob 40.00

Total: 2,461.00

As an appendix to this bill, Curtiss wrote a few lines about his personal suffering,

I Shall not put any price upon my sufferings as your honorable body will Consider that I was a fellow Sufferer with the Rest and when you Judge what others ought to have you will consider that I had ought to have an equal porportion with the Rest [Signed] Nahum Curtiss. [^16]

One hundred twenty-one people wrote more than one petition. Generally their first petition claimed monies, properties, abuses, etc., suffered in Missouri, while their second petition usually gave a detailed account of what happened at an event or place. Ninety-eight of the petitions make no claim against Missouri. However, 85 percent claimed losses totalling $2,275,789, for an average of $3,761 against the state of Missouri. Claims on land amounted to $197,911; for improvements and property (defined as livestock, houses, personal property, etc.), $197,127; and miscellaneous, wherein only totals were listed by the petitioners, came to $92,339. In fact, Joseph and Hyrum Smith each claimed $100,000 but gave no breakdown of expenditures. [^17]

Order of Extermination and Expulsion from MissouriHCTwo hundred thirty-two petitioners stated that they purchased 6,501 acres of land for $55,046, which averages less than nine dollars per acre. The average per claim was $237.27 for land. Nine dollars per acre for land in Missouri was not an extremely high price in 1839. In their 1839 petition, Joseph Smith, Sidney Rigdon, and Elias Higbee stated that land prices ranged from ten dollars per acre for undeveloped prairie land to $425 per acre for improved land. Some of the land purchased by the Saints was called "congress land," which could be bought from the federal government for $1.25 per acre; however, some of the Mormons paid between five dollars and $415 per acre for congress land and received preemption certificates which gave them the right to the land if and when the federal government decided to sell it. In the meantime they settled the land, tilled the ground, and built houses and barns, thereby increasing the property value.

The petitions reflect the fact that most of them owned at least one town lot and 40 acres, whereas a few owned 80 acres, and still fewer owned 160 acres. Only a very few who had lived in Missouri for several years owned several hundred acres. [^18]

Many of the petitioners indicate that they lived in more than one county during their sojourn in Missouri. Of those who designated property losses in Missouri counties, 119 lived in Jackson County. Once they were driven from Jackson County, the Mormons dispersed in every direction. For example, 125 petitioners moved to Clay County where they purchased property. Ninety-one lived in Daviess County, 25 owned property in Ray, 19 in Carroll, 13 in Clinton, seven in LaFayette, and four in Livingston. Vanburen, Randolph, and Chariton each had three Mormon families as residents.

An analysis of all of the petitions indicates that Mormon migration to Missouri did not cease even though there was persecution. By the summer of 1838, there were 4,900 people living in Caldwell County. [^19] Among them were 290 petitioners who owned property.

Probably the reason the Saints owned so little property was their recent arrival in Missouri. Of 176 petitioners who gave the year they arrived in Missouri, 58 had come between 1831 and 1836, and 126 had entered the state between 1837 and 1838. One claimant arrived in 1839. Thus, the majority of the petitioners arrived just in time to be expelled, and several others indicated that they were detained by mobs while on their way to Far West.

Albern Allen's affidavit illustrates another example of a loss sustained by the Mormons. Allen, who wrote that he was "taken prisoner," claimed a total of 80 acres. His petition defined his property using survey terms. He possessed land duplicates or deeds of intent in Caldwell County, which he surrendered to the mobbers, verifying that he owned the "N.E. 1/4 of S.W. 1/4 of D&C 32 Township No. 56 Range 28 North of the base line and west of the 5th principle meridean also the N.W. 1/4 of the S.E. 1/4 of D&C 32 Township 56 Range 29 North of the base line of the 5th principle Meridian." [^20] Allen's petition is typical of many who claimed they were forced to sign over their lands to the Missouri Militia at Far West before they left the state.

In the summer of 1838 Smith Humphrey had purchased a farm near the town of De Witt and planned to settle there. [^21] He stated that he was an eyewitness to much that happened at De Witt, 19 August 1838. Humphrey wrote that he was taken prisoner by a force of about 100 men commanded by a Colonel Jones. During the time he was a prisoner, they threatened his life and declared that they were going to "drive them [Mormons] from that[Co[unty]." The mobbers made good their threats because in October 1838 Humphrey stated that he arose early one morning and found that his "Stables had been Set on fireÂ�during the night." He also described an incident that occurred when "12 armed men" took him prisoner. The leader of the group, a Captain Hiram Standly took Humphrey to his "own house &Â�compelled me to remove my goods from my house." After Humphrey and his family removed their personal belongings, the mobbers "Set fire to & burned my twoÂ�houses." [^22]

When he was driven from his home, his wife was "sick with the ague," and the mobbers forced him to move his family to De Witt. The Humphreys remained in De Witt until 11 October 1838 when the Mormons were forced to abandon the settlement. According to Humphrey, the mobbers fired into the town and kept it under siege. No one could leave or enter the small community. Finally, sick and starving, the Saints abandoned De Witt and moved to Caldwell County where they arrived 13 October 1838. Humphrey noted that "one woman died on the road." [^23]

Philandia Myreck, Nancy Cary, and Anne Christiana Benner, together with other women, wrote testimonials concerning the deaths of their husbands. Philandia wrote that 15 men were killed at Haun's Mill and others were wounded. Her husband, Levi N. Myrick, died a few hours after the attack, and her small son, Charles, lived "four weeks" before he died of the wounds he had received from a shotgun. [^24]

Nancy Cary wrote that "a man by the Name of Donihue Struck him [her husband] with the britch of his gun and broke his skull." Mr. Cary "lived two DaysÂ�Deprived of ade or comfort" before he died. [^25]

Christiana Benner claimed $5,150 in property, and then she stated, "My husband was[kil[l]ed at Haun's mill By a mob who Robbed me of my Goods and land and have left me Destitute of a companion or means of support." [^26]

At least one woman wrote of the personal abuse she suffered at the hands of a mob. Ruth Naper, who survived the Haun's Mill Massacre, wrote concerning an attempted assault upon her. She noted that a few days following the massacre the mob returned, "took possession of Haun's Mill" and moved into "our houseÂ�without my consent." While living there "one night one of them came to my bed and laid his hand upon me which so frightened me that I made quite a noise and crept over the back side of my children." Ruth said that he "offered no further insult at the time." [^27]

Although none of the petitioners directly mentioned rape, Elijah Reed witnessed an attempted assault on a Mrs. Jimmison. To escape from the mob, Reed had fled to the Jimmison's home where they hid him from the mob. While he was there he and the Jimmison's were surprised when a group of men came to the house and demanded admittance. As Mr. Jimmison opened the door, Elijah hid under the bed. The men identified themselves as soldiers and insisted that Jimmison accompany them to the militia camp. Jimmison dressed himself and "he & one of the men went for a horse at the Stable when they had got a little from the house the man fired a gun & said the Dmd rascal had run from him." Following Jimmison's escape the men "began to[abus[e] Mrs Jimmison wanting to sleep with her But she begged & cried for them to Desist & they Did so." [^28]

An affidavit sworn by Hyrum Smith, which is published in the History of the Church, states that one woman, who was living in Nauvoo, had been raped repeatedly by mobbers. [^29] Parley P. Pratt, in his petition sworn at Nauvoo, said that he knew of one woman who had been raped, but would not give her name for he felt that "delicacy prevents me from mentioning names." [^30]

Seventy-three petitions indicate that the mobbers whipped, beat, and abused members of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. Charles Hulet described the beating of his son. [^31] [^32] Keyes said they whipped him until he "was scarcely able to stand," and added that a mob whipped Barnet Cole and Lyman Leonard. [^33]

Tarlton Lewis, who had been severely wounded at Haun's Mill wrote that during the weeks following the massacre mobbers returned to the mill site. Lewis was confined to his house while recuperating from his wounds. Several times six or eight men entered his house "threatening to take me a prisoner." On two occasions they "Examined my wounds to see if I were able to be moved but concluded that I was not." [^34]

Nathan Knight, who was wounded at Haun's Mill, saved his own life by running "through their thickest fire." He "made his way several rods over a hill" where he secreted himself in a thicket. For six weeks he hovered between life and death, and although he recovered somewhat from his wounds, he remained a cripple the rest of his life. [^35]

One of the most descriptive petitions regarding personal abuse suffered at the hands of the mobs was sworn by Lydia B. English. Lydia and her first husband settled in Jackson County in 1832. She said, "We was greatly prospered through our industry for the first year." And then she noted that the "insults and threats were becoming very frequent." By 1833 the threats had turned into violent acts and Lydia recalled that one night she "heard the logs begin to fall from[of[f] the buildings" as men unroofed houses. While some were destroying homes others were "whipping and houndingÂ�husbands and fathers with clubs and stones." During the confusion the women and children who were "astonished at the horrible noise Scarcely knew what to do but in a moment all made for the woods." She continued, "I was then the mother of three children a boy two years old and a pair of twin babes but five weeks old." She swept her young son into her arms, and two young girls took her twins as they ran into the forest. Unfortunately they became separated, and Lydia noted that she "could find but one of my twins untill morning." Once hidden and safe from the mob she listened as the mobbers threw "the roof[of[f] my house." During this encounter her husband was wounded in the foot which eventually caused his death. [^36]

Many of the petitioners did not specify monetary losses, but they did provide detailed accounts of the events that drove the Mormons from county to county in Missouri and finally from the state.

Conclusions

The petitions demonstrate that Latter-day Saints followed the Prophet Joseph Smith to Missouri. These documents describe their feelings, circumstances, and losses and in so doing help us to evaluate Mormon life in Missouri during the 1830s.

In addition, these affidavits represent sworn legal documents, which tell a horrifying story of murder, beating, thievery, and general lawlessness perpetrated upon the Saints during their residency in Missouri.

The documents explain that the abuses suffered by the Mormons were not the result of spontaneous uprisings led by drunken town rabble (although there were opportunists among the mobbers), but that these uprising carried the sanction of the local and state governments. The petitions imply that the mob leaders had two objectives: first, to drive all the Mormons into Caldwell County; and second, to drive them from the state. They achieved both objectives.

In the past, Mormon and non-Mormon writers have argued objectively that the Mormons were driven from Missouri because of political, cultural, economic, social, and religious differences existing between themselves and the Missourians. They base their statements on documents written by some of the citizens of Jackson, and later, Clay County, when they demanded that the Mormons leave their counties. [^37] Such reasoning may be true in part, but the petitioners indicate that religious differences were the primary cause of their troubles. The affidavits demonstrate that the Saints could have spared themselves great abuse and suffering if they had succumbed to the mobbers' demands that they deny their faith, slander Joseph Smith and give up their membership in the Church or denounce the Book of Mormon.

An examination of all the petitions shows that even though the affidavits were written by different individuals they tell the same sobering story about Mormon persecution in Missouri. In addition, the affidavits give details about Mormon migration to Missouri, ownership of land, and personal property.

Although this study has not attempted to examine both sides of the Mormon persecutions in Missouri, it has taken legally sworn documents and substantiated the fact that the suffering and abuse committed on the Mormons by the Missourians came largely because of their differences of opinion regarding religious beliefs. Simply stated, the Missouri Redress Petitions tell the story of a people wrongfully deprived of their rights as free men and women under the constitutions of the state of Missouri and the United States of America.
