Expulsion from Jackson CountyHCMax H Parkin

Expulsion from Jackson CountyHCThe Latter-day Saint experience in Clay County, Missouri, in 1833-36 is a link in the chain of conflicts which people suffered in early Missouri. While it is a less familiar link, the distress of the Latter-day Saints in Clay County was abundant and took several forms. It started with their untimely and hazardous crossing of the Missouri River into Clay County from their homes in neighboring Jackson County, where mob violence in November 1833 had forced them away in a confused and unprepared state. This disorderly departure contributed to an uncomfortable survival of the Saints in Clay County and elsewhere, particularly during the first winter of exile. Then, defeat and frustration-coming from many failed efforts to return to Jackson County or to gain redress, and later hostile treatment from their new neighbors-were other hapless phases in that distress.

Expulsion from Jackson CountyHCSurvival

Expulsion from Jackson CountyHCTheir early hardships in Clay County were lamentable. At the forced river crossing from Jackson County, the fearful and sometimes confused Saints suffered from the lack of supplies and other preparations. Some of the suffering, however, could have been avoided if the Saints had met the unlawful demands of the Jackson mob, for after the mobs attacked them in Jackson County in July 1833, their enemy provided them sufficient time to harvest their crops and take provisions with them. Instead, the Saints accepted a suggestion from Missouri's governor to seek protection in the courts and hire attorneys to help. When the mobs learned of it, they forcefully evicted them, unprepared, with beatings and frightful threats of worse horrors if they did not leave. Of their unready condition and abrupt expulsion, Eliza, the young daughter of Bishop Edward Partridge, ecclesiastical leader of the Latter-day Saints in Jackson County, lamented, "A great amount if not all of our provisions that we had laid up for the winter must be lost and our houses left with many of our things in them, our lands and orchards and improvements of every kind left to the benefit [of] those who have driven us away." [^1]

As the first Latter-day Saints crossed the Missouri River northward on 7 November 1833 into Clay County from the ferry landing at Independence, Bishop Partridge, his family and others spent a fearful night in the heavy rain and cold. Others would cross the river at ferry stations elsewhere-east and west-or at other places on their own for the next several days. In fact, during that inclement November about a thousand expatriates set up housekeeping randomly for miles along the Missouri River-bottoms in Clay County, and a few hundred escaped south to Van Buren or to counties to the east. On the day he crossed the river, Nathan Tanner Porter observed that "the banks of the Missouri was now being lined with campfires of the little suffering multitude." [^2] Many huddled in makeshift shanties of stakes supporting quilts or brush coverings, or wigwam-like structures while attempting to shelter themselves from the storm. Scattered as they were and generally destitute, some Saints could not locate family members, acquire food, or find adequate protection from the elements. A few of the aged or sickly died from exposure or from want of sufficient nourishment or other proper care.

The Colesville Branch, which crossed the river several miles west of Independence and managed to stay together, lived in tents or built temporary shelters on the river-bottoms below Randolph Bluffs. Emily Austin, a Latter-day Saint, described the condition of some of this group: "We lived in tents until winter set in, and did our cooking out in the wind and storms. Log heaps were our parlor stoves, and the cold, wet ground our velvet carpets, and the crying of little children our piano forte; while the shivering, sick people hovered over the burning log piles here and there, some begging for cold water and others for hot coffee or tea." [^3]

Hunger was often an acute problem. Some were so in need of food that they risked their lives by returning to their farms in Jackson County to obtain provisions. Orange Wight described some in his group, a mix of Saints from various settlements west of Independence, as being "almost in a state of starvation." One of them, John Higbee, smuggled himself back to his house near the Big Blue River, Wight said, got a gun, manufactured his own rounds and killed eleven deer that winter to feed his family and others. [^4] To return to Jackson County was a risky matter. Some who did it even for reasons of survival were caught and severely beaten. Those who feared returning gathered wild honey (a popular venture of the time), gleaned residue from the already harvested fields in Clay County or acquired corn and other staples on credit from sympathetic farmers.

By late November, conditions worsened. Lower temperatures forced some to abandon their temporary quarters in order to seek better shelter near the bluffs above the flood plain of the Missouri River or beyond, where they began to occupy every shanty, unused farm shed or abandoned summer slave cabin available to them. Even with these improved conditions, some Latter-day Saints suffered from the severe weather when freezing temperatures made the passage of time tedious and otherwise difficult to endure. In December, more than a month after the expulsion, William W. Phelps, whose press had been destroyed at Independence the previous July, wrote to Joseph Smith at Church headquarters at Kirtland, Ohio. "The situation of the Saints, as scatteredÂ�affords a gloomy prospect," he said. "Our clothes are worn out [and] we want the necessaries of life." [^5]

As the grim unpleasantness of their winter passed, the warming promise of spring invited many to seek employment from sympathetic and often kind planters or business people at Liberty, seat of Clay County, or elsewhere in the southern and eastern part of the county. They cut cord wood for wages or provisions, worked at the mills, slaughtered hogs, made brick, worked in the fields, and taught school. While his people shared menial tasks among the Missouri slaves, Lyman Wight wrote with evident chagrin that the Latter-day Saint men, being used to better conditions, had to eke out a subsistence "like men of servitude." [^6] Judge Joseph Thorpe, a resident of Liberty and one who befriended the Saints at first but later was critical of their unrestrained religious zeal, wrote of their survival economy, "The Mormons, in the main, were industrious, good workers, and gave general satisfaction to their employers, and could live on less than any people I ever knew." [^7] Eventually, the Saints rented land in Clay County or bought it, or simply squatted on what they termed "Congress land," and then built houses, planted crops, and otherwise commenced to make their lives more comfortable.

Attempts at Redress

Enduring these early rigors of survival, Church leaders sought for a solution to their expulsion from Jackson County. Joseph Smith at Kirtland directed Bishop Partridge in December 1834 to seek redress before the law by prosecuting their Jackson County enemies and to carry their complaint, if necessary, up the political line to the president of the United States (D&C 101:76). [^8]

While Governor Daniel Dunklin at Jefferson City encouraged the Saints to process their grievances in the courts, he informed them in February 1834 that he could organize them into a company of the state militia and return them to Jackson County under arms if they desired; moreover, they could receive additional state troops to escort them back, if they would petition him for that assistance. The governor believed, however, that the state did not possess authority to keep its militia in Jackson County to police it after the Saints returned; once there, they must protect themselves. [^9] While Joseph Smith, in the East, began to recruit an army to bring them needful supplies, and later protection, he expected the leaders in exile to press for redress through the courts and through other means.

Criminal Prosecution

In seeking redress, the exiled leaders first filed criminal charges against their persecutors. About a dozen Latter-day Saint witnesses for the state, led by Bishop Partridge and editor Phelps, were escorted on 23 February 1834 to the courthouse at Independence by the Clay County militia under Captain David R. Atchison of Liberty. The witnesses were to testify before a court of inquiry at the February term of the Circuit Court in Jackson County. Once there, they were indignantly received and found that the Grand Jury itself was composed largely of enemies of the Saints. Some in fact resorted to intimidation against the witnesses and fostered such a bold front of animosity toward them and a spirit of belligerence in the community that Attorney General Robert W. Wells, representing the governor, Circuit Attorney Amos Reese, who was also the Latter-day Saint council, and Circuit Judge John F. Rylands concluded that the case could not proceed under the hostile climate at Independence, so these officers ended the investigation. [^10] The Grand Jury, therefore, filed no indictments, and the witnesses were escorted back to Clay County. This left the first attempt at redress fraught with frustration and marked by harassment of Church leaders, leaving them without success because neither the judicial power of the state nor the state militia from Clay County could provide order in Jackson County.

Civil Prosecution

Meanwhile, Church leaders also sought redress at the February term of the Circuit Court by filing civil charges in Jackson County against some of their mob attackers. But the civil suits progressed so slowly and were attended with such risk of mounting costs and ridicule, and the trials were so complex and served by a jury that was unsympathetic toward the plaintiffs, that all the charges were dropped but two. These were two acts of violence against Church leaders in July 1833 and were to be used as test cases. One was the assault upon Bishop Partridge, who had been beaten, tarred and feathered in the Independence courthouse square on 20 July before hundreds of witnesses, and the other was the destruction of Phelps' press the same day. These cases, too, floundered because of the unwillingness of the court to convict the accused assailants, who pleaded innocent on grounds of self defense.

The Church leaders, however, did not stop with this setback. They sought a change of venue to nearby Richmond, Ray County, where the attorneys for Partridge and Phelps pursued the civil cases. At length Circuit Judge Ryland, in the July 1836 term at Richmond, ruled in favor of the Mormon plaintiffs, but the ruling proved disheartening. Bishop Partridge was awarded "one cent" for assault damages against his person and Phelps was awarded a judgment of 750 dollars instead of the $50,000 in damages to the press, paper and building he had requested. [^11] The Saints felt that the awards were an insult to justice, and held their attorneys derelict for not pursuing their case more responsibly. [^12]

An Appeal to the Governor for Arms

A threatening problem arose for the Saints in Clay County in the Spring of 1834, which created in them a desire to be armed. Their enemies in Jackson County stirred up some Clay County citizens against them with evident violent intentions. The exiled people asked Governor Dunklin to arm them in order to help protect themselves from the emerging local menace and from a possible mob invasion from Jackson County. Specifically, they wanted their guns returned, which Lieutenant Colonel Thomas L. Pitcher of the state militia of Jackson County had illegally confiscated on 5 November at Independence under the pretext of quieting disturbances.

While exerting effort to seek a return of the weapons, the governor meanwhile offered to organize the exiled Saints into a Clay County company of Jackson County Guards and provide arms for them that way. However, in April, Bishop Partridge and Algernon Sidney Gilbert, whose Church store had suffered under the mob at Independence, and others turned down the offer for fear it would agitate their enemies in both counties and produce unwarranted "excitement" because of the open, public nature of organizing such a militia. [^13]

Apprehension on their part was well grounded. The atmosphere by May 1834 was so volatile that it threatened to explode into violence. By the first week of May, rumors circulated in Jackson County that the exiled Saints were preparing to cross the river, and some Jackson County citizens feared that the Mormons were going to return "mob-like to kill their women and children." [^14] In turn, to protect themselves against an attack by Missourians who might be frightened by the mounting rumors and while waiting for their weapons to be returned to them, the Saints felt the need to quietly establish an armory of their own for self defense. They manufactured or purchased "swords, dirks, pistols, stocking rifles, and [were] repairing arms in general," wrote Phelps and others, "for their own defense against [possible] mob violence." [^15]

In response to the rumor that the Saints might return to their lands that spring, the citizens in Jackson County went throughout the vacated Latter-day Saint settlements there in May and burned their houses. This scorched-earth effort was to reduce Latter-day Saint assets in their former Zion and thereby discourage a return. The citizens burned approximately 150 Latter-day Saint houses and other possessions and left the improved property wasted. [^16] But this was not enough. To further prevent a crossing, Jackson County citizens positioned cannons at some of the river landings and gathered in large numbers at those posts in the event the Saints did attempt to cross. The exiles, however, did not intend to cross until the arrival of their brethren from the East.

Meanwhile, the Saints pursued their request to the governor for the return of their arms. Governor Dunklin had issued a warrant to investigate Lieutenant Colonel Pitcher at a court of inquiry at Liberty and followed it up in January 1834 with a court martial at Richmond for illegally confiscating the Saints' weapons. The investigation was strung out for months, and finally on 2 May 1834 before the court martial ended, the governor ordered the arms returned to the Saints. Dunklin sent a request to Colonel Samuel D. Lucas, commandant of the Jackson County regiment of the militia, to deliver to Phelps and Partridge the 52 guns and one pistol confiscated the previous year. Colonel Lucas acted slowly until 24 May when he resigned his command and moved to Lexington, Lafayette County. The governor appointed Lieutenant Colonel Pitcher as Lucas' successor and ordered him to "collect the said armsÂ�and deliver them" to the Saints. [^17] [^18]

In his efforts with Lucas and Pitcher, Governor Dunklin proved powerless to return the weapons to the Saints and also later failed to help them materially in other ways. Later that year, the governor lamented to the state legislature for his failure to get the officers of the state to execute his orders; he therefore requested the legislature to provide him with more power to discharge the duties of his office. [^19]

An Appeal to the President

The Church leaders appealed for redress to the president of the United States-Andrew Jackson. The revelation that instructed the Church to seek redress had informed the leaders to take their grievance if necessary to the chief executive of the land. It stated, "And if the governor heed them not, let them importune at the feet of the president" (D&C 101:88). On 10 April 1834, Gilbert, Partridge and Phelps petitioned the president of the United States to send a federal force (Ft. Leavenworth was 20 miles west of Clay County), or use state troops under his authority to restore the Saints to their lands. They also wanted the president or the state to police the county after they returned because of the breakdown of law enforcement in Jackson County. After reviewing with the president his duties to them and his powers to act, the Saints requested,

For these purposes the President of the United States is authorized to make the call upon the executives of the respective States; therefore, we your petitioners, in behalf of our society, which is so scattered and suffering, most humbly pray that we may be restored to our land, houses and property in Jackson County, and protected in them by an armed force, till peace can be restored. [^20]

The Church leaders strengthened their petition to the president with signatures of 114 witnesses, who had suffered from the mob. They also sent the president a copy of a letter by Governor Dunklin offering his limited help to them and a printed circular describing their trials in Jackson County.

Notwithstanding their efforts, the president turned down their request. The weak link in the appeal was the governor. Believing that the national executive was not empowered to do as the Saints had asked, that is, to protect them "by an armed force" of US troops after they returned to their houses, Dunklin refused to endorse the Saints full request. Some historians have seen Governor Dunklin as a "strict constructionist" and a rigid exponent of states' rights. This was a time when some Jacksonian Democrats (which Dunklin was), feared any "encroachment of federal power upon the rights of the individual states." [^21] Actually, President Jackson passed the Latter-day Saint documents to Secretary of War Lewis Cass, who ruled that the Saints' problem did not fall within the guidelines of presidential power inasmuch as federal law had not been violated, to which Dunklin probably agreed. Moreover, Secretary Cass wrote that "the President [could not] call out a military force to aid in the execution of State laws," unless the governor requests it. [^22] Inasmuch as Governor Dunklin had not endorsed that request, the president did not feel disposed to help.

The Church leaders in Ohio generally were articulate Jacksonian Democrats. As such, they expected national support and prepared to publish a Jacksonian newspaper at Kirtland called the "Democrat" in support of party politics. [^23] As Jackson men, they hoped for the president's support and believed also that he had the power to act in their behalf. To apply pressure on the president, Church leaders petitioned Congress. They asked Missouri's powerful democrat, Senator Thomas Hart Benton, to support their cause. But Senator Benton, though a personal friend of the president, did not help them, and their appeal to Washington failed.

Negotiation

Meanwhile, an ongoing effort to gain redress through a negotiated peace with the citizens of Jackson County was attempted. Eventually, a delegation of Saints and Church leaders in Jackson County met at Liberty on 16 June 1834 with the governor's representative, John Thornton of Clay County, as moderator. The Jackson County delegation offered the Saints a solution: the citizens of Jackson County would buy Latter-day Saint lands or the Saints could buy the land of their former neighbors; either purchase was to be at double the property value and to be paid for within 30 days. But the Saints felt they could do neither under the terms proposed. The previous December, Joseph Smith had emphasized to them, "It is better in the eyes of God that you should die, than that you should give up the land in Zion." Hence, to sell their land, they said, "would be like selling our children into slavery." [^24] Moreover, since the ratio of land ownership in Jackson County was as high as thirty to one-Gentile to Latter-day Saint-the Saints could not buy out their neighbors; consequently, they turned the proposal down. [^25] The Saints made a counter-proposal to buy out only those who could not live among them, but that was rejected by the citizens. These negotiations, like later ones amidst tension and sometimes marked hostility, failed to resolve the conflict between the two groups.

In fact, hope for future successful negotiations seemed hopelessly damaged by a tragedy associated with this conference. When the Jackson County delegates returned home on the evening of 16 June, the ferry sank midstream drowning five men and raising fear in the survivors. Only one of the Jackson committeemen drowned (the other dead were ferrymen or other passengers), but rumor attached the blame to the Latter-day Saints. [^26] Reportedly, someone had drilled holes in the side planking so that when the ferry was loaded it drew water under the subfloor and sank.

The cause of the accident is unknown, but the sinking of the ferry generated widespread hostility against the Saints. The history of Joseph Smith states simply that the "angel of God saw fit to sink the boat." [^27] Judge Joseph Thorpe of Clay County attributed the sinking solely to an overloaded craft. [^28] Emily Austin said a rumor among the Saints attributed the sinking to the elders by their auguring holes just above the water line and the weight of the men and horses lowered the boat so that it took on water. [^29] Whatever the cause, the incident further damaged the Church's reputation in the community. Alexander Majors, a Jackson County resident, a close observer of the Latter-day Saints (and one later famed as the founder of the Pony Express), said that the ferry tragedy engendered in "the whole population the most intense feeling against" the Latter-day Saints. [^30]

Zion's CampHCZion's Camp

Meanwhile, Church leaders at Kirtland organized a military and relief party, known as Zion's Camp, to help the exiles in Missouri. In addition to bringing humanitarian supplies, the purpose of the expedition was to assist the state in escorting the Saints back to their homes in Jackson County. Also, the Camp was to remain in Jackson County to protect its people after the state troops withdrew; for the governor persisted in the belief that neither the state nor the US military possessed the right to maintain a police force in Jackson County. By mid-June 1834, the approach of Zion's Camp, however, had so heightened hostility in western Missouri-again partly because of belief of the retribution the armed Saints might inflict upon their enemies in Jackson County-that it prompted the local citizens to prepare to fight. With the threat of a civil war, Governor Dunklin, the public protector, understandably became alarmed.

As the Camp arrived in eastern Missouri while traveling toward Clay County, Joseph Smith, Camp leader, sent Orson Hyde and Parley P. Pratt to Jefferson City to seek the governor's assistance. Knowing that the condition in western Missouri was inflamed, Dunklin expressed to the men his fear of a bloody civil conflict. He appealed to the two elders for patience and a resolution of the problem through some peaceful means. Hyde and Pratt were unimpressed with the governor's request and instead assigned cowardice to his conciliatory attitude. [^31]

But citizens in western Missouri, including Clay, Jackson, Ray, and Lafayette Counties, indeed, were ready for war. One Latter-day Saint estimated the mob members in the several counties in the hundreds and a neighboring Lafayette County defender estimated them at as high as a thousand. [^32] That same defender, a resident of nearby Lexington, wrote to his family in the South on 20 June: "Next Monday is supposed to be the day [the Mormons] intended to cross the river to take Jackson County. The whole country is in an uproar." [^33] [A week later the crisis had passed, but the same observer wrote of his still present resolve. "I know we had neither law nor gospel on our side," he said, "but self-preservation urged us to pursue [our] course." He predicted that if a battle had taken place, there would have been a carnage. "If [the Mormons] had crossed the [Missouri] river, I very much question if one would have been left to tell the tale," he wrote. "No quarter would have been given. We could have killed most of them before they got across the river." [^34]

Meanwhile, tension mounted as the citizens waited for Zion's Camp, consisting of 205 men, to arrive in Clay County. While having seen signs of movement of the mob in Ray and Clay Counties, the Saints set up camp on the eastern edge of Clay County, near where the road crossed the Fishing River. Later that day, 19 June, instead of a battle, the providence of a violent storm prevented a meeting of the two forces. The storm front struck the camp near evening with severe winds, rain, hail, and lightening, which also continued throughout the night and scattered the citizens as well as the arriving Latter-day Saints. Levi Hancock, a member of the Camp, was so impressed with the timely violence of the storm in their protection from the mob that he attributed it to God as a "greater miracle" than some Moses had performed. [^35] Two days later, Colonel John Sconce, who was one of three leading men of Ray County that met with Camp leaders, seemed to agree: "I see that there is an Almighty power that protects this people," he said. [^36]

Unable to continue on the road west to Liberty because of the flooded Fishing River, the Camp traveled north on high ground between the river's two main forks. Three days after the storm, Sheriff Cornelius Gillium of Clay County met with Joseph Smith at their new encampment and commenced negotiations to prevent a battle. The sheriff expressed a willingness to help the exiled Saints if the Camp would not cross the river into Jackson County. Joseph Smith agreed. Later that day, Sunday, 22 June 1834, as the Camp met on land owned by Church member James Cooper on the upper east fork of the Fishing River, the Prophet received a lengthy revelation which among other things directed him to disband Zion's Camp (D&C 105:9).

Encouraged in their hope for a solution through amiable discussion, the Church leaders postponed asking the governor for a military escort to help return their people to Jackson County. On 26 June, Phelps, Gilbert, and John Corrill, Partridge's counselor, wrote the governor: "We think it wisdom to defer petitioning for a guard, while there exists a hope of compromise." [^37] In August, Phelps wrote again, this time giving the governor another reason they had not petitioned him for the military escort. Phelps stated that their reason for holding back was to avoid a war at a time of crisis while trusting that a satisfactory solution to the Mormon problem could be found through means of discussion and compromise. [^38] Eventually, hope faded. Though negotiations continued, they failed the Saints; once the emergency had passed, the citizens of western Missouri lost interest in reconciliation or accommodation.

Cholera

By the last week of June 1834, as the threat of battle was subsiding, another misfortune beset the Saints in Clay County. The arrival of Zion's Camp into Clay County brought Cholera which is a disease of the digestive tract and was often a frightful malady of the sometimes unsanitary frontier. It struck members of the Camp violently when they stopped at Rush Creek two miles east of Liberty, 24 June, two days after the reception of the "Fishing River Revelation." Some of the members of Zion's Camp believed the disease was a divine retribution for their own misconduct during their journey to Missouri. [^39] About seventy members of the Camp were stricken with it and thirteen of them died as did two local Church members, including Algernon Sidney Gilbert. Gilbert had treated some of the diseased Camp members at his house near Rush Creek. The presence of the disease in Clay County terrified the citizens, and with the sinking of the ferry still fresh in their memory, it further hardened them against the Latter-day Saints. Just before the outbreak of the disease, the now General David R. Atchison had advised Camp members not to enter Liberty because of the enraged feelings of the people against them. The sinking of the ferry, the arrival of Zion's Camp-and the enmity associated with both-and the outbreak of cholera deepened the animosity. Thus, though less than a year in Clay County, the Saints suffered repeated disappointments, the hardships of a displaced people, and the hatred from some of their new neighbors.

The Gathering

The revelation at the Fishing River instructed Church members not to cross to Jackson County at that time, but the revelation did not leave them without a strategy for the future. Though the Lord stated in the revelation that "my elders should wait for a little season for the redemption of Zion," the Lord said that the "little season" was to give the Saints in Clay County time "to gather up the strength of my house" to that place (D&C 105:9, 27). They were to gather and purchase land in Clay County and otherwise fortify their position until their "army became very great" (D&C 105:31). During the build up, Church leaders advised their followers to keep their plans to themselves and to be wise in living there. They were not to talk of "judgements, neither boast of faith nor of mighty works" (as they had done in Jackson County), but were to prepare quietly for the day to return to their lands across the river (D&C 105:23-24).

Joseph Smith left Clay County in July 1834 after establishing the Church's second stake there and appointing David Whitmer as its president. Then, at Kirtland he developed plans for the "little season" of preparation. He instructed Partridge and Phelps to take the initiative in the West in encouraging the eastern Saints to gather to Clay County. The Prophet spoke of the two years which was provided for the "little season" of preparation. The day he set for them to return to Jackson County was 11 September 1836-the appointed date for the "redemption of Zion," he wrote. [^40] Moreover, he said that would be the time when they could petition Governor Dunklin for a military escort, as the governor had offered. To bolster the courage of the exiles and to strengthen their resolve, Joseph declared that they should commit themselves to that date and to "live or die" as they returned to Jackson County. [^41] To help build a strong presence in Clay County through gathering, Phelps wrote a series of articles in 1834 and 1835, which appeared in the essenger and Advocate t Kirtland, and were circulated throughout the Church. Starting slowly, the immigration became substantial by 1836. Immigrants purchased land in Clay County or rented it, and many waited for the "little season" of preparation to end.

The year 1835 served as a time of healing from former troubles, but in 1836 the surge of increased numbers in Clay County began to awaken resentment. Even some of the citizens who had been helpful to the Saints objected to their expanding numbers there. Called "Jack Mormons," these friendly citizens had themselves sometimes suffered ridicule or hostility because of their helping the Saints. Now some of them became concerned over what they viewed as an excessive enthusiasm by the Latter-day Saints for Clay County, which place they had understood was only to be a temporary home for them. [^42]

As the gathering heightened, some Saints did not follow counsel and were viewed as speaking with inordinate zeal for their home in exile. Friendly Joseph Thorpe lamented over what he saw as boasts of some of the Saints.["[The Latter-day Saints,] with all their experience in Jackson, began to tell the citizens of Clay the same old tale; that this country was theirs by gift of the Lord, and it was folly for them to improve their lands, they would not enjoy the fruits of their labor; that it would finally fall into the hands of the saints." [^43] After reviewing a conversation with a zealous Latter-day Saint, Thorpe unsympathetically reflected: "This kind of talk, with their insolence and impudent behavior, so enraged the citizens that they began to consult about the best course to take to rid themselves of a set of religious fanatics." [^44] Thus, lingering dissatisfaction by some old settlers of Clay County erupted into animosity against the Latter-day Saints.

Hostilities

Adverse sentiment heightened by late spring 1836. On 29 June, friendly leading citizens at Liberty held a public meeting to help prevent violence by issuing suggestions and where they felt needful to file complaints against the Latter-day Saints. "Their rapid emigration," the committee report said of the Saints, "their large land purchases," and their claims that Clay County was "destined by heaven to be theirs" were some of the objections they noted. The list included also that the cultural differences of the Saints offended the people. This consisted of the fact that they were "eastern men" and "non-slave holders"; moreover, they were keeping up "communication with the Indian tribes" and held a belief in certain objectionable "religious tenets," the report stated. [^45]

But the leading citizens at the meeting and hundreds of other participants or spectators-some riotous ones-met and filed their complaints only after belligerent groups had organized and violence had begun in the eastern part of the county. This outbreak of violence and the fact that the Saints intended to protect themselves and perhaps shed blood in doing so created a threatening atmosphere that prompted the meeting. [^46]

Because of its frontier character, Clay County required able bodied men over eighteen to work several days a year in building and improving public roads and bridges. This forced-labor it- self often made the men ill-tempered and quarrelsome. Large companies of Latter-day Saint immigrants traveling on county roads sometimes aroused workers to a pitch of "much excitement," stated one worker, who viewed the Latter-day Saint immigration with contempt. [^47] [^48] Nathan Tanner Porter stated that the "rapid increase of the Saints" was the issue that enraged the people against them. The Latter-day gathering caused some to fear that the citizens would lose political control of the county, Porter said. [^49] Watching the immigration irritated the road workers and prompted some of them and others to organize against the Saints. Wilson wrote of an anti-Mormon planning meeting he attended:

We were gathered together as one man. The Preachers of the Gospel were there, the Elders of the Churches was there, the gray headed fathers were there the Beardless Boys were there and the Memorable Spirit of 76 was there. We then appointed a committee of 7 persons to draw up a preamble and resolutions declaring the intention of our meeting and what coarse we intended to pursue which was in effect to drive [the Mormons] away if they would not go without, and ordered the same to be published in the far west. [^50]

Groups such as this elected delegates for the public meeting at Liberty to consider the Saints' departure from the county. Before that meeting was held hostilities erupted. On the night of 28 June, "several outrages" were committed against the Saints, said Wilson. Among the violence, a mob attacked a settlement of Latter-day Saints in the eastern part of the county southwest of the Fishing River, which harassed many and whipped one man nearly to death. [^51]

Mob surveillance over the county roads attempted to keep immigrating Saints in check. One group late in June, lead by Jesse Clark, turned back a large arriving caravan of immigrating Latter-day Saints from the East as it entered Clay County. Lyman Wight took the offensive against Clark's regulators. He organized Latter-day Saint defenders at their villages near the Fishing River and went out with 250 men to confront Clark and thereby protect the roads for the Latter-day Saint travelers, but the two forces never met. Hostile conditions continued. The feelings of some old settlers became so inflamed against the Saints, Wilson wrote, that he, his two brothers, and six other angry citizens pledged to "fight by each others Side & die," if necessary, to drive the Saints away. [^52]

To prevent a war from erupting, leading citizens called for the 29 June meeting at Liberty. While the meeting was itself was sometimes hostile and inflammatory, the cooler-headed members, Chairman, John Thornton, and other men in the committee William T. Wood, Alexander Doniphan, David R. Atchison, James T. Thompson-all sympathetic to the Saints-and others, drafted a conciliatory appeal to have the Latter-day Saints act to prevent the county from being ignited "into all the horrors and desolations of a civil war." [^53]

The committee report listed the objections the citizens had against the Saints, as noted above. It reminded the Saints that they had been expected to use Clay County only as a "temporary asylum." Finally, while acknowledging that it had no power to expel the Saints, the committee issued a request: "We, therefore, in a spirit of frank and friendly kindness, do advise them to seek a home where they may obtain large and separate bodies of land and have a community of their own." [^54]

Leaders of the Church in Clay County on 1 July 1836 defended themselves against their critics, but agreed to leave the county. Such a decision this time would allow them a period of preparation before their departure. Phelps acknowledged a willingness to leave "for the sake of friendship and to be in a covenant of peace." [^55] Church leaders offered to stop immigration and to find a new home for their people north of Ray County, which in fact they had already considered that spring. At Kirtland, Joseph Smith ratified the decision to leave. He wrote on 25 July: "Be wiseÂ�and preserve peace with all men." [^56]

Meanwhile, the Saints again appealed to Governor Dunklin. This time they asked him to protect them from the mob that now existed in Clay County inasmuch as he had told the Saints earlier that he could quell an active mob when one existed. Bishop Partridge believed that the governor, indeed, now had such a mob to deal with, perhaps one sufficiently powerful and active even to petition the president of the United States for help. [^57] Notwithstanding, having recently failed to get assistance from the legislature in an attempt to help the Latter-day Saints, the governor was unwilling to act without greater public support. His reasoning this time was not that he lacked the authority to act, but that the Saints themselves, he felt, should not contribute to social disorder by being so out of line with popular opinion. He said that in some cases "public sentiment may become paramount law." He felt that the Saints had an obligation toward other citizens not to excite their neighbors to hostility. "If you cannot do this," he wrote to Phelps, 18 July 1836, "all I can say to you is that in this Republic the vox populi is the vox Dei." [^58]

That was a day of majority rule rather than minority rights if minority conduct seemed objectionable enough. In the case of the Saints, Governor Dunklin placed the popular will of the people as his higher duty; beneath that was his commitment to enforce the law. Some historians have observed that the Jacksonian social structure generally was unfriendly to objectionable minority rights. [^59] In fact, some minorities were viewed as the "antithesis of American ideals," wrote David Brian Davis. [^60] (The Jacksonian American ideals were often reflected in separation of church and state, individualism instead of collectivism, and avoidance of certain forms of separatism. Where such conventional values seemed sufficiently violated (as it was seen to be by the Latter-day Saints), citizens sometimes justified corrective violence on grounds of preserving what they believed was the American way. Governor Dunklin gave into that prevailing view and failed to help this unpopular minority.

This left the Latter-day Saints in Clay County bereft of certain rights; the most important one was to return to build their Zion in Jackson County. While it is true that their experience in Clay County was not a passive one, for they asserted themselves there to reclaim Zion and even to extend her borders, in doing so they remained peaceful. Nevertheless, their sojourn there was spent partly in anguish and was a failed effort to fulfill their unique form of religious and social life; and as such it provided another link in their ongoing chain of distress in Missouri.
