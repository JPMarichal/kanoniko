# Messengers of Peace in the Latter Days

### Messengers of Peace in the Latter Days
Victor L. Ludlow, Unlocking Isaiah in the Book of Mormon (Salt Lake City 2003)
 
 

Messengers of Peace in the Latter Days
 

3 Nephi 20/Isaiah 52
 

In the last days, Zion shall return and Israel be redeemed— Messiah shall deal prudently and be exalted
 

Isaiah 52 is a profoundly significant chapter in which Isaiah speaks to the house of Israel in the latter days. It is hopeful and triumphant, describing the events of the last days leading up to the eventual exaltation of Israel and the second coming of Christ. Its significance is established by the fact that in addition to the Savior, at least four Book of Mormon prophets quote portions of this chapter: Nephi, Jacob, Abinadi, and Moroni.1 Seventy-five verses of additional scripture refer directly to or expound upon this chapter, and many other verses relate to it indirectly. Most important, the resurrected Savior Himself cites the words of Isaiah 52 to teach the Nephites about covenant promises with Israel in the last days. Indeed, this is the most quoted chapter of Isaiah in the Book of Mormon. The main block of the chapter is found in 3 Nephi 20:32–45, within the last major sermon of Christ to the people at Bountiful. This passage and other segments of Isaiah 52 recorded throughout the Book of Mormon are outlined in the chart below:

As will be seen in the later discussion in this chapter, Christ’s commentary on this material not only stresses its importance but also gives fuller understanding of its messages.
 

The Savior uses the words of Isaiah during the second day of His ministry in the Americas to stress that when Isaiah’s prophecies are fulfilled, the Father’s covenant with Israel will also be fulfilled (3 Nephi 20:11–12). Thus, Isaiah’s words truly are significant to our day—because God’s covenant with Israel will not be accomplished until Isaiah’s prophecies are realized.
 

Following this important disclosure concerning the covenant relevance of Isaiah’s words, the Savior teaches about different aspects of the covenant remaining to be fulfilled, culminating with the gospel restoration. He then quotes most of Isaiah 52, arranging the verses in a slightly different order than found in Isaiah:

The different order of the verses does not detract from their meaning, so we will discuss them in the order presented by the Savior.
 

3 Nephi 20:32–35 (Isaiah 52:8–10): “Sing together, . . . for the Father . . . hath redeemed Jerusalem”
 

Before the Savior starts quoting from Isaiah on the second day of His resurrection ministry in the land of Bountiful, He reminds the people of the words of Isaiah that He cited the previous day (3 Nephi 20:10–11; 16:17–20). Those words from Isaiah 52:8–10 had confused them, so He had commanded them to go to their homes and ponder the things He had told them (3 Nephi 17:2–3). Now on the second day, it appears that He is again going to quote Isaiah, but He merely reminds them that Isaiah’s words are written: “Ye have them before you, therefore search them” (3 Nephi 20:11; compare 3 Nephi 23:1). He could give us exactly the same instruction today: “You Latter-day Saints have the words of Isaiah; they are written in the Bible, and many of them are in the Book of Mormon; therefore search them!”
 

The Savior then gives an important reason why His people should search Isaiah’s words: “When they shall be fulfilled then is the fulfilling of the covenant which the Father hath made unto his people, O house of Israel” (v. 12). In other words, when the words of Isaiah are fulfilled, then God’s covenant with the house of Israel is fulfilled. Moses had brought the house of Israel into a covenant relationship with his instructions recorded in the book of Deuteronomy. But in order for the covenant to be completed, certain conditions and prophecies needed to be fulfilled—and these are recorded in the book of Isaiah. Deuteronomy is the covenant contract with Israel; Isaiah is the covenant promise.
 

The Savior next reviews some elements of Israel’s gathering that must take place (vv. 13–14). He calls upon the Gentiles to repent after they receive the gospel blessing; if they refuse, they will face God’s justice and the power of His covenant people (vv. 15–20). He promises them a New Jerusalem and His presence in their midst (vv. 21–22). He reminds His audience that all the ancient prophets had foretold His coming (vv. 23–24). He continues with a summary of His prophetic and historic relationship with covenant Israel, including an explanation of why the gospel went first to the house of Israel and then to the Gentiles and why Israel was scattered by the Gentiles (vv. 25–28). He assures them that He will remember His covenant promises with them and that they will eventually receive the fullness of His gospel (vv. 29–30). And when they will believe in Him, that He is Jesus Christ (v. 31), then the words of Isaiah will be fulfilled. He then quotes the same three verses from Isaiah 52:8–10 that He cited at the end of the previous day. These verses promise Christ’s peace and power, and they portray the Lord’s messengers taking the gospel to the world.
 

Isaiah addresses the vital role of missionary work by using a metaphor of a watchman upon a mountain. The work of the singing watchmen is to share the peace and joy that the gospel message brings (vv. 32, 34, 40). The watchmen, working as the Lord’s messengers, might be prophets, General Authorities, full-time missionaries, or local leaders and members. They all serve as Christ’s representatives as they take His joyous word to the world.
 

Isaiah speaks directly to his audience in verse 34, commanding them to “break forth into joy [and] sing together.” This direction to “sing” not only refers to the act of making music but also suggests that the Lord wants us to joyfully proclaim the gospel in the latter days. Isaiah’s words of cheerful encouragement to faithful missionaries are similar to the promise given in D&C 18:15: “If it so be that you should labor all your days in crying repentance unto this people, and bring, save it be one soul unto me, how great shall be your joy with him in the kingdom of my Father!” We should remember that we are all called as missionaries of the gospel—whether we serve a full-time mission or not. We should never forget the encouraging words of President David O. McKay: “Every member a missionary!”
 

In verse 35, Isaiah alludes to the great and dreadful calamities of the Second Coming and describes the Lord making “bare his holy arm in the eyes of all the nations.” Other scriptures use similar imagery associating the Lord’s destructive power with the symbol of His arm.2 By using imagery that depicts the power of the Lord, Isaiah warns his readers: God is a real, physical power to contend with, so we should heed His commandments or we will feel the power of His judgments!
 

The text in verse 35 contains interesting changes from Isaiah’s text in the Bible (Isaiah 52:10). In the Book of Mormon, the title “Father” replaces the titles “Lord” at the beginning and “God” at the end of the verse, and a phrase is added: “And the Father and I are one.” The First Presidency taught that this is an example of Jesus Christ teaching of His authority by divine investiture from the Father. The Son has received full power from the Father, making appropriate Christ’s title as the “Father” of humankind: “Thus the Father placed His name upon the Son; and Jesus Christ spoke and ministered in and through the Father’s name; and so far as power, authority, and godship are concerned, His words and acts were and are those of the Father.”3 Everything Jesus did on the earth was in His Father’s name, by His Father’s will, and through His Father’s power.
 

3 Nephi 20:36–38 (Isaiah 52:1–3): “Awake, . . . and put on thy strength, O Zion”
 

These verses foretell a triumphant time when the people of Zion and Jerusalem will be restored to power, particularly priesthood power, and will be free from oppression through the redeeming powers of Christ. Verse 36 proclaims this joyful future of Israel when only the righteous and pure will dwell in Jerusalem. The phrases “put on thy strength, O Zion” and “put on thy beautiful garments, O Jerusalem” illustrate promises connected to priesthood blessings, especially associated with the temple. The Prophet Joseph Smith clarifies these phrases in a question-and-answer section of the Doctrine and Covenants. Zion putting on her strength refers to people putting on the power and authority of the priesthood (D&C 113:7–8).
 

The imagery of beautiful clothing has various implications, alluding to modern-day ceremonial temple garments as well as to new figurative clothing of righteous followers. The Lord commands the Latter-day Saints, “Take upon you my whole armor, that ye may be able to withstand the evil day” (D&C 27:15). Jacob promises the faithful that they can wear more comfortable attire as they are clothed with “the robe of righteousness” (2 Nephi 9:14). Isaiah promises that if we remain faithful and true, our battle gear will be replaced with magnificent garments. The righteous among us will also be clothed in glory as we receive our heavenly rewards. In addition, the image of clothing also suggests the significance of our external, visible behavior. For example, modest clothing reflects our inner commitment to respect ourselves as children of God. Both our clothing and our behavior reveal to others the principles that we represent. Our “spiritual attire” should reflect the saintly person we wish to represent as covenant followers of Christ.
 

In verse 37, Isaiah describes the liberation of the Israelites and implores his audience, “Shake thyself from the dust; . . . loose thyself from the bands of thy neck.” Zion loosening her bands refers to the scattered remnants of Israel returning to the Lord (D&C 113:9–10). The metaphor of the captive being freed refers not only to the literal freeing of the children of Israel from their political captors and scattered condition but also the spiritual freeing of all mankind through the atonement of the Savior. This redemption from sin is further described in verse 38: “Ye have sold yourselves for naught; and ye shall be redeemed without money.” The children of Israel had indeed sold themselves “for naught,” making their souls the ransom for sin and condemning themselves to torment and hell as they awaited redemption. It is only through the Savior’s complete and infinite sacrifice that such sin-indebted souls can be redeemed. Further, because of Christ’s mercy, the transaction requires no real payment from the sinner, and the soul is “redeemed without money.” As sinners, we are truly indebted to the Lord for His mercy.
 

3 Nephi 20:39–40 (Isaiah 52:6–7): “How beautiful upon the mountains”
 

The Savior, still quoting Isaiah, now describes the personal relationship He will establish with His people and the peace and power that His gospel will bring in the last days. He promises that the children of Israel will know His name and understand that He is the source of their comfort. Through the trials and tribulations of life and in spite of weaknesses and failures, He brings peace into our lives.
 

The message of His gospel’s peace continues in verse 40, where the Lord’s messengers are portrayed as “beautiful” servants who bring “good tidings.” They are depicted as standing on a mountain, which, like most of Isaiah’s imagery, does not symbolize just a mountain. Other scriptures give us increased insight into this symbolism. For example, Isaiah uses the imagery of the mountain of the Lord’s house to represent the temple (2 Nephi 12:2/Isaiah 2:2). He talks about people from throughout the world coming to the tops of the mountains, such as during general conference, to receive instruction from the Lord and His servants4 (2 Nephi 12:3/Isaiah 2:3). Isaiah also talks about lifting a banner or ensign upon a mountain, symbolizing the taking of the gospel to the peoples of the world (2 Nephi 23:2/Isaiah 13:2; Isaiah 18:3). Likewise, in his interpretation of King Nebuchadnezzar’s dream, the prophet Daniel presents a comparable metaphor, describing a “stone . . . cut out of the mountain” that “shall break in pieces and consume all [other] kingdoms” (Daniel 2:44–45). This “stone” from the mountain is representative of the work of the Church. Joseph Smith elaborates on the church/mountain image, explaining that the “stone” will “roll forth, until it has filled the whole earth” (D&C 65:2). Thus, the watchmen or servants upon the mountain are doing much more than just standing on the mountain. From their elevated position of gospel understanding and testimony, they share the gospel light and truth with the world as they add to the stone cut out of the mountain. We, as the faithful watchmen, have the unique latter-day opportunity to assist in its progress and growth down the mountain.
 

Elder Jeffrey R. Holland teaches that the good tidings shared by the watchmen refer to the “divine source” of the “comforting latter-day direction” we receive in general conference. He testifies that, in this “troubled world,” we can receive the blessings of “peace and good tidings” only through the “Only Begotten Son of God Himself.” He points out that the slight variation of this verse in Mosiah 15:18 refers to Christ as the “founder of peace.” This change is significant, because Elder Holland concludes, “Ultimately it is Christ who is beautiful upon the mountain.”5 Fundamentally, the Lord is the ultimate messenger of the good news of the gospel, for He is the good news Himself!
 

3 Nephi 20:41–42 (Isaiah 52:11–12): “Be ye clean that bear the vessels of the Lord”
 

This passage warns the Lord’s servants to be pure and prepared, promising the Lord’s presence among them if they are pure. Modern prophets comment often on these verses because of their pertinent message for Latter-day Saints. Elder Henry B. Eyring clarifies the importance of purity, stating that “Be ye clean” is a commandment, not a suggestion.6 Elder Mark E. Peterson similarly comments, “If you believe in Christ, touch not the unclean thing!”7 Clearly, Isaiah’s insistence of cleanliness among those that “bear the vessels of the Lord” is applicable to us today.
 

Elder Jeffrey R. Holland further clarifies the meaning of verse 41, describing the symbolism of the phrase “bear the vessels of the Lord”:
 

Let me tell you what that phrase “bear the vessels of the Lord” means. Anciently it had at least two meanings, both related to the work of the priesthood.
 

The first refers to the recovery and return to Jerusalem of various temple implements that had been carried into Babylon by King Nebuchadnezzar. In physically handling the return of these items, the Lord reminded those early brethren of the sanctity of anything related to the temple. . . . They themselves were to be as clean as the ceremonial instruments they bore (2 Kgs. 25:14–15; Ezra 1:5–11).
 

The second meaning is related to the first. . . . The Apostle Paul, writing to his young friend Timothy, said . . . , “If a man . . . purge himself [of unworthiness], he shall be a vessel . . . sanctified, and meet for the master’s use, and prepared unto every good work.” Therefore, Paul says, “Flee . . . youthful lusts: . . . follow righteousness, . . . call on the Lord out of a pure heart” (2 Tim. 2:20–22; emphasis added).
 

In both of these biblical accounts the message is that as priesthood bearers not only are we to handle sacred vessels and emblems of God’s power—think of preparing, blessing, and passing the sacrament, for example—but we are also to be a sanctified instrument as well. Partly because of what we are to do but more importantly because of what we are to be, the prophets and apostles tell us to “flee . . . youthful lusts” and “call on the Lord out of a pure heart.” They tell us to be clean.8
 

Elder Holland clearly echoes Isaiah’s message of leaving uncleanliness and being clean, especially for those who are servants in the Lord’s work.
 

President Ezra Taft Benson offers another valuable insight into verse 41. He uses the verse to teach the youth of the Church about morality. He encourages young men to follow the example of Joseph in Egypt, who “maintained his purity and virtue” when faced with temptation. He encourages us to identify what “the lusts of your eyes” (Alma 39:9) means today, and to forsake media that is “suggestive,” “lewd,” “obscene,” or “pornographic.” He exhorts, “We counsel you . . . not to pollute your minds with such degrading matter, for the mind through which this filth passes is never the same afterwards.”9 Like the modern-day prophets who instruct us to turn away from filth, Isaiah urges his readers to remain pure in thought and action. He is indeed a prophet for all ages.
 

Verse 42 contains directions for the final gathering of the chosen people, instructing them to depart from their impure regions in preparation for the coming of the Savior. The command for the Israelites to escape their wicked residences is very similar to directions given by John in his apocalyptic revelation. John describes the exodus of the Saints from Babylon, writing, “I heard another voice from heaven, saying, Come out of [Babylon], my people, that ye be not partakers of her sins, and that ye receive not of her plagues” (Revelation 18:4).
 

It is noteworthy that in Isaiah’s directives the people are told to remove in an orderly manner, “not . . . with haste, nor . . . by flight” (3 Nephi 20:42; Isaiah 52:12). Perhaps the most enlightening explanation of this instruction was given by the “Presiding Elders” of the early Church. In July of 1833, they issued counsel to the Saints who were fleeing Missouri. This was a difficult time for the Saints. It was foolish and vain, the elders said, for the Saints to suppose that the Lord would miraculously provide food, clothes, and shoes for them along the way. The elders, however, did not “doubt in the least that the Lord [would] provide for His Saints in these last days.” They promised to help as much as possible and extend their hands “to steady the ark” during the arduous journey. They maintained, however, that each Saint is a steward over what the Lord has given him or her, and that they must not be “imprudent, or lavish, or negligent, or indolent” in doing their part. The difficulties of their gathering, they said, “has taught us to revere that sacred word from heaven, ‘Let not your flight be in haste, but let all things be prepared before you.’ (D&C 133:15).” Their comments help us better understand what the Lord means when He tells His followers that their departure from Babylon be not “with haste.”10 We must always trust in the Lord and His power to help us, but we must also be prepared and do our best as we leave the worldly culture of today’s Babylon and gather within the security of Zion. With the directions of living prophets and the Holy Ghost, we can remain calm and organized in times of turmoil and uncertainty.
 

3 Nephi 20:43–45 (Isaiah 52:13–15): “Behold, my servant shall deal prudently”
 

The final verses in this passage describe a great servant of the Lord who will miraculously serve among the leaders of the earth. Though Isaiah describes certain characteristics of the divine messenger with a degree of accuracy, there are various possibilities for who the servant represents. Some scholars suggest that Isaiah was referring to Christ; others think Isaiah may have been prophesying about his own martyrdom. Some LDS teachers believe the servant is a latter-day prophet like Joseph Smith or the prophet in office when the Savior returns. And still others propose that the servant represents not just one man but the whole house of Israel or The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints as a body. Isaiah, in his multifaceted prophecies, may have been referring to any or all of the above.
 

Isaiah describes the servant as being prudent in judgment, praised and extolled by God, and exalted above other men (v. 43). The servant, however, will experience many hardships, and both his countenance and form will be “marred, more than any man” (n. 44). The original Hebrew word for “marred” suggests that this servant will be disfigured, marked, or scarred, perhaps through a natural blemish or through an accident, illness, or abusive action. However, it could refer to a severe personal or inner emotional distress that would also be visible upon the person’s face. In either case, Jesus is already an example of one who was physically abused, marked, and scarred by His enemies,11 and He was also a man acquainted with grief and sorrow, especially in His suffering at Gethsemane (Mosiah 14:3–5). These attributes could apply to others of the suggested servants, and they may pertain to more than one.
 

The great missionary work to be carried out by the servant is described in verse 45: “That which had not been told [the kings] shall they see; that which they had not heard shall they consider.” Both of these phrases imply that the “sight” of the nations will be restored in the latter days, and all will come to see and understand the teachings of the gospel and the divinity of the Savior. Elder Neal A. Maxwell explains: “Having been given so many marvelous truths, we are to share them in order that ‘wise men and rulers may hear and know that which they have never considered.’ (D&C 101:94; see also 2 Ne. 20:45; 21:8). So much of the gospel we bring is what people have ‘never considered’ and ‘never had supposed.’ (Moses 1:10.)”12
 

After quoting these passages from Isaiah, the Savior Himself promised that the things prophesied in them will surely come to pass. He continues with a promise that when these words of Isaiah are fulfilled, then the Father’s covenant with His people will be fulfilled, and they will be established in their lands of inheritance (3 Nephi 20:46). The wonderful covenant promises given to Abraham almost four millennia ago will finally be accomplished as a numerous people will live in their chosen land where they can be a blessing to other nations and peoples (Genesis 12:1–3; 17:1–8). As latter-day heirs of the covenants made with Abraham, we need to be involved in this marvelous work. As pure messengers of truth, we share a wonderful opportunity for significant service while we build His kingdom of peace. The Savior’s instructions to us, His covenant people of the latter days, provide understanding of Isaiah’s words and give us incentive to work toward their fulfillment.
 

After quoting most of Isaiah 52, the Savior gives us a sign so that we can “know the time when these things shall be about to take place” (3 Nephi 21:1). The sign is the coming forth of the Book of Mormon from the Gentiles unto the descendants of His Nephite-Lamanite audience (vv. 2–6). When the sign is given, their seed will know that the Father’s work has already commenced toward the fulfillment of His covenant with the house of Israel (v. 7). Wonderful works will come forth, including divine protection and healing for the marred servant referred to earlier (vv. 8–10).13 The Savior proceeds with severe warnings for the Gentiles, including His covenant people coming among them as a lion among sheep (vv. 11–21). He invites the Gentiles to join the remnants of Jacob in building the New Jerusalem (vv. 22–24). He promises the power of heaven and His presence in their midst as His work is established among all the dispersed of His people (vv. 25–28). He concludes with a promise that His Father’s people, the children of Israel, will “go out from all nations” but not in haste, nor by flight, for the Father and the Son will go with them (v. 29).14 The Savior then quotes all of Isaiah 54, which will be discussed in the next chapter.
