# Sacred Records from the Dust

### Sacred Records from the Dust
Victor L. Ludlow, Unlocking Isaiah in the Book of Mormon (Salt Lake City 2003)
 
 

Sacred Records from the Dust
 

2 Nephi 26, 27/Isaiah 29
 

Nephites shall speak as a voice from the dust— The apostasy, restoration of the gospel, and coming forth of Book of Mormon are foretold
 

In this famous chapter of latter-day prophecy, Nephi quotes Isaiah’s description of sacred records coming forth as a voice from “out of the dust” (2 Nephi 26:16) and provides insights into how these records will be received in latter days. These records signify the opening of a notable period in the world’s history, when the gospel of Jesus Christ is brought forth and reinforced through various ancient religious writings. 
 

The following phrases from the Book of Mormon rendition of Isaiah 29 are often cited in Latter-day Saint literature and church meetings: “a marvelous work and a wonder” (27:26); “speech [or records] . . . out of the dust” (26:16); “words of the book which were sealed” (27:11); “three witnesses . . . shall testify to the truth of the book” (27:12); and “people draw near unto me with their mouth, and with their lips . . . , but . . . their hearts [are] far from me” (27:25). Obviously, key gospel doctrines and latter day prophecies come from Isaiah 29.
 

The additional material provided by Nephi in 2 Nephi 26–27 makes the Book of Mormon rendition of Isaiah 29 significantly longer than the biblical text. While Isaiah 29 contains only twenty-four verses in the King James Version, Nephi’s version contains fifty-four (2 Nephi 26:15–27:35). Two explanations may account for the difference:
 

• Isaiah’s original record may have been longer than the King James Version is today. Multiple biblical copying and revisions (and even errors) may have resulted in missing portions of the Isaiah text. Nephi’s version of the book of Isaiah, obtained from the brass plates of Laban, was therefore less edited and corrupted than our earliest available copies today, such as the Dead Sea Scrolls. Thus, the more ancient and complete version is recorded in Nephi’s copy of Isaiah’s words.
 

• Isaiah’s record may not have been edited and tampered with substantially and was essentially the same size during Nephi’s day as it is today. Nephi simply elaborated upon Isaiah’s words by writing the additional thirty verses of inspired insight. Thus, the Book of Mormon rendition provides both the original prophetic text accompanied by the revealed commentary of another prophet.
 

Either explanation may be correct, but some combination of the two probably provides an accurate representation of fact. Whichever set of facts best explains how the Isaiah text was expanded in the Book of Mormon, Nephi’s adaptation of Isaiah 29 is highly pertinent to us today, describing events that have, or will shortly, come to pass. With Nephi’s inspired comments, we actually see the prophecy through the perspective of two prophets—Isaiah and Nephi.

2 Nephi 26:14–33 (Isaiah 29:3–5):1 “Their speech shall whisper out of the dust”
 

Isaiah begins chapter 29 by describing terrible destruction that will fall upon Judah (part 1 in the chart above). In Isaiah 29:1, these wicked are described as from “Ariel,” another name for Jerusalem. Thus, the first verses of Isaiah 29 foretell the literal defeat of Judah that occurred in 587–86 b.c. at the hand of the Babylonians and again in 70 a.d. as the Romans destroyed Jerusalem and scattered the Jews. Interestingly, Ariel is not mentioned in the Book of Mormon version of these verses. Rather, Nephi describes events happening to his descendants as they are smitten by the Gentiles in the “last days” (part 2). Nephi next applies Isaiah’s words of Jerusalem’s defeat to the downfall of his Nephite nation, highlighting special records that come forth and contain the words (“speech”) of a beleaguered nation. These words have a familiar message or “spirit” to them because they are the words of God as recorded by the ancient prophets (part 3). These scriptures are collected in a special, sealed book (now known in part as the Book of Mormon). After revealing the Lord’s protection of this sacred, sealed book (part 4), Nephi again uses Isaiah’s words as he describes the sudden collapse of his nation (part 5).
 

Nephi next provides his most extensive commentary, foretelling how Isaiah’s words and other prophetic warnings apply to his people (part 6). For example, verse 20 contains phrases that Nephi probably borrowed from Isaiah: the “stumbling block” imagery is found in Isaiah 8:14 and 57:14,2 and the metaphor of grinding the face of the poor into the ground is recorded in Isaiah 3:15.3 Nephi also testifies that God does not work in darkness. He constantly invites His children to partake of His word and salvation. Here again, Nephi uses some words of Isaiah to reinforce his teachings. In verse 25, he cites Isaiah 55:1, where the Lord says, “Come, buy wine and milk without money and without price.” Nephi provides other divine instruction and concludes with a strong witness that the Lord invites all people “to come unto him and partake of his goodness” (v. 33).
 

2 Nephi 27:1–5 (Isaiah 29:6–10): “A dream of a night vision”
 

With a new introduction (part 7), Nephi looks to the last days after Gentiles and Jews from “all the nations” will have come to America. He identifies this time period as the “days of the Gentiles.” The time or “day” of the Gentile is explained in several other scriptural references, most significantly the Savior’s Olivet discourse.4 In the latter days, the Savior declared, “when the times of the Gentiles is come in, a light shall break forth among them that sit in darkness, and it shall be the fulness of my gospel; but they receive it not; for they perceive not the light, and they turn their hearts from me because of the precepts of men. And in that generation shall the times of the Gentiles be fulfilled” (D&C 45:28–30; italics added).
 

The fulness of the gospel spoken of was initially reserved for the house of Israel, especially first for the Jews in conjunction with the Savior’s first coming. This period of gospel exclusivity that constitutes the “times of the Jews” ended soon after the Savior’s mortal ministry. President Joseph Fielding Smith explains that “the times of the Gentiles commenced shortly after the death of our Redeemer. The Jews soon rejected the Gospel and it was then taken to the Gentiles.”5 Peter’s vision of the unclean beasts and Paul’s subsequent mission to the Gentiles mark the first preaching of the gospel to non-Jews (Acts 10–15). Preaching to those in the Gentile nations continues today. However, the “days of the Gentiles” are now transferring back to the time or day of the Israelites as more descendants of Lehi’s family join the Church in Central and South America.
 

Isaiah elaborates on the events of this latter-day period, describing “thunder,” “earthquake,” “storm,” “tempest,” and “flame of devouring fire” (part 8; v. 2/Isaiah 9:6). These signs, familiar to many as events that will occur prior to the Second Coming, further illustrate that Isaiah’s prophesies are for Saints of the latter days.
 

The wicked who “fight against Zion” will be punished greatly for their unrighteousness. Isaiah describes their punishment in terms of a nightmare (“dream of a night vision” v. 3/Isaiah 29:7), in which they are starving and thirsting yet cannot be filled. They stagger and struggle like drunken men, drunk not with strong drink but with the wickedness of their actions (v. 4/Isaiah 29:8). Elder Bruce R. McConkie suggests that “in a spiritual sense drunkenness means apostasy.”6 Thus, Isaiah’s imagery of the starving, intoxicated man represents the unrighteous, who because of their stubborn wickedness starve for want of spiritual food and drink. They stumble through life, unsure of their spiritual desires or heavenly origin and destiny.
 

Isaiah further elaborates on the unrighteous nightmare, describing the wicked as asleep to the truths of the gospel. In their obstinate pride, they have “closed [their] eyes” to the truths of the gospel and have “rejected the prophets” and “rulers” (v. 5/Isaiah 29:10). This image is similar to one revealed to Isaiah as the Lord calls him and depicts the disobedient who will refuse Isaiah’s words: “[I will] make the heart of this people fat, and make their ears heavy, and shut their eyes—lest they see with their eyes” (2 Nephi 16:10/Isaiah 6:10). In reality, these two prophecies fulfill each other, for those who ignore Isaiah are rejecting the prophets, and those who reject the prophets blind themselves.
 

2 Nephi 27:6–24 (Isaiah 29:11–12): “He shall deliver the words of the book”
 

Following this account of the destruction that will come to the wicked, Isaiah delivers what is perhaps one of the most profound prophecies of his writings—the foretelling of the Book of Mormon and other sacred writings (parts 9–15 on the earlier chart). As in most of his prophecies, Isaiah does not clearly state the specific meaning of the revelation. He does, however, describe some unique aspects of this sealed religious record that help us identify it. Nephi explains that its words will be from those “who have slumbered in the dust” (v. 9). LeGrand Richards teaches that slumbering in the dust implies death—or the buried records of an ancient people. He affirms, “The only way a dead people could speak ‘out of the ground’ or ‘low out of the dust’ would be by the written word, and this the people did through the Book of Mormon.”7
 

Nephi continues, explaining that the “sealed” book will contain a “revelation from God” of things “from the beginning of the world to the ending thereof” (v. 7; see also v. 10). It will be withheld for a period of time, until the Lord sees fit to restore its truth to His children. At the time of its unveiling, men will rejoice, declaring its truths from “the house tops,” reading and communicating its word “by the power of Christ” (v. 11). These prophecies undeniably foretell the coming forth of the Book of Mormon, a document “sealed” as an untranslatable text, a record describing events from the premortal world until the end of time, and a work withheld from humanity until the conclusion of the great apostasy. Today, we rejoice in its truths, “establish [its] word” (v. 14), and declare its prophecies to all nations of the world. Truly, Isaiah’s prophecies speak of events fulfilled in the latter days!
 

The book will have “three witnesses” who will each behold it and “testify to the truth of the book and the things therein” (v. 12). The three witnesses of the Book of Mormon—Oliver Cowdery, David Whitmer, and Martin Harris—did indeed see the plates and other miraculous things revealed “by the power of God” (v. 12). Though each witness left the Church and struggled with Church membership, not one ever denied his testimony of the Book of Mormon or its contents.
 

Perhaps most miraculous in this chapter is the prediction of Martin Harris’s delivering the book to a “learned” man. President Joseph Fielding Smith elaborates on this remarkable prophecy that was recorded in verses 15–21:
 

This prophecy was literally fulfilled when Martin Harris took copies of the engravings of the plates of the Book of Mormon to Professor Anthon in New York. Mr. Anthon answered Martin in almost the language of Isaiah, when he was informed that the book from which the characters were taken was sealed. Said he: “I cannot read a sealed book.” (JS—H 1:63–65). How remarkable it is that Isaiah said that the words of the book were delivered to one who was learned and that the book was delivered to the one who was not learned. How perfectly this harmonizes with the history of the case respecting Mr. Anthon and Joseph Smith!8
 

Isaiah’s prophetic powers are revealed in this remarkably accurate revelation, which, though predicted thousands of years ago, was fulfilled in every detail in latter days.
 

As one reads Nephi’s eighteen verses of prophetic commentary about two short verses of Isaiah, one might wonder, “Why is it so important whether learned or unlearned men can read the words of a ‘sealed’ book?” In fact, it appears that Nephi is telling us that no one, learned or unlearned, can understand the message of the sacred texts. Granted, when one talks of the ancient texts themselves, the unlearned cannot read the ancient languages in which the texts are written. And the scholars, who can read the ancient texts, often lack gospel insights and the prophetic perspectives recorded in the scriptures outside the Bible. Thus, they may be able to read the text, but they do not really understand the spiritual message of the writings. So if the unlearned cannot read these religious records coming “from the dust,” and the learned cannot understand them, what are we to do with them? The revelation given to Joseph Smith about the Apocrypha applies to many ancient religious writings, such as the Dead Sea Scrolls,9 the Ebla tablets, the Nag Hammadi texts, and others: “There are many things contained therein that are true . . . ; there are many things contained therein that are not true. . . . Therefore, whoso readeth it, let him understand, for the Spirit manifesteth truth; and whoso is enlightened by the Spirit shall obtain benefit therefrom” (D&C 91:1–5). Being enlightened by the Spirit is the key! Latter-day Saints who are worthy of the companionship and enlightenment of the Holy Ghost can glean truths from the writings of ancient religious communities. More important, they can understand the message and importance of God’s word as revealed through His prophets, even the difficult words of a prophet like Isaiah. As a help, they can especially gain insight and inspiration from the clear, powerful words of the prophet Nephi. To better understand the writings of Isaiah in 2 Nephi, read carefully the words of Nephi in 2 Nephi 25–33. As one reads and seeks understanding through the Spirit, one can understand Isaiah!
 

2 Nephi 27:25–28 (Isaiah 29:13–17): “I will proceed to do a marvelous work”
 

Isaiah continues his account of the great work of the Book of Mormon, describing its power in dispelling the wicked “precepts of men” (part 16; v. 25/Isaiah 29:13). He explains that though the unrighteous continue in wickedness, “remov[ing] their hearts far from [God]” (v. 25), the truths revealed in the Lord’s “marvelous work and a wonder” will cause worldly “wisdom” to “perish” and man’s “understanding” to “be hid” (vv. 25–26/Isaiah 29:13–14). The coming forth of the Book of Mormon truly did shatter the vain learning of man as it restored the gospel. Isaiah’s prophecy of a “marvelous work and a wonder” thus described more than just a book. Elder LeGrand Richards explains, “What would really constitute a marvelous work and a wonder? . . . In the accomplishment of this promis[e] . . . the Lord had in mind a ‘restitution of all things.’”10 In this sense, the restoration of the gospel—its priesthood, ordinances, and truths—constitutes the marvelous work that Isaiah foresaw.
 

In verse 27, Isaiah again warns the wicked. He describes their secretive acts and the “dark” works that they seek to “hide . . . from the Lord” (Isaiah 29:15.) The Lord, however, declares, “I know all their works” (v. 27). Isaiah further describes the superiority of the Lord over His people, using the image of the people as potter’s clay. This analogy, outlined in Jeremiah 18, describes the children of the Lord as “clay in the potter’s hand.” In Isaiah’s account of the analogy, he describes the wicked who mock the idea that God is shaping their lives. He explains the irony of this situation in which the “work”—such as a clay pot—has come to deny that it was created by the potter, or a framed object refutes the hand of the artist in its framing. God clearly shapes our lives as a potter shapes his pots, and we should give Him credit and thanks for His help.
 

Though the wicked persist in blindly refuting the presence and power of Lord, He will continue to manifest His works on earth. Verse 28 highlights a miraculous blossoming of Lebanon in the latter days (Isaiah 29:17). This prophecy, like that of the Book of Mormon’s coming forth, has been fulfilled in the latter days with the considerable flourishing of regions of the Middle East in the past few decades. President Joseph Fielding Smith explained the early stages in the fulfillment of this prophecy for the area of Palestine and then testified, “This is all in fulfilment of the prophecies.”11 Thus, the powerful hand of the Lord is made manifest to His people in the Middle East. Although many political and social problems remain to be overcome, their recent productivity and prosperity compared to recent historical times is a direct demonstration of God’s goodness.
 

2 Nephi 27:29–35 (Isaiah 29:18–24): “The poor . . . shall rejoice in the Holy One of Israel”
 

Isaiah again refers to the great influence of the prophetic book, describing its power to make the “deaf hear” and the “blind . . . see” (v. 29/Isaiah 29:18). These allusions are especially figurative, as Isaiah is referring to those who are spiritually deaf and blind to the things of the gospel. Elder Bruce R. McConkie elaborates upon this imagery, describing spiritual deafness as “the state of those who are lacking in spirituality, whose spirit ears are not attuned to the whisperings of the still small voice of the Spirit.” He correspondingly explains that spiritual blindness is “the identifying mark which singles out those who are unable to see the hand of God manifest in the affairs of men. Such have ‘unbelief and blindness of heart’ (D&C 58:15); they are ‘hard in their hearts, and blind in their minds.’ (3 Nephi 2:1).”12
 

Isaiah concludes his prophecies of the latter days by again depicting the fate of the meek and the proud. He promises that God’s humble followers will increase and their joy will be in the Lord. Even those who were spiritually impoverished will rejoice in their God (v. 30/Isaiah 29:19). In contrast, he declares that the Lord will ensure that the “terrible one” (that is, the devil and his followers) will be “brought to naught,” the scorners “consumed,” and the iniquitous cut off from God’s presence (v. 31/Isaiah 29:20).
 

Isaiah also describes wonderful blessings of the last days. He prophesies the physical and spiritual return of the house of Jacob, or unrighteous Israel, who had initially rejected the Savior (vv. 33–34/Isaiah 29:22–23). When those who have turned from the gospel see the glorious works of the Lord, they will come to recognize Him as the “God of Israel” (v. 34). For example, the Jews’ eventual recognition of the Savior is described in the Doctrine and Covenants: “Then shall the Jews look upon me and say: What are these wounds in thine hands and in thy feet? Then shall they know that I am the Lord; . . . I am he who was lifted up. I am Jesus that was crucified. I am the Son of God” (D&C 45:51–52).
 

Isaiah explains that more than just the Jews or the house of Israel will come to an understanding of the truth. All who have “erred in spirit” will “come to [an] understanding” of the gospel (v. 35/Isaiah 29:24). Much of this enlightening has already occurred as the Book of Mormon and its gospel precepts have gone forth to all nations. Elder Orson Pratt says that the “blindness of the Christian world . . . ‘shall come to an understanding’ when [the Book of Mormon] comes forth.”13 Missionary work and other means of sharing God’s word will reach more and more of God’s children in these latter days. Thus God’s word shall be wholly fulfilled as it literally goes forth to teach and bless His children with the eternal doctrines of truth and salvation.
 

Isaiah presents various important themes in this chapter, mostly focusing on the significance of the Book of Mormon and its purpose in instructing and redeeming the unrighteous in the latter days.14 Besides opening spiritual ears and eyes, the Book of Mormon increases the number of meek and righteous men and women who praise the Lord and rejoice in His greatness. The Book of Mormon does indeed lead one to righteousness as it teaches the gospel’s truths and the Lord’s magnificence. Ultimately, His revealed insights to Nephi about the book’s power and meaning send a clear message to His audience—read the book’s words and enjoy light, truth, and God’s blessings with the meek, or turn from the book’s message and suffer blindness, ignorance, and God’s judgments with the proud!
