# Zion’s Growth and Israel’s Power in the Latter Days

### Zion’s Growth and Israel’s Power in the Latter Days
Victor L. Ludlow, Unlocking Isaiah in the Book of Mormon (Salt Lake City 2003)
 
 

Zion’s Growth and Israel’s Power in the Latter Days
 

3 Nephi 22/Isaiah 54
 

In the last days, Zion and her stakes shall be established— Israel shall be gathered in mercy and tenderness— They shall triumph
 

The Savior delivered three sermons to the Nephites during His ministry on the American continent. On the first day, He repeated the Sermon on the Mount as found in Matthew and gave a sermon on the fulfillment of the Mosaic law and the Abrahamic covenant. On the second day, He delivered a discourse about covenant people and God’s promises to them for the last days. 3 Nephi 22, which includes all of Isaiah 54, is in the last half of the third discourse (3 Nephi 20:10–23:5). The resurrected Christ places these particular Isaiah prophecies within a latter-day setting. As Elder Bruce R. McConkie explains, Jesus puts Isaiah’s words “in their perspective, in their context.” He continues,
 

[Jesus] had just announced that the restitution of all things was to take place; that the gospel was to come again in its fulness; that Israel was to be gathered; and that the kingdom of God on earth was to be established in the last days. [See 3 Nephi 20–21.] Then he quoted these words from Isaiah, words which are addressed to the Church and which specifically describe the stability, growth, and eventual destiny of the Church.1
 

Thus we see that this chapter is meant specifically for members of the Church today. Its central focus is on the tender and merciful gathering of Israel, the establishment of the people of Zion in their lands of inheritance, and the eventual victory of the righteous over the wicked. In that time of gathering and triumph, lineage Israel will join the Gentiles as a part of covenant Israel. Immediately after the Savior quotes this chapter, He commands us to study the words of Isaiah (3 Nephi 23:1). As a part of our study, we will highlight the poetry and symbolism Isaiah uses to portray this radiant vision of future happiness in Zion, and we will review prophetic commentary about Isaiah’s words.
 

3 Nephi 22:1–3: “Enlarge the place of thy tent, and . . . strengthen thy stakes”
 

In verse 1, Isaiah compares the covenant between the Lord and the house of Israel to a marriage relationship. The barren woman, who represents Israel, has not yet brought forth the full fruits of her covenants with the Lord. Isaiah calls Israel “barren” because of her inability or unwillingness to produce strong spiritual offspring. Because of her wickedness, she has been separated from her husband, the Lord, and has therefore been unable to have children. However, Isaiah tells Israel to “break forth into singing” (v. 1) because many children have been born to the “desolate” woman. The house of Israel has no reason to weep over her lack of children, for the children of the desolate woman will be adopted. This forlorn woman and her relationship to the wife can be understood in two ways: (1) The desolate woman represents the Gentiles, and the barren wife Israel; thus the Gentiles will bring forth greater numbers or spiritual fruits than Israel has delivered. (2) The forlorn woman is Israel in her scattered condition, while the wife is those people remaining in the Holy Land. Thus, the house of Israel will bring forth more children (both physically and spiritually) outside the land of her original inheritance than within. In either case, Isaiah uses these images to symbolize the relationship between the Lord and Israel, promising that many others will become children of that covenant relationship (compare 1 Nephi 21:19–21).
 

The analogy of the barren domestic wife and the fertile foreign woman is similar to the story of Sarah and Hagar, wives of Abraham. Because of her barren state, Sarah gave the “Gentile” (Egyptian) handmaiden Hagar to Abraham. Hagar bore Ishmael and was promised great posterity. Sarah grieved at her own inability to produce children, and the strain between the two women resulted in Abraham’s temporarily casting out Hagar and her son. Because of Sarah’s righteousness, she was later promised that she would be a “mother of nations.” At ninety, she miraculously bore Isaac, through whom the Abrahamic promise of endless posterity was fulfilled (Genesis 17).
 

Similar to Old Testament passages that represent the covenant between the Lord and Israel as a marriage relationship, New Testament sections characterize the Church as a bride and Christ as her husband.2 Christianity soon expanded from its Jewish roots to the larger Gentile community, which provided greater numbers for the early church. Christians soon outnumbered Jews in the world. The Jews were familiar with being outnumbered by their Arab kinsmen in the Near East, so being a minority was nothing new to them. Isaiah alludes to these far greater numbers outside the house of Israel in this verse (v. 1). In other words, the posterity of the “desolate” or abandoned woman (those outside the land or house of Israel) would became greater than the numbers of the “married” or faithful wife (those who stayed in their original lands of inheritance or maintained their identity as descendants of Jacob).
 

Isaiah employs another metaphor in verse 2, commanding the chosen people, “Enlarge the place of thy tent,” or allow for the influx of adopted children (Gentiles) into the house of Israel. He also directs the Israelites to “strengthen th[eir] stakes.” This image of tent stakes has many meanings. Elder Bruce R. McConkie explains, “In prophetic imagery, Zion is pictured as a tent upheld by cords fastened securely to stakes.” In these verses, Isaiah is “envisioning the latter-day glory of Israel, gathered to her restored Zion” (also Isaiah 33:20). These wooden stakes, which stabilize the tent, parallel our modern-day church “stakes,” which help to “sustain and uphold the restored Zion.” LDS stakes organize the expanding Church membership into structured, manageable units that serve as “the rallying points and the gathering centers” for righteous members of the gospel, who represent the scattered remnants of Israel.3 As Israel is gathered to the stakes of Zion, we who are members in them should work to strengthen them for continued expansion and stability. With firmly anchored stakes, the tent of Zion will withstand the storms of adversity in the last days!
 

The natural growth of Israel and the adoption of non-Israelites into the house of Israel is highlighted in verse 3 as Isaiah tells the Israelites that their seed “shall inherit [or dispossess] the Gentiles” and occupy their desolate cities. Isaiah describes this glorious event using imagery of hands “break[ing] forth on the right hand and on the left.” This description is similar to one given in Genesis as Israel blesses his posterity: “Israel stretched out his right hand, and laid it upon Ephraim’s head, who was the younger, and his left hand upon Manasseh’s head, . . . and said, . . . let them grow into a multitude in the midst of the earth” (Genesis 48:14–16). Their promised posterity would be so great that they would “become a multitude of nations” (Genesis 48:19).
 

3 Nephi 22:4–10: “With great mercies will I gather thee”
 

The Lord, through Isaiah, once again instructs the Israelites not to fear their barren state; He compares Himself to a kind husband who will fully forgive His unrighteous and desolate wife. Israel’s barren or forsaken years, though they seem long, will gradually fade from her memory after Zion is established. In verse 5, Isaiah uses a poetic form in which each phrase builds upon the previous one (composite parallelism) to describe several of the Lord’s attributes. He first portrays Him as “thy Maker, thy husband.” Elder McConkie explains the meaning of this phrase:
 

Christ (the Bridegroom) shall claim his bride (the Church), celebrate the marriage supper, and become the Husband of his wife (Isaiah 54:5; Jeremiah 31:32; Ephesians 5:23; Revelation 19:7–9; 21:2). As a Husband he shall deal intimately, with tenderness and compassion, toward the remnant of his people who have returned to enjoy millennial rest with him.4
 

The Lord is compassionate with His people. The remaining phrases in verse 5 are other titles used for Christ: “the Lord of hosts,” “thy Redeemer,” “the Holy One of Israel,” and “the God of the whole earth.” These titles portray varied dimensions of His divine nature and glory.
 

Isaiah describes events precursory to the Second Coming in verse 7, explaining that the house of Israel will experience a period of brief abandonment prior to the final gathering. This prophecy may be referring to the apostasy that preceded the restoration of the gospel as well as the period just before the Second Coming when the Jews seem abandoned by God as the armies of Armageddon come against them. However, Isaiah stresses that Israel’s period of suffering is brief when compared to her time of redemption. Her day of chastisement will be counterbalanced by a year of the Lord’s favor (Isaiah 61:2; 63:4). Likewise, our small time of separation from God is momentary when compared to the vast eternity we can live in His presence. Even though we may deserve divine rejection because of our unworthiness, we can be welcomed back into God’s company because of His “everlasting kindness” (3 Nephi 22:8).
 

Indeed, the final gathering of Israel will happen because of the Lord’s “great mercies” for His children. God’s mercy is an essential element of the restored gospel. If we accept it, the atonement of Jesus Christ provides a way for us to return home, even though we may have departed temporarily from His path.
 

In verse 8, Isaiah uses contrasting phrases (antithetic parallels) to highlight the Lord’s ability to bestow both mercy and vengeance upon His chosen people. He describes Jehovah’s “everlasting kindness,” which will follow His “little wrath.” The Lord’s mercy is everlasting, and His saving atonement is available to all. Isaiah’s portrayal of the Savior’s relationship with the house of Israel reflects both divine mercy and godly justice. Members of the house of Israel can choose to accept the Lord’s compassion or to suffer the consequences of His just wrath.
 

In verse 9, Isaiah reminds his readers of the Lord’s covenants with Israel by comparing them to the covenant between the Lord and Noah. After God cleansed the earth with a flood, He promised Noah that He would never again destroy the earth in that manner: “Neither shall all flesh be cut off any more by the waters of a flood; neither shall there any more be a flood to destroy the earth” (Genesis 9:11). Here Isaiah impresses upon Israel that the promise God made to gather and redeem her is as valid as His promise to Noah. Although the unrighteous children of Israel will experience a short period of destruction, such as a forty-day flood or a period of trial and tribulation, those who are faithful need not fear the wrath of the Lord.
 

Isaiah uses an additional parallelism in verse 10 to contrast the permanence of Jehovah’s promise of mercy with the transience or changing landscape of hills and mountains. Nothing on earth compares to the everlasting state of the Lord. This verse also foretells the earth’s destruction following the opening of the sixth seal. John foretells, “The heaven departed as a scroll when it is rolled together; and every mountain and island were moved out of their places” (Revelation 6:14; compare Isaiah 34:3–4). When Christ comes, the surface of the earth will be restored to its “undivided” appearance before the days of Peleg (Genesis 10:25).5 Joseph Fielding Smith explains that during the earth’s latter-day restoration, there will be “famine, and pestilence, and earthquake in divers places.” In addition, the valleys will rise and the mountains will be “laid low,” and “the land of Zion and the land of Jerusalem shall be joined together, as they were before they were divided in the days of Peleg.”6 Despite the great calamities of the last days, the Lord promises that His kindness and peace will remain with those who follow His ways.
 

3 Nephi 22:11–13: “Great shall be the peace of thy children”
 

In this passage, Isaiah addresses those who are not yet comforted by the Lord’s assurance of mercy and protection. The latter days will be a time of great turmoil, and both the righteous and the wicked will be afflicted. To those who are afflicted and not yet “comforted,” Isaiah promises the magnificent glory of a millennial earth. He describes the beautiful, gem-encrusted architecture of Zion, revealing an image very similar to the one found in Revelation 21:18–21. Indeed, the two prophets seem to be describing the same city: New Jerusalem.
 

Isaiah next promises that the Lord Himself will teach the children of the righteous during His millennial reign. Verse 13 implies an intimate relationship between the people and Christ during the Millennium. If parents will properly teach their children, then their descendants can enjoy the legacy of having Christ Himself as their teacher and leader (D&C 45:58; see also D&C 68:25–28). About this promise, President Spencer W. Kimball says, “Surely every good parent would like this peace for his offspring. It comes from the simple life of the true Latter-day Saint as he makes his home and family supreme.”7 He assures us that this is not too much to ask of any parent.
 

In recent years, President Gordon B. Hinckley has quoted this verse several times. Like President Kimball, he admonishes fathers and mothers to love and respect their children:
 

How much more beautiful would be the world and the society in which we live if every father looked upon his children as the most precious of his assets, if he led them by the power of his example in kindness and love, and if in times of stress he blessed them by the authority of the holy priesthood; and if every mother regarded her children as the jewels of her life, as gifts from the God of heaven who is their Eternal Father, and brought them up with true affection in the wisdom and admonition of the Lord.8
 

President Hinckley then quotes 3 Nephi 22:13/Isaiah 54:13, adding this phrase to the verse: “Great also shall be the peace and gladness of their fathers and mothers.”
 

In a very different application of this verse, Elder James E. Talmage explains that the phrase “taught of God” characterizes true followers of Christ during His mortal ministry. In his discussion of John 6:32–59 (the Bread of Life teachings), Elder Talmage describes the scene: Jewish leaders in the synagogue were saying unkind things about Jesus because He had said He had come down from heaven. They only knew Him as the son of a carpenter, and did not believe that He was more powerful than a mortal man. Jesus advised these rulers to stop their criticism. Elder Talmage explains, “Unless they had been ‘taught of God’ as the prophets had written,” they could not understand or believe His role as their Savior.9 Only those who have studied and received God’s word can understand, and therefore believe, Jesus Christ. Those who internalize the assurances of future beauty, comfort, and peace in these few verses will be able to withstand the trials of the latter days as they are taught from on high.
 

3 Nephi 22:14–17: “No weapon that is formed against thee shall prosper”
 

In verses 14–15, Isaiah promises the blessings of righteousness: continual protection and peace, and freedom from oppression and fear. He uses composite parallelism in verse 14 to stress the safety of the righteous: “thou [shalt] be established,” “thou shalt be far from oppression,” and “thou shalt not fear.” Then, Isaiah describes Satan’s oppressive influence on the earth in the latter days, portraying the devil as a “smith that bloweth the coals in the fire” (v. 16). God is technically the Creator of this wicked blacksmith (He being the Father of all spirits), but this does not mean the Lord creates evil. He is aware of the evil of the devil, but because of His eternal perspective and power, the Lord will eventually triumph over him.
 

The image of the coal-blowing smith can be associated with the image of the refiner’s fire that will purify the righteous in the last days (Malachi 3:2; 3 Nephi 24:2; D&C 128:24). The trials of the Second Coming are described clearly in D&C 128:24: “Behold, the great day of the Lord is at hand; and who can abide the day of his coming, and who can stand when he appeareth? For he is like a refiner’s fire.” The Lord does not condone the devil’s work, but He sometimes allows Satan’s evil actions to fulfill His own plan: to help purify the Saints for their eventual redemption. Thus, although bad things may occur because of Lucifer’s temptations and dealings with humanity, all things will eventually be for our good.
 

Isaiah concludes this chapter with the Lord’s promise that no power will prevail against His people (D&C 71:9–10; 109:25). Indeed, “no weapon that is formed against [the Saints] shall prosper” (v. 17). Although Satan will be allowed free reign on the earth, those that follow the Savior will have peace, comfort, and protection. Elder Neal A. Maxwell testifies of this verse:
 

Yes, “the enemy is combined,” but when we are combined with the Lord’s “chariots of fire,” then “they that be with us are more than they that be with them”! (2 Kgs. 6:16–17.) Furthermore, the divine promise is that no weapon formed against the Lord’s work shall finally prosper. This “is the heritage of the servants of the Lord.” (Isa. 54:17; D&C 71:9.) I so assure, I so testify!10
 

Finally, Isaiah explains that the tongues (or words) of the wicked will be condemned in the latter days. This prophecy parallels Alma’s words in Mosiah 27:31, which foretell that every tongue, both wicked and righteous, will declare that Jesus is Lord of the earth. Although some tongues may deny the Savior’s divinity, all people will eventually recognize and declare His supremacy as the ruler of this earth (Isaiah 45:23).
 

In short, 3 Nephi 22 is a promise to the righteous members of Zion. Its various poetic forms depict the relationship between the Lord and covenant Israel. Like a husband who provides gifts and strengths to his family, the Lord blesses righteous Israel, as seen in the following chart:11

Because the Lord has invited Israel into a covenant relationship, He has an eternal commitment to bless each member of that relationship. As highlighted in this chapter, wonderful blessings await the faithful in the last day. The Savior emphasizes key promises for them as He concludes His public teachings to the Nephites. It is wonderfully appropriate that this is the last Isaiah chapter to be quoted in the Book of Mormon. Christ’s message to His followers comforted them because of the recent persecutions, tribulations, and destructions they had experienced. The promise of a rapidly expanding Zion society strengthened them as they took His gospel to the other inhabitants throughout the land. Soon, His church was established throughout the land, and a Zion people lived in harmony for almost two centuries. We in the last days should also find comfort and strength in the Lord’s promise that Isaiah’s vision will come to pass. When Zion is firmly established and verified at His Second Coming, His kingdom will quickly be established throughout the world for His millennial reign. Sing aloud, O Israel, for He is coming!
