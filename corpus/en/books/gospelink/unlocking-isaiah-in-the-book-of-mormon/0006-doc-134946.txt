# Israel’s Judgment in God’s Court

### Israel’s Judgment in God’s Court
Victor L. Ludlow, Unlocking Isaiah in the Book of Mormon (Salt Lake City 2003)
 
 

Israel’s Judgment in God’s Court
 

1 Nephi 21/Isaiah 49
 

Messiah shall be a light to the Gentiles and shall free the prisoners— Israel shall be gathered with power in the last days— Kings shall be their nursing fathers
 

Nephi’s citation of Isaiah 49 in 1 Nephi 21 builds upon the preceding chapter, addressing scattered Israel and promising a great servant and the assistance of the nations in gathering Israel home. The chapter contains one of Isaiah’s poetic servant songs and describes a righteous man “called . . . from the womb” (v. 1). Although the attributes of this man can be applied to many of God’s prophets, it is the Savior who best fits Isaiah’s beautiful eulogy. Once Isaiah identifies the great servant, he moves into a courtlike format, voicing the complaints and accusations made by the house of Israel before the bar of heaven. The praised servant serves as judge, kindly yet firmly refuting the groundless grievances of the children of Israel who complain that the Lord has broken His contract with the covenant people. He not only promises to fulfill His covenant, but He also prophesies signs and events of its fulfillment.
 

While the message of this chapter was originally addressed by Isaiah to all of scattered Israel, Nephi applies it particularly to his people in their new promised land. With the fulfillment of these promises in the last days, every Latter-day Saint should carefully study this chapter to see how he or she can help bring about the promised gathering of Israel. President Wilford Woodruff mentioned one reason this chapter is important for us:
 

The revelations that are in the Bible, the predictions of the patriarchs and prophets who saw by vision and revelation the last dispensation and fulness of times plainly tell us what is to come to pass. The 49th chapter of Isaiah is having its fulfillment.1
 

As children of God, we need to study and apply the messages of this chapter.
 

1 Nephi 21:1–6: “Thou art my servant, O Israel, in whom I will be glorified”
 

Isaiah begins the chapter by illustrating some attributes of the Lord’s great servant. He uses beautiful poetry in the style of climactic parallelism to portray the servant’s works as well as His efforts in perfecting the servant. As Isaiah announces each of the three stages in the servant’s development, he tells how the Lord has protected or hidden the servant. These attributes describe several of the Lord’s great servants (such as Moses, Isaiah, Jeremiah, John the Baptist, Peter, Joseph Smith, and others) but most closely parallel the life and mission of the Savior. As Isaiah outlines, the Messiah was called before He was born, having His mission established in the premortal existence. His name, Jesus, was known before His birth. His mouth was a “sharp sword” (v. 2) as His teachings and doctrines rebuked those who were unrighteous, unworthy, or unwilling to understand His word. He was hidden in the shadow of God’s hand during His mortal life, as few recognized His true divinity as the Son of God and the Redeemer of mankind. Through His trials and His perfect life, the Savior became a polished shaft and was able to atone for the sins of the world. And He is now concealed in Heavenly Father’s celestial realms, awaiting His time to be brought forth and establish His millennial reign, like an arrow coming forth and hitting its target.

The capstone or climax of this short, profound poem is in verse 3, where the servant is promised that he will be one in whom God “will be glorified.” The Savior’s atoning sacrifice brought forth our deliverance from death and hell as He fulfilled Heavenly Father’s glorious plan of salvation. His culmination of the law of Moses brought forth a new dispensation that offered the gospel to all—Jew and Gentile alike. Thus, Christ served as the ultimate “light to the Gentiles” (v. 6.), offering them the path and guidance needed to return to Heavenly Father. The life, atonement, and resurrection of Christ exalted God supremely, for it is through the sacrifice and redemption of the Savior that the ultimate work and glory of Heavenly Father is fulfilled—to bring to pass the “immortality and eternal life of man” (Moses 1:39).
 

Although Isaiah’s eloquent description refers to the Savior, it is also meant to describe the Lord’s righteous children. We, like the Savior, were called to this dispensation during premortality, long before our births. We serve as mouths for God’s word as we teach and testify gospel truths to all willing to listen. Our sacred covenants and righteous sacrifices are often hidden “in the shadow of [God’s] hand” (v. 2), revealed only to those sensitive to truth and goodness. And we, just as the Savior, are required to experience tribulations in this life, hopefully becoming like the “polished shaft” of Isaiah’s illustration. As we are called forth to serve, we should be like an arrow from the quiver that flies true to its mark. We then reflect glory to God as we emulate Him through our Christlike behavior.
 

The critical element in this process of godly growth and service is to allow God to perfect us like a polished shaft. In order to become pure and exalted as the Savior was, we must undergo the fires of the “furnace of affliction” (1 Nephi 20:10), which will smooth away our flaws, strengthen our weaknesses, and melt away our sins. Joseph Smith, another possible model for Isaiah’s servant song, explains this painful yet fundamental process of becoming a polished shaft:
 

I am like a huge, rough stone rolling down from a high mountain; and the only polishing I get is when some corner gets rubbed off by coming in contact with something else, striking with accelerated force against religious bigotry, priestcraft, lawyer-craft, doctor-craft, lying editors, suborned judges and jurors, and the authority of perjured executives, backed by mobs, blasphemers, licentious and corrupt men and women—all hell knocking off a corner here and a corner there. Thus I will become a smooth and polished shaft in the quiver of the Almighty.2
 

We will each experience hardships, but the key to enduring and overcoming them is to maintain the Prophet’s perspective: the Almighty allows tribulations in our lives so we can eliminate our flaws that we may serve Him and some day return to dwell in His presence.
 

Although the great servant of Isaiah’s chapter is faithful and devoted to the Lord, he becomes discouraged when the people turn from his word. All in God’s work experience such frustration, such as when the Savior is ignored, the Lord’s prophets are scoffed at, or we are rejected for sharing the gospel truths. The servant grieves, “I have laboured in vain, I have spent my strength for naught and in vain” (v. 4). Isaiah responds to the servant in the next verses, speaking as the Lord: “Yet shall [you] be glorious in the eyes of the Lord, and [your] God shall be [your] strength. . . . I will also give thee for a light to the Gentiles, that thou mayest be my salvation unto the ends of the earth” (vv. 5–6). This dialogue between the servant and the Lord is unique, showing the obedience and righteousness of both the Savior and the faithful followers of God. If we are to become true servants of the Lord, becoming “glorious” in His “eyes,” we must act as a light to the Gentiles, bringing forth the gospel in this final dispensation.
 

1 Nephi 21:7–13: “He that hath mercy on them shall lead them”
 

In these verses, Isaiah introduces the court scene that will unfold throughout the rest of the chapter by outlining the obligations and corresponding promises associated with the covenants the Lord has made with the house of Israel. He begins verse 7 by introducing a great servant who will serve as mediator, or judge, of the trial. Isaiah refers to him as God’s qâdôsh, a Hebrew term that roughly translates as “Holy One” or “saint.” Isaiah emphasizes several features of this saint, describing him as a person “whom man despiseth,” “whom the nation abhorreth,” who is “servant of rulers,” whom “kings shall see and arise,” and whom “princes also shall worship.” Although these traits, like the previous servant description in the first verses of this chapter, can be applied to prophets such as Isaiah or Joseph Smith, they most likely refer to the Savior and His mission as Redeemer.
 

Isaiah uses repetitive phrasing (an example of synonymous parallelism) in verse 8 to emphasize that the Lord constantly cares for His chosen people. The declaration “In an acceptable time have I heard thee” parallels the following line, “in a day of salvation have I helped thee.” Clearly, the Lord wishes the house of Israel to know He is a good provider and faithful keeper of His covenant, and He is very aware of His promises to His children.
 

In verses 8–11, several of God’s specific promises to the house of Israel are outlined. Isaiah explains that the righteous covenant keepers will “inherit the desolate heritages” and “feed in the ways.” They “shall not hunger nor thirst” and “by the springs of water shall he guide them.”
 

This imagery of the righteous who “feed in the ways” with “their pastures . . . in . . . high places” (v. 9) conjures up the oft-used metaphor of the Savior as a shepherd with Church members as His flock. In Palestine, the vocation of the shepherd was considered “dignified” and represented an important role in the community’s economy.3 Thus, in describing Himself as a shepherd, the Savior illustrates that He will play a fundamental role in our lives, leading us from the dangers of the world. The suggestion that the grazing land will be in “high places” also indicates the protective care of the Lord’s pasture—in a raised state of righteousness where the faithful sheep will be safe from the “wolves” and “sheep robbers” of the world.
 

Isaiah further promises the covenant children that if they follow the ways of the Lord, they will feel no pain from hunger and thirst, and they will not be scorched by the heat of the sun (v. 10). This is very similar to the protection provided to the children of Israel as they traveled in the wilderness following their exodus from Egypt. The Lord provided water and protection to His children because of their covenant commitments (1 Nephi 20:21/Isaiah 48:21). Like the ancient children of Israel, we will be shielded from terrible hunger and thirst. However, these modern-day threats of starvation may be more spiritual than physical. As Amos, a prophet contemporary with Isaiah, describes, it will be “not a famine of bread, nor a thirst for water, but of hearing the words of the Lord” (Amos 8:11). If we stay close to the teachings of the gospel, we can escape the effects of this famine. As Christ promised the Samaritan woman at the well, we too can drink “of water springing up into everlasting life” (John 4:14).
 

The source of these life-giving waters is “he that hath mercy” (v. 10), who will lead His children. In the same verse, Isaiah makes a corresponding promise that the righteous will be led by “springs of water.” Nephi describes similar imagery in his vision of the tree of life, which depicts springs of water. Nephi explains the significance of this image, writing, “I beheld . . . the fountain of living waters . . . which waters are a representation of the love of God” (1 Nephi 11:25). Thus, using this comparable image in the context of Isaiah’s words, the Lord promises that if the children of Israel are faithful to their covenants, they will be continually guided by the love of Heavenly Father.
 

Isaiah also promises the children of Israel that a great highway through the mountains will be established to lead the children of Israel to exaltation (v. 11). This highway can be either symbolic or literal. A “highway,” or path, has certainly been established for this church through the often daunting and seemingly impassible mountains of the world. The inspired translations of a country boy have been published into more than a hundred languages and distributed to millions; a church that began with a handful of members in a one-room house has become an international phenomenon; and nations once impenetrable have been softened, opening up before faithful and diligent missionaries and other Latter-day Saints. In a literal sense, a highway will be opened up before the house of Israel, as detailed when Zechariah describes the Lord cleaving the Mount of Olives, creating a passageway through which the children of Israel will escape the wrath of the invading nations (Zechariah 14).
 

Isaiah completes his catalog of the Lord’s promises with a jubilant tribute to God’s goodness. The judge or mediator commands, “Sing, O heavens; and be joyful, O earth; . . . and break forth into singing, O mountains” (v. 13). This is a key latter-day promise elaborated upon by the resurrected Savior later in Nephite history (3 Nephi 20/Isaiah 52). By listing the creations of the Lord—the heavens, the earth, and the mountains—Isaiah emphasizes the great power of the Lord and His power to provide all for the house of Israel.
 

1 Nephi 21:14–18: “I have graven thee upon the palms of my hands”
 

The children of Israel, obviously skeptical of the promises outlined in their covenant, bring their complaints before the Lord and ask for an explanation of what they think are broken commitments. Zion’s first complaint is emphasized through a simple synonymous parallelism: “The Lord hath forsaken me, and my Lord hath forgotten me” (v. 14). The children of Israel, blinded by self-pity, truly believe that they have been betrayed by Jehovah.
 

Isaiah (speaking for the Lord) responds to Israel’s outrageous claims of abandonment, using symbolism that compares the Lord to a doting mother. The judge incredulously asks, “Can a woman forget her sucking child?” He answers the obvious question, stressing the Lord’s care: “[Even if the mothers were to] forget, yet will I not forget thee” (v. 15). Clearly, the Lord’s love is incomprehensible—it goes beyond even the love of a mother for her newborn child. The image of mother and child is also significant because it portrays the house of Israel as little children, helpless and dependent upon the Lord for their care.
 

In what is perhaps the most powerful image of the chapter, Isaiah describes the Lord engraving the house of Israel upon the palms of His hands (v. 16). This imagery of the hand is significant, referring to both the crucifixion of the Savior as well as symbolic temple ordinances and sacred promises. The marks in the palms of His hands would distinguish Christ’s supreme calling as He later visited the Nephites (3 Nephi 11:14–15).
 

Isaiah describes some of the Lord’s other promises that assure the house of Israel He has not forsaken them. He compares the righteous to a bride being dressed for her wedding (v. 18). The images of sacred clothing and ornaments are again significant in their allusion to symbolic ordinances and covenants, especially in the temple. We can take counsel from this scripture, preparing ourselves for the coming of the bridegroom by dressing ourselves in appropriate attire. Just as one would never attend a wedding in casual street clothes, we cannot expect to enter the Lord’s kingdom clothed in inappropriate, immodest, or casual “spiritual” attire. President Brigham Young counseled us: “Let our anxiety be centered upon this one thing, the sanctification of our own hearts, the purifying of our own affections, the preparing of ourselves for the approach of the events that are hastening upon us. . . . Seek to have the spirit of Christ, that we may wait patiently the time of the Lord, and prepare ourselves for the times that are coming.”4 It is very important that we prepare ourselves mentally and spiritually for the coming of the Savior!
 

1 Nephi 21:19–23: “I will lift up mine hand to the Gentiles”
 

Israel’s claims of neglect are made even more ridiculous by Isaiah’s account of the great posterity of the children of the Lord. Contrasting images are used in verses 19 and 20 to describe the vast numbers of “unforgotten” children that will crowd the once desolate lands. Descriptions of wasted, empty territory are contrasted with lands too “narrow” to contain the infinite posterity of the righteous Israelites in latter days. The resurrected Savior later amplifies these promises when He cites Isaiah 54 to the Nephites (3 Nephi 22). Clearly, as the judge implies, the Lord has not forsaken His chosen people but has provided for them and their children through many generations.
 

In verse 21 Isaiah voices yet another accusation of the children of Israel as they question why they have been left childless. Ironically, this claim comes just after the Lord has described the future lands of the people, which will be “too narrow by reason of the inhabitants” (or will be full to the point of overpopulation; v. 19; compare 3 Nephi 22:1–3). The Lord patiently responds to Israel’s groundless complaint, promising the raising of a “standard” (v. 22) or banner (the gospel of Jesus Christ) that will gather the sons and daughters of the righteous. This response refers to the latter-day role of Israel’s scattered remnants among the Gentiles in gathering the nations to prepare for the Savior’s return. We are a part of the raising of this great ensign!
 

The redemption of the Israelite children is further emphasized in verse 23 as Isaiah presents a parallel that promises gathering Israel that “kings shall be [their] nursing fathers” and “queens [their] nursing mothers.” Israel’s complaints of barrenness are obviously inaccurate with the Lord’s magnificent promise that they will have not just posterity but royal offspring.
 

In a sense, we are acting as the king “nursing fathers” and queen “nursing mothers” in these latter days, bringing the gospel to the spiritually childlike and ignorant children of Israel who have forgotten their heritage. President Spencer W. Kimball described our role as caretakers of the Lamanite remnant of the ten tribes, explaining, “The brighter day has dawned. The scattering has been accomplished; the gathering is in process. May the Lord bless us all as we become nursing fathers and mothers unto our Lamanite brethren and hasten the fulfillment of the great promises made to them.”5 As bearers of the ensign of the gospel, it is our responsibility to share the gospel and make the house of Israel aware of their covenanted promises. Through our diligence, the lost ten tribes will be gathered and the preparations for the second coming of the Savior will commence!
 

1 Nephi 21:24–26: “I, the Lord, am thy Savior and thy Redeemer”
 

The house of Israel’s final complaint—that Israel has been robbed by the wicked—is refuted by the Lord’s promise of godly vengeance. In an ironic twist of justice, the spoil and captives of Israel’s adversaries will be taken from them and returned to Israel. The wicked oppressors who have attempted to take “the prey” from the Israelites and captivate the “lawful” children of Israel (v. 24) will be forced to eat their own flesh and drink their own blood in retribution for their evil crimes (v. 26). This prophecy will be fulfilled in the end of days as Christ’s millennial kingdom is established.
 

Isaiah completes the chapter by listing several names by which people will know the Savior. He testifies that the God of Israel is the “Lord,” the “Savior,” the “Redeemer,” and the “mighty One of Jacob.” These titles help clarify the glorious mission of the Savior and His magnificent sacrifice for the righteous Israelites. They help focus Israel’s worship on their true God. And, as promised, eventually all flesh will know the Lord of this earth (v. 26; compare Isaiah 45:23).
 

If we follow the counsel of the Lord, allowing Him to mold us into polished servants, acting as a light to the Gentiles, following the call of the Shepherd, clothing ourselves in righteousness, and assisting in the gathering of Israel, we too can partake of the endless posterity and eternal happiness provided by the atonement of our Savior.
