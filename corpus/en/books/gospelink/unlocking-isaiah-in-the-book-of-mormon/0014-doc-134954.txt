# Witnesses of the Messiah

### Witnesses of the Messiah 
Victor L. Ludlow, Unlocking Isaiah in the Book of Mormon (Salt Lake City 2003)
 
 

Witnesses of the Messiah 
 

2 Nephi 18/Isaiah 8
 

Christ shall be as a stone of stumbling and a rock of offense— Seek the Lord, not peeping wizards— Turn to the law and to the testimony for guidance
 

Many of Isaiah’s prophecies recorded in the Book of Mormon deal with the punishments that come upon the unrighteous. Demonstrating mastery of Hebrew language and poetry, Isaiah uses powerful words and symbolic names to promise ultimate judgment to the wicked—a sure consequence unless they turn from their evil ways. In Isaiah 8, he describes flood waters of destruction awaiting them unless they return to the gentle waters of inspiration that the Lord offers. Nephi later uses Isaiah’s message to teach his people what can happen if they turn away from their God. We can apply these teachings to our lives as we learn from God’s witnesses about His judgments and the symbolic meaning of key names and signs.
 

2 Nephi 18:1–4: “Concerning Maher-shalal-hash-baz”
 

Occasionally, the Lord commands Isaiah to deliver messages of current historical importance to the children of Israel. In these verses, Isaiah warns Israel of impending judgments within the context of the Syrian-Israel attack upon Judah, which was discussed in the previous chapter. Against Isaiah’s advice, King Ahaz of Judah had formed an alliance with Assyria, and an imminent Assyrian invasion against Syria and Israel seemed likely. Isaiah addresses these contemporary historical issues with unique messages and signs to the people.
 

In the first verse, the Lord commands Isaiah to write the word Maher-shalal-hash-baz on a large roll. In the ancient Near East, rolls (scrolls) were made by sewing sheets of papyrus or leather together, forming a long, pliable strip suitable for columns of writings.1 When not being read or studied, these long sheets would be rolled up for storage—giving them the name “rolls.” The scroll that Isaiah was commanded to write on was supposed to be large—perhaps like a banner. The banner was to be prepared in the presence of two associates of Isaiah, Uriah (a religious leader) and Zechariah (a political leader (v. 2). These witnesses were called to fulfill God’s divine law of witnesses—a law followed throughout the dispensations. Paul spoke of this law when he wrote, “In the mouth of two or three witnesses shall every word [of God] be established” (2 Corinthians 13:1). This pattern was established to solidify the authority and power of a prophet’s message.2
 

Instead of writing columns of religious text with neat, small letters on the scroll, Isaiah writes a single long message of ten Hebrew consonants. Maher-shalal-hash-baz is not only the longest name in the Bible but is also an unusual set of two phrases, meaning “haste [to the] booty, swift [to the] spoil.” The phrases describe Assyria’s impending plundering in Syria and Israel. In essence, the name means “destruction is imminent,”3 and further understanding of the message is given in verses 3–4. Isaiah prophesies that his wife would conceive and have a son, and that his name would be Maher-shalal-hash-baz. Isaiah then foretells that before his son would be old enough to call out for his mother or father, the riches (“booty”) of Damascus (the capital of Syria) and the good (“spoil”) of Samaria (the capital of Israel) would be taken away by the king of Assyria. As prophesied, the Assyrian king, Pul, led his army in conquering Damascus and invading Samaria. He then returned to Nineveh with slaves, livestock, wealth, and treasures. Isaiah foretold this devastation within three to four years of its occurrence. As his son’s name symbolized, destruction was indeed imminent for the people of Israel and Syria.
 

Although this prophecy was fulfilled more than a century and a half before Nephi included it in his record, a consistent principle is being taught: wickedness leads to destruction.4 Nephi, as a prophet of God, may have foreseen the fate of his people, and he may have used this scripture to teach and warn of the consequence of their disobedience. Like the Israelites of Isaiah’s day, the Nephites were preparing for war against their enemies (2 Nephi 5:4, 14, 25). Unfortunately, later generations of Nephites did not follow the counsel of Nephi and their later prophets, and they, like their fellow Israelites in the Old World, were often afflicted with war and destruction.
 

This passage teaches another important principle: prophecies are often associated with signs and events, not specific dates. Isaiah’s prophetic sign was that before the child could speak, destruction would come to Syria and Israel. In a similar manner, Samuel the Lamanite prophesied signs of the Savior’s birth and death (Helaman 14). Indeed, the scriptures record various prophecies of Christ’s birth and His first coming, and they foretell many signs preceding His second coming. However, these prophecies do not contain exact dates for specific events. It is up to God’s enlightened children to study these passages and to be ready when the events and signs finally come to pass. With this knowledge we can either live righteously and warn others of the coming judgments, or we can experience these judgments ourselves because of our blindness to the signs or our disobedience to God’s laws. As in Isaiah’s time, each hearer of God’s warnings must decide for himself or herself. But remember, as Isaiah warns, destruction is imminent for the enemies of God’s people, and key signs warn of the impending judgment.
 

2 Nephi 18:5–8: “This people refuseth the waters of Shiloah”
 

Continuing his warning of destruction to the Israelites, Isaiah uses symbolic words to illustrate why the Lord is displeased with His people. In verse 6, we learn that the Israelites have refused the waters of Shiloah. Located just outside the city wall, the waters of Shiloah were the main source of culinary water for the inhabitants of Jerusalem in the time of Isaiah.5 Shiloah comes from the Hebrew word shalam, meaning peace, so the valid accusation leveled at the Israelites is that they were rejecting the waters of peace—a term that can be applied to the Savior. Jeremiah, a prophet contemporary with Lehi, spoke of the people’s rejection of the Lord with a message similar to Isaiah’s: “My people . . . have forsaken me the fountain of living waters” (Jeremiah 2:13). In His mortal ministry, Christ invited His listeners, “If any man thirst, let him come unto me, and drink” (John 7:37).
 

The living waters Christ offered are still available today. Like the gentle waters of Shiloah, which are still adjacent to the walls of the Holy City, Christ’s influence is waiting just outside the walls of our homes. However, we need to bring His light and love into our lives. In His own words, “Behold, I stand at the door, and knock: if any man hear my voice, and open the door, I will come in to him, and will sup with him, and he with me” (Revelation 3:20).
 

Because the Israelites rejected the gentle waters of peace, Isaiah warns that the violent floodwaters of war, represented by the raging Euphrates river, would come upon them (vv. 7–8). Like a river filling and overflowing its banks, the Assyrian army would cover “the breadth of thy land, O Immanuel” (v. 8). The term Immanuel, meaning “God with us,” is introduced in the previous chapter as a name signifying God’s protective care. Even when it has been shown that the Israelites have turned from the Lord and chosen an alliance with worldly men, and even in the midst of a prophecy about their impending destruction, the Lord delivers the message that He is still with them, waiting for them to repent and come back to Him. The Lord continues to operate in this way. When we have turned away from Him and then decide to turn back and seek His redemption, He, the source of peace, is there to help us out of our calamity.
 

2 Nephi 18:9–15: “For a stone of stumbling, and for a rock of offense” 
 

Isaiah next warns against unholy alliances: “Associate yourselves, O ye people, and ye shall be broken in pieces” (v. 9). This warning against misplaced allegiances is directed especially to the leaders of Judah who were seeking to make alliances with other nations (such as Assyria). After counseling the Judeans, Isaiah directs his warning to the nations that would attack Judah. The twofold repetition of the phrase “gird yourselves, and ye shall be broken in pieces” emphatically drives home the point that even if Israel’s attackers gird themselves with the strongest armor, they will be broken in pieces and destroyed. He repeats this warning to emphasize the futility of their military preparations against God’s people.6 Continuing the message in verse 10, Isaiah warns that verbal efforts of counseling and speaking together will not prevail against those protected by the Lord.
 

The last words of the warning are a promise, “for God is with us.” The message warns God’s wicked enemies and promises His righteous followers that He is with His people to protect and strengthen them. This message should be a comfort to us, for it reveals that no matter how strong our adversaries are, they will not ultimately prevail against the righteous. Satan and his followers can “counsel together” and “speak the words” of their plans as long as they want, but they will not succeed if we dress ourselves in the “whole armor” of God (D&C 27:15–18). The Prophet Joseph Smith echoed a similar sentiment when he proclaimed,
 

The Standard of Truth has been erected; no unhallowed hand can stop the work from progressing; persecutions may rage, mobs may combine, armies may assemble, calumny may defame, but the truth of God will go forth boldly, nobly, and independent, till it has penetrated every continent, visited every clime, swept every country, and sounded in every ear, till the purposes of God shall be accomplished, and the Great Jehovah shall say the work is done.7
 

No matter what forces combine against those who stand on the Lord’s side, His followers will prevail in the end, for “God is with us.”
 

Departing from prophetic forecasting, Isaiah describes a direct communication he receives from on high that comes with “a strong hand,” which commands him not to fear what man fears, only to fear the Lord (vv. 11–13). Judah’s greatest fear was of conquest by the Syrian-Israel coalition. This fear led King Ahaz to form a confederacy with Assyria. Isaiah is commanded by the Lord to “say ye not, A confederacy . . . ; neither fear ye their fear, nor be afraid” (v. 12). This command for Isaiah to oppose the confederacy and communicate the need to trust in the Lord is rooted in the concept of fearing God more than men. By doing so, the Israelites would no longer fear their enemies, for the Lord would be their protection.
 

In verse 14, the Lord promises Israel that He will be their sanctuary. As a contrast to covenant Israel coming to His sanctuary, the Lord describes how the wicked will look upon Him. Using terms like “stone of stumbling,” “rock of offense,” “gin,” and “snare,” the Lord describes how He will be regarded by those whose self-centered beliefs are built on the philosophies of man. Those who deny the Savior’s divinity stumble over His precious teachings, thus condemning and trapping themselves in the inescapable snares of sin, inhibiting their ability to partake of the gifts of the Atonement. Jacob describes this rejection when he writes, “By the stumbling of the Jews they will reject the stone upon which they might build and have safe foundation” (Jacob 4:15).
 

Those who stumble on the “rock of offense” will find additional troubles down the road if they choose not to repent. Following a pattern of increasing intensity, Isaiah portrays what lies in store for them: they will “stumble and fall, and be broken, and be snared, and be taken” by the enemy (v. 15). Likewise, as we turn away from the Lord, our ability to escape punishment becomes more difficult, until we are eventually weakened and taken captive by the adversary.
 

2 Nephi 18:16–22: “I and the children . . . are for signs” 
 

In contrast to the weakened state of the captive wicked, the disciples of the Lord are strengthened through their sacred bonds with God. As a witness of their relationship, the Lord commands Isaiah, “Bind up the testimony [witness], seal the law [teachings] among my disciples” (v. 16). Because of His desire to teach and assist His children in their mortal probation, the Lord instructs prophets to teach and testify to the people of their time as well as to record their revelations and teachings for future generations. Like Nephi, Paul, Joseph Smith, and other prophets, Isaiah was commanded to keep a record of the “testimony” and “law” for the benefit of God’s children, present and future. The Lord issues a similar command to His servants in latter days, directing them “to seal up the law, and bind up the testimony, that they may be prepared against the day of burning.”8 God’s word is a sanctuary to many, but to others it is a stumbling block. And while Isaiah’s words inspire the righteous, they also stand as a witness against the wicked.
 

In verse 17, Isaiah describes the Lord as “hid[ing] his face from the house of Jacob.” Because the Jews in Isaiah’s time had become hard-hearted and stiff-necked, Jehovah’s identity as the Son of God and Savior of the world would go unrecognized by many in the house of Israel. Isaiah, however, is not daunted by the concealed state of the Lord, declaring that he will actively seek Him. The prophets continually teach this important concept of seeking the Lord. Moses explains, “If . . . thou shalt seek the Lord thy God, thou shalt find him, if thou seek him with all thy heart and with all thy soul” (Deuteronomy 4:29). Clearly, we must show genuine faith and sincere desire as we open our hearts and souls to invite the Lord into our lives, even if at times He seems hidden.
 

Verse 18 is a key verse of promise. It reveals that even though we may not see the Lord, He has provided signs and miracles of His works. Isaiah proclaims that he and his children are signs of the Lord’s actions for Israel. Reviewing the meanings of the names of Isaiah and his sons gives understanding to this verse. Isaiah’s name, Yeshayahuw, means “Yahweh’s (Jehovah’s) salvation.” Although he may have had other children, Isaiah mentions only two sons in his writings, Shearjashub (2 Nephi 17:3/Isaiah 7:3) and Maher-shalal-hash-baz (2 Nephi 18:3/Isaiah 8:3). Shearjashub means “the remnant shall return,” and Maher-shalal-hash-baz means “destruction is imminent.” Both sons’ names are prophecies of events in the history of Israel, some that occurred anciently and others that are in the process of occurring. Thus both of Isaiah’s sons, as well as Isaiah himself, are symbolic signs of the Lord’s promises for the house of Israel.
 

Nephi amplifies the meaning of these verses in his inspired commentary after quoting these Isaiah chapters in 2 Nephi. He foretells two remnants of Judah returning as he describes both the historic scattering and gathering of the Jews during the Assyrian-Babylonian-Persian period (2 Nephi 25:10–11) and their later scattering and gathering after Christ’s ministry, which continues into the last days (2 Nephi 25:15–17). Nephi then gives insights into the role of the scriptures and how some sacred teachings are hidden from the wicked (2 Nephi 26:16–17). He also foretells false teachings and practices that will come forth in the last days. Even though additional sacred records, such as the Book of Mormon, bring forth new truths, these distortions will seem to prevail in the world (2 Nephi 26–28). Nephi’s insights enrich our understanding about symbolic names, a hidden God, sealed teachings, and distorted doctrines that are briefly presented by Isaiah in these verses.
 

Because the Lord “hideth,” and the law is “sealed” up among His disciples, many turn to other sources for knowledge. From ancient times, people have turned to spiritualists, fortune-tellers, and others to help them commune with the deceased, invoke good luck, or learn about the future. Isaiah warns against turning to “wizards that peep and mutter,” and he asks the question, “Should not a people seek unto their God for the living to hear from the dead?” (v. 19). Speaking on this subject, Elder Bruce R. McConkie says:
 

No matter how sincerely mediums may be deceived into thinking they are following a divinely approved pattern, they are in fact turning to an evil source “for the living to hear from the dead.” Those who are truly spiritually inclined know this by personal revelation from the true Spirit; further, the information revealed from spirits through mediums is not according to “the law and to the testimony.” Accordingly, . . . it . . . has the effect of leading souls into the clutches of those evil powers that give the data.9
 

In verse 20, Isaiah reveals an important key of discernment: if a person speaks not of the law and the testimony (that is, the gospel truths), there is no light in that person. For example, we can know that someone is receiving light and revelation from God if he or she is full of love, because love and light abide together.10 Through thoughtful study and meaningful prayer, we can avoid being “blinded by the subtle craftiness of men” (D&C 123:12). Isaiah compares those who are clouded by misguided teachings to people who wander distressed and hungry (v. 21), seeking but never finding truth. On the other hand, those who follow the Lord will be comforted and fed. He is “the bread of life: he that cometh to [Him] shall never hunger” (John 6:35). As Nephi tells us in his commentaries on these Isaiah passages, we must feast upon Christ’s words, which will tell us all that we should do (2 Nephi 31:20; 32:3).
 

Isaiah concludes with a powerful pronouncement against those who seek not unto God for wisdom and truth. Continuing his theme of absent light (v. 20), Isaiah teaches that there is no peace in the end for the unholy. Indeed, he seems to be repeating the message that he delivered at the beginning of the chapter: “Destruction is imminent.” Those who start on the wrong path will find that they are surrounded by “trouble, and darkness, dimness of anguish, and [they] shall be driven to darkness” (v. 22). If we want to avoid the darkness, we must accept the invitation given in 2 Nephi 12:5/Isaiah 2:5: “O house of Jacob, come ye and let us walk in the light of the Lord.” In the next chapter, Isaiah will prophesy of a light that will come forth from the dark landscape of Galilee to bring truth and redemption to Israel.
 

In summary, Isaiah’s teachings in 2 Nephi 18 are mostly a series of warnings coupled with some promises and sealed with his prophetic witness. This chapter and chapter 17 provide inspirational warnings, signs, prophecies, and witnesses to Israel. To the unfaithful, these words are a snare that reveals their true relationship to God’s truths. To the faithful, Isaiah’s words are an inspiration that provides meaning to symbolic names and signs of God’s work with a promise that “God is with us!”
