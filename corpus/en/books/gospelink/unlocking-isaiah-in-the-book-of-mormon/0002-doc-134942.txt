# Isaiah the Profound Prophet

### Isaiah the Profound Prophet
Victor L. Ludlow, Unlocking Isaiah in the Book of Mormon (Salt Lake City 2003)
 
 

Isaiah the Profound Prophet
 

The writings of Isaiah constitute the most important prophetic discourses of the Old Testament. Indeed, Isaiah is quoted more in the New Testament, the Book of Mormon, the Doctrine and Covenants, and the Dead Sea Scrolls than any other Old Testament prophet. Jesus quoted Isaiah extensively as he began His public ministry in Nazareth,1 taught His disciples as recorded in the New Testament, and visited the Nephites as witnessed in the Book of Mormon. As recorded in 3 Nephi, the resurrected Savior cited some of Isaiah’s prophecies and then proclaimed that all of them would eventually be fulfilled, and He commanded His followers to diligently search Isaiah’s words.2
 

This book will help readers of the Book of Mormon understand the Isaiah passages found in 1 Nephi, 2 Nephi, Mosiah, and 3 Nephi. This first chapter highlights the historical setting and the prophetic role of Isaiah in biblical times. The next chapter reviews Isaiah’s significance within the Book of Mormon and also provides some suggestions of how to study the Isaiah chapters in the Book of Mormon. The third chapter explains the poetic style used by Isaiah in his writings. The following chapters 4 through 23 present insights and prophetic commentary about the individual Isaiah chapters in the order in which they appear in the Book of Mormon. The concluding chapter summarizes the significance of Isaiah’s words for Latter-day Saints.
 

Isaiah’s life history
 

In spite of the comparatively large collection of Isaiah’s writings, relatively little information was recorded about the man, his family, and his ministry. Isaiah was born around 775 b.c., approximately one century after the great prophet Elijah’s ministry. He lived in Jerusalem, seems to have been well educated, was married, and had at least two sons and possibly one daughter. He served as the Lord’s prophet for almost fifty years before suffering a martyr’s death.
 

Isaiah grew up during the reign of two ambitious Israelite kings. In the northern kingdom of Israel, King Jeroboam II beautified Samaria and expanded his country’s borders and influence to their greatest extent since Solomon’s time, some two centuries earlier. In the southern kingdom of Judah, King Uzziah served as Jerusalem’s most powerful king in more than a century. Wealth, social injustices, immorality, and growing pagan worship characterized both societies, but more so in the north. This was also a time of relative peace for both kingdoms since neither Assyria (to the northeast) nor Egypt (to the southwest) had strong rulers.3 However, Assyria was asserting increased political and religious influence in the region. It was probably during Isaiah’s teenage years, during the reign of Jeroboam, that the prophet Jonah traveled to Nineveh and called the Assyrian king and his subjects to repentance.4
 

Shortly after Isaiah reached adulthood, the two Israelite kingdoms weakened as one of Assyria’s most powerful kings, Tiglath-pileser III (or Pul, as he was called in the Bible), came to the throne in 745 b.c. Civil dissensions, rebel rulers, international warfare, and assassinations had disrupted the northern kingdom of Israel after Jeroboam’s death. Meanwhile in Judah, Uzziah had become a leper, social outcast, and co-regent king with his son after trying to burn the priestly incense in the temple. Both Israelite nations also became increasingly spiritually and morally corrupt. The prophet Hosea spoke of Israel’s broken covenants and especially warned her of wickedness and pending destruction. The prophet Amos chastised both nations for their neglect of the poor and needy. These prophets were preaching as Isaiah married and began his family.
 

Isaiah tells us very little concerning his family life. His two sons had symbolic names, Shear-jashub and Maher-shalal-hash-baz, both of which contained prophetic warnings (Isaiah 7:3; 8:1). Ancient Jewish tradition suggests that Isaiah was related to the royal families of Judah, and the scriptures record that his ministry spanned the reigns of four Judean kings—Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah (Isaiah 1:1). He received his prophetic call at the end of Uzziah’s reign (about 740 b.c.) and served for almost half a century until the end of Hezekiah’s reign. Jewish tradition also says that one of Isaiah’s daughters married King Hezekiah. According to Josephus, a later Jewish historian, Isaiah suffered a martyr’s death at the hands of the wicked King Manasseh, Hezekiah’s son, who would have been Isaiah’s grandson. Manasseh had Isaiah encased within a rotting tree trunk and sawn asunder with a wooden saw.5 This martyrdom would have occurred around 695 to 692 b.c. Thus, Isaiah completed his long prophetic ministry almost one century before Lehi’s family left Jerusalem.
 

Isaiah as a prophet
 

Isaiah was called as a prophet “in the year that King Uzziah died,” about 740 b.c., when he experienced a great vision concerning his call (2 Nephi 16/Isaiah 6). He was soon recognized by both kingdoms as a prophet of the Lord, although the people and their kings did not always heed his counsel. He was the last major prophet to teach all the Israelite peoples before they began to scatter from the Holy Land. His words went with them to the four corners of the earth to instruct, inspire, and comfort them throughout following generations. However, as his descendants and others study his messages today, they experience difficulty in comprehending his writings because of the varied and complex nature of his prophecies and poetry. As Nephi said of his brothers, “They know not concerning the manner of prophesying among the Jews” (2 Nephi 25:1). Because Isaiah’s ancient culture was so drastically different from modern society, most readers also lack an understanding of Isaiah’s terminology, symbolism, imagery, phraseology, and poetic style.
 

One valuable step toward understanding Isaiah is to evaluate his unique prophetic style. He developed prophetic gifts just as recent living prophets of the Lord have. Isaiah was a great scriptorian like President Joseph Fielding Smith and Elder Bruce R. McConkie. He could speak fluently on a variety of complex gospel topics like Elder Boyd K. Packer. He could speak on a high literary level like Elder Neal A. Maxwell. Or he could communicate with a straightforward style like President Thomas S. Monson. It is small wonder that his prophetic writings challenge us readers today.
 

Isaiah was also a master writer of the Hebrew language and of poetry. He was a genius in the Hebrew language as Shakespeare was in English and Goethe was in German. Translators and scholars have found words, phrases, and expressions of speech in his writings that are found nowhere else in the Hebrew scriptures. Even the most learned of men and women are challenged by his writings.
 

Readers of Isaiah should not despair, however. As they read and study Isaiah’s words, they become more familiar with his writing style, and they gradually begin to recognize and understand his dominant themes, key words, and gospel concepts. They see how Israelites throughout the ages, especially the Book of Mormon peoples, received inspiration from his messages. They also see how his prophecies can be expanded from an ancient Israelite setting to an early American Book of Mormon framework and then to a latter-day universal context. This universality is especially evident in the Isaiah chapters quoted in the Book of Mormon, since many of his pronouncements have at least a double fulfillment, with application to earlier times and to a later age. Additional insight is gained by following the cross-references listed in the footnotes of the scripture text. This provides potential meanings for difficult passages and the inspired commentary of other prophets.
 

Nephi recognized that many of Isaiah’s teachings could be applied to his own people, so he “likened,” or compared, Isaiah’s messages to his generation for their “profit and learning” (1 Nephi 19:23). Modern readers continue to learn from Isaiah as they study his gospel insights, witness the fulfillment of his prophecies, and seek to apply his teachings in their lives.
 

Isaiah’s warnings and prophecies cover almost three thousand years of Israelite history. They also foretell the first and second comings of the Messiah, the restoration of the gospel, the gathering of the house of Israel, the events and leaders before the Millennium, and some characteristics of the Millennium. As Christ said about Isaiah, “Surely he spake as touching all things concerning my people which are of the house of Israel” (3 Nephi 23:2).
 

Isaiah and his contemporary prophets (Amos, Hosea, and Micah) exhorted the Israelites to return to complete obedience to the law of Moses. According to his own writings, he did not perform many great miracles, although he promised a miraculous deliverance of Jerusalem (Isaiah 37) and prophesied health to Hezekiah before giving him a sign or miracle by having the sun’s shadow recede (Isaiah 38). His greatest prophetic power came not as a lawgiver (like Moses) and not as a miracle worker (like Elijah), but as a seer who prophesied about many future events of the world.
 

The major contents of Isaiah’s writings
 

Isaiah’s writings divide easily into two main collections or “books” of prophecies: collection 1, with an emphasis upon prophecies of judgment (chapters 1–35); and collection 2, with significance placed upon promises of redemption (chapters 40–66). The two books are written almost entirely in Hebrew poetry, and they are joined by a few historical chapters (36–39) written mostly in prose. It is assumed that Isaiah arranged his writings into their present order, although a scribe or disciple may have done so. Passages from both collections are recorded by various Book of Mormon writers: chapters 2–14 and 29 from collection 1, and chapters 48–54 from collection 2.
 

Portions of Isaiah’s writings (especially his history of Uzziah; see 2 Chronicles 26:22) were later lost, perhaps during the fifty-five-year reign of Manasseh, who had many religious records and prophetic writings destroyed. He also had many righteous people killed, including Isaiah. Manasseh was so religiously and morally corrupt that he offered his own son as a human sacrifice to the pagan gods. The Babylonian invasion and captivity resulted in the loss of more spiritual records. After the return from Babylon, Jewish scribes, led by Ezra, collected sacred writings, compared variant manuscripts, and prepared standardized copies. The accepted Hebrew translation of the Old Testament (called the Masoretic text, also known as the MT) was a continuation of the Ezra tradition.6 The contents of an early Greek translation (called the Septuagint, also known as the LXX) differ somewhat from the Masoretic text. The standard translation or Authorized Version for the English translation of Isaiah is the King James Version, although some excellent modern English translations are very helpful in understanding the awkward, stilted passages of Isaiah. The Book of Mormon rendition of Isaiah is helpful because it comes from the brass plates of Laban, taken from Jerusalem before the Babylonian captivity and later corruptions occurred. As will be seen in the next chapter, the Book of Mormon translation makes important improvements in the Isaiah passages.
 

It is important to note that in spite of the passage of time and the ravages and pressures within and without Judaism and early Christianity, most of Isaiah’s writings have remained relatively intact. The fact that his writings have been so well preserved demonstrates God’s protective power in their preservation. And the fact that Isaiah’s writings are so dominant in both the Bible and the Book of Mormon indicates their power and significance. They provide powerful warnings and prophecies to the world from a major Old Testament prophet who was a profound poet, a great teacher, and a man who truly communed with God! (2 Nephi 11:2).
