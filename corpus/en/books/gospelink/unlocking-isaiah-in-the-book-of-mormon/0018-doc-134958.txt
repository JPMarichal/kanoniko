# Joyful Psalms of Thanks and Praise

### Joyful Psalms of Thanks and Praise
Victor L. Ludlow, Unlocking Isaiah in the Book of Mormon (Salt Lake City 2003)
 
 

Joyful Psalms of Thanks and Praise
 

2 Nephi 22/Isaiah 12
 

In the millennial day all men shall praise the Lord— He shall dwell among them
 

This chapter contains two short, sacred psalms, or hymns, and is an appropriate conclusion to the powerful messianic messages and images in 2 Nephi 21 (Isaiah’s description of the great latter-day servants and the millennial golden age). In fact, these inspirational psalms serve as the ideal capstone to all the chapters quoted by Nephi thus far (2 Nephi 12–21), which focus on the problems and deliverance of the house of Israel. Isaiah speaks for all Israel as he gives thanks and praise to the Lord for providing salvation and millennial blessings.
 

Numerous psalms of thanks and praise are found in the scriptures, especially in the book of Psalms. Giving thanks and praise are two different expressions of worship, whether through song or prayer. As scholars distinguish the two, thanks recognizes God for what He does, while praise recognizes God for what what He is. Isaiah expresses beautiful words of both thanks and praise as he reveals what people will say “in that day” (usually referring to the last days).
 

2 Nephi 22:1–3: “With joy shall ye draw water out of the wells of salvation”
 

In verse 1, Isaiah indicates that all people are praising the Lord as they recognize His power and comfort. The historical setting of this verse seems to be twofold. It refers to both Isaiah’s day, when the Lord’s wrath protected Jerusalem against the invading Assyrians, and to the latter days, when the Savior will protect the righteous from their enemies, especially the adversary. At the Savior’s return, the covenant followers of the Lord will praise and thank Him for His protective care. In His infinite mercy, the Savior will forgive the children of Israel (turn away His anger from them) and cleanse them of their sins (comfort them).
 

Isaiah highlights God’s “salvation” in verse 2, teaching the essential doctrine that salvation comes only through the Savior, called here by His premortal name of Jehovah. Prophets from Adam to Joseph Smith have taught this important principle, and we can study their words in the scriptures listed in the Topical Guide and Book of Mormon index under “save” and “salvation.” In one Book of Mormon example, Alma explains this concept to his son, Shiblon: “And now, my son, I have told you . . . that there is no other way or means whereby man can be saved, only in and through Christ” (Alma 38:9). Continuing in 2 Nephi 22:2, Isaiah designates both “God” and the “Lord Jehovah” as the source of our salvation. In this beautiful verse, Isaiah reminds us that our Heavenly Father has provided the divine plan of happiness and Jesus Christ has fulfilled it. By fulfilling our Father’s plan for us through the Atonement, Christ became our salvation.
 

Verses 1–3 comprise Isaiah’s psalm of thanks. Interestingly, this passage contains unique images of water that connect five different generations: ancient Israelites delivered from Egypt (13th century b.c.), Israelites during Isaiah’s time (8th–7th century b.c.), Nephites (6th century b.c.), early-day Saints (1st century a.d.), and latter-day Saints.
 

The second verse alludes to the ancient Israelites in its similarity to a song of thanksgiving sung by Moses and the children of Israel following their deliverance through the parted waters of the Red Sea: “The Lord is my strength and song, and he is become my salvation: he is my God” (Exodus 15:2). Another parallel is between the exodus and the “water out of the wells of salvation” (2 Nephi 22:3). After giving thanks to the Lord, the ancient Israelites came upon twelve wells of water that sustained them on their journey to the promised land (Exodus 15:27).
 

By using a similar text for his psalm, Isaiah may have meant to compare the liberation of his people from the Assyrians to that of the Israelites from the Egyptians. In addition, Isaiah had earlier used the symbol of gentle waters to teach Israel about messianic salvation in response to an impending Assyrian attack (2 Nephi 18:6/Isaiah 8:6).
 

Nephi’s inclusion of Isaiah’s psalm carries the symbolic imagery of saving waters to the Nephites. Lehi’s family had also made a journey to a promised land and may have felt their “exodus” was similar to that of the ancient Israelites. Perhaps the psalms in this chapter expressed their feelings of thanks and praise to the Lord as well. The water spoken of by Isaiah in verse 3 can also be likened to the “fountain of living waters” that Nephi sees in his vision of the tree of life. Nephi explains that the waters in his vision represent “the love of God” (1 Nephi 11:25).
 

Jesus uses the symbol of water to teach a woman of Samaria, saying to her, “the water that I shall give . . . shall be . . . a well of water springing up into everlasting life” (John 4:14). However, this verse may have been fulfilled literally during an ancient temple ceremony in Jerusalem. After some time in Galilee, Jesus returned to Jerusalem for the Feast of the Tabernacles (John 7). Elder James E. Talmage teaches that the prophecy of water from “wells of salvation” in verse 3 was physically fulfilled “as part of the temple service incident to the feast.” The fulfillment occurred when “the people went in procession to the Pool of Siloam, where a priest filled a golden ewer, which he then carried to the altar and there poured out the water to the accompaniment of trumpet blasts and the acclamations of the assembled hosts.” The last day was “marked by ceremonies of unusual solemnity and rejoicing,” and on this great day, “Jesus cried aloud, His voice resounding through the courts and arcades of the temple: ‘If any man thirst, let him come unto me, and drink. He that believeth on me, as the scripture hath said, out of his belly shall flow rivers of living water (John 7:37, 38).’”1 Jesus exhorts His followers to drink of His love that we may bring forth our own living waters of love for others.
 

These biblical and Book of Mormon accounts remind Latter-day Saints how Christ’s life and power rescue us from Satan’s grasp. When we are faithful to the Lord and partake of the waters of His love (that is, the gospel), we can receive eternal life, the greatest gift of God. It is easy to see how this psalm of thanks applies not only to earlier peoples but also to each of us, for as we strive to love and understand the Savior, we naturally give thanks for His atoning grace.
 

Timeless sections of scripture like this one help us create meaningful relationships with religious communities from the past because they reveal to us that people throughout history have found strength and relief through Heavenly Father. Each child of God who has ever lived comes into this life and is given a unique set of challenging circumstances. We all have challenges, and we join our brothers and sisters from each dispensation in praising the Lord as we share our belief in our Heavenly Father’s plan as fulfilled through Christ’s atonement.
 

2 Nephi 22:4–6: “Praise the Lord. . . . Sing Unto the Lord”
 

The second half of 2 Nephi 22 is a “praise psalm,” in which people in the millennial era glorify the Lord as they sing praises to Him and His doings. Definite parallels exist between the events in Isaiah’s psalm and prophecies of the latter days. In these few verses, Isaiah describes:
 

1. A recognition of the Savior—an event that will occur when all people recognize Jesus Christ as the Son of God.
 

2. A declaration of the Lord’s power—a work that is currently transpiring through the efforts of latter-day missionaries and the building of Christ’s kingdom on earth, which will culminate during the Millennium.
 

3. The arrival of “the Holy One of Israel”—a prophecy that will be fulfilled by the second coming of the Savior, who will dwell in the midst of His Saints.
 

The combined themes of the two short psalms in this chapter follow the natural pattern of spiritual growth: the development of faith in the Lord and His atonement (vv. 1–2), the receiving of the gifts of baptism and salvation (v. 3), the expression of gratitude and wisdom in a declarative testimony (v. 4), and the praising of the Lord’s goodness and magnificence (vv. 5–6). Nephi records a similar poem of thanks and praise in 2 Nephi 4 in which he expresses faith in and thanks for the Lord’s deliverance (vv. 30–32), alludes to the protective power of covenants (vv. 33–34), and testifies of God’s goodness and power (v. 35). Another comparable psalm is found in Doctrine and Covenants 84:99–102, this one recorded by one of Isaiah’s modern-day counterparts, Joseph Smith. His themes follow the same general pattern as Isaiah’s. First, the Lord’s redemption is celebrated and the principles of faith and covenant-making are brought together (vv. 99). Then the Lord’s deeds are described (vv. 100–101). Finally, the hymn concludes with glorious praises of God (v. 102).2
 

In our spiritual development, we experience similar stages: first we develop faith in the Savior and His gospel, followed by a desire to participate in sacred ordinances. We next have the desire to testify of Christ, and we bear witness of the truth through song and joyous praise. Finally, we desire everybody to experience these things.
 

Isaiah, Nephi, and Joseph Smith each yearned for the Millennium, and each prophet wrote short, powerful hymns to give sincere thanks and profound praise to the Lord for His blessings and goodness. With poetry and power, they express the gratitude we feel as the Lord’s works and love are manifest among us.
