# Isaiah’s Calling as a Deliberately Difficult Prophet

### Isaiah’s Calling as a Deliberately Difficult Prophet
Victor L. Ludlow, Unlocking Isaiah in the Book of Mormon (Salt Lake City 2003)
 
 

Isaiah’s Calling as a Deliberately Difficult Prophet
 

2 Nephi 16/Isaiah 6
 

Isaiah sees the Lord—His sins are forgiven and he is called to prophesy— He prophesies of the rejection by the Jews of Christ’s teachings— A remnant shall return
 

This chapter diverges from Isaiah’s usual canon of prophetic warnings to God’s children, covering instead Isaiah’s calling as a prophet of God. The beautiful imagery and powerful symbolism of Isaiah’s vision help us to better understand the Lord’s holiness and glory as well as our own imperfection in His presence. Isaiah’s call echoes the foreordination of the Savior and His selfless submission to the Father’s plan. Isaiah’s charge from the Lord also foreshadows the challenges faced by the Master in His earthly ministry. Isaiah briefly alludes to the Savior’s profound sacrifice, highlighting the house of Israel’s initial rejection and then acceptance of their Master. Finally, Isaiah is promised that his words will eventually be understood as Israel is gathered in the latter days. As we review Isaiah’s great vision, we connect to our own personal spiritual experiences and their significance in the context of our premortal desires and earthly missions.
 

2 Nephi 16:1–4: “I saw . . . the Lord sitting upon a throne”
 

The burning bush for Moses and the sacred grove for Joseph Smith set the stage for their prophetic callings. The house of the Lord is the setting for Isaiah’s prophetic calling. The first few verses of the chapter provide a unique and detailed description of the heavenly place where Isaiah receives his call. By meticulously describing the event, Isaiah essentially transports his readers to the scene, allowing us to experience a calling of our own. Although distanced by education, time, spiritual knowledge, and even culture, we as readers can identify with his feelings of awe and bewilderment before the majestic throne of God.
 

Isaiah’s first observation of his environment focuses not on the peculiar and miraculous surrounding of heaven but on the glorious presence of the Lord Himself. Only later in the chapter does Isaiah comment on the unique furnishings, clothing, and seraphs of heaven. Isaiah describes the Lord as sitting on a “high and lifted up” throne—His elevated position clearly places Him in an exalted position.
 

Many symbolic parallels exist between Isaiah’s heavenly vision and our latter-day temples. As examples, Isaiah describes the Lord’s robe or train that “filled the temple” (v. 1). This train may be symbolically related to the modern-day temple veil that separates us from the direct presence of God. The seraphim are like temple attendants, calling to one another in a threefold petition (v. 2–3). The moving door posts (v. 4) suggest a stirring at the veil. Isaiah receiving a live coal from the temple altar (v. 6–7) reminds us of the eternal life promised to those who keep the covenants made at the temple altars.1 
 

After depicting the Lord’s majesty, Isaiah describes special beings, called seraphs, who attend the Lord. Joseph Smith also mentions seraphs in God’s presence in his dedicatory prayer of the Kirtland Temple (D&C 109:79; see also Bible Dictionary, “Seraphim,” 771). These seraphs are such unusual creatures that Isaiah carefully describes their three sets of wings and their manner of praising the Lord. Isaiah explains, “Each one had six wings; with twain [two] he covered his face, and with twain he covered his feet, and with twain he did fly” (v. 2). This depiction of the seraphim appears to be a literal one, but it could also be connected to symbolic representations. The multiple wings perhaps represent the protective veil that separates the Lord from mortals. The ark of the covenant, as described in Exodus and Kings, was adorned with cherubim similar to the seraphim, who had wings that spread over the mercy seat upon the ark. If the ark was indeed a symbolic representation of the Lord and a place where He revealed Himself to His servants, then the cherubic wings would technically cover the Lord as He descended upon the seat of the ark, acting as a sort of barrier, or veil, between the Lord and His servants (Exodus 37:9; 1 Kings 8:6). The wings of the seraphs may have similarly acted as a sort of veil between Isaiah and the Lord. Doctrine and Covenants 77:4 teaches that the wings also represent the seraphs’ power “to move, to act, etc.”
 

Isaiah uses the triumphant praise of the seraphim to further describe attributes of the Lord. The seraphim repeatedly exalt the Lord, proclaiming, “Holy, holy, holy, is the Lord of Hosts: the whole earth is full of his glory” (v. 3). The word holy defines a person, object, or place that is dedicated or consecrated for a sacred purpose.2 The threefold repetition of “holy” means “most holy,” a superlative grammatical form used in the Semitic languages. This declaration not only emphasizes the greatness of the Lord and His creations, but it also maintains that the Lord’s handiwork itself testifies of His glory.3 The manifestation of His glory is shown in His power and love for us. Indeed, after considering the great beauty and intricacy of the world we live in and the personal and eternal purposes of our earthly existence, we can testify that the Lord is a majestic God, whose sacred purpose is to bring about our immortality and eternal life (Moses 1:39).
 

The “posts of the door” mentioned in verse 4 may be describing the doorway that leads into the holy of holies in the Jerusalem temple. They may also be symbolic of the entrance that leads to God’s presence. In either case, both images allude to the special “doors,” or covenants, that we must all pass through in order to come into the presence of the Lord. Nephi explains that it is only through repentance and baptism that we can enter the holy gate that leads to eternal life (2 Nephi 31:17–18). Thus, in order to dwell with the Lord again, we must keep ourselves pure, able to pass through the doors of the heavenly kingdom.
 

2 Nephi 16:5–7: “I am a man of unclean lips”
 

Isaiah’s first sentiments following his witness of the Lord are ones of shame and unworthiness. Fear strikes his heart as he realizes that he is an imperfect person, ashamed to be in the presence of the Lord, the Ruler and Sovereign in whom all power rests, who rules over the heavens and the earth and all things that are in them. Although Isaiah may have been a very righteous man, these feelings of unworthiness in the presence of the heavenly King are to be expected. Nephi teaches us that “no unclean thing can dwell with God” (1 Nephi 10:21). Thus, even a very good person would feel the pangs of personal imperfection in the presence of the Lord.
 

To ease Isaiah’s discomfort in the Lord’s presence and to purify him for his task as prophet to the world, a seraph is sent to Isaiah with a hot coal that he presses to Isaiah’s lips. This symbolic purging of sin through a hot coal parallels the doctrine of refinement though “trials of fire” that prepare us for the coming of Christ (Zechariah 13:9; 1 Peter 1:7). Often it is when we undergo difficult and heated ordeals in life that we turn to the Lord and look to His counsel.
 

It is interesting that Isaiah’s lips embody his sin. His lips, or his words, are most essential to his calling as a prophet! Why would the Lord wish to call someone who is ashamed of His declarations? A simple explanation of the Lord’s choice of an imperfect man to become His prophet is the fact that He often strengthens men through overcoming their imperfections, making “weak things become strong unto them” (Ether 12:27). Although Isaiah is obviously insecure about his worthiness to proclaim truth, he valiantly fulfills the commandments of the Lord, becoming strong where he once was weak. With pure lips, he is ready to go forth as the mouthpiece or spokesman of the Lord.
 

2 Nephi 16:8–13: “Here am I; send me”
 

The actual calling of Isaiah is strikingly similar to events that took place in the premortal council before the world was formed. Abraham describes the Lord asking the spirits in heaven, “Whom shall I send?” just as He asks as Isaiah stands before Him. In the pre-earthly council, the Savior responded to His Father’s appeal, “Here am I, send me” (Abraham 3:27). Christ’s voluntary submission parallels Isaiah’s response to the Lord’s request for a faithful servant. Hearing of an opportunity to serve, Isaiah responds, “Here am I; send me” (v. 8). This reply not only presents Isaiah as a type of Christ, but it also shows that he willingly pledged himself to his calling, unlike some other great prophets who were initially reluctant to accept their roles (such as Enoch, Moses, and Jeremiah).
 

The Lord’s instructions to Isaiah in verse 9 have perplexed Bible scholars through the years. The phrasing and grammar of the earliest Hebrew manuscripts is awkward and hard to translate. With the more accurate version given in Nephi’s translation, we are able to understand the meaning of Isaiah’s words: “And he said: Go and tell this people—Hear ye indeed, but they understood not; and see ye indeed, but they perceived not.” The dialogue becomes clear when read as a conversation between Isaiah and the Lord: the Lord tells Isaiah to preach to the people while telling them that they don’t (or won’t) understand. The Lord commands Isaiah to help the people perceive truth, but they refuse to see. Thus, the people have obstinately turned against the Lord, refusing to accept the apparent. The Savior later quotes these words during His earthly ministry to describe the people hearing His word (Matthew 13:14).
 

After warning Isaiah of the pending (and current) wickedness of his people, the Lord continues to instruct him, using imagery to describe a people burdened with fat hearts, heavy ears, and closed eyes because of their unwillingness to hear the words of His servants. The Lord explains that He wants Isaiah to be deliberately confusing “lest [the people] be . . . converted and be healed” (v. 10). Like most of Isaiah’s writings, this prophecy refers not only to the people of his own day but also to the people during the Savior’s lifetime and of the latter days. Joseph Fielding Smith relates this passage to our time, stating:
 

One of the marked signs of the last days is the blindness of the people; we are told they would have eyes and see not, and ears but hear not, and hearts but understand not. If in the days of Jesus this was true of the Jews and surrounding nations, it is doubly so now in relation to the nations with which we are acquainted.4
 

Initially, we might wonder why the Lord seems to not want His people to repent. Does He want us to be confused? Does He desire that we go astray?
 

First, it is important to recognize that Isaiah’s sometimes perplexing teaching techniques are not unique. The Savior Himself “sp[oke] . . . in parables: because they seeing see not; and hearing they hear not, neither do they understand” (Matthew 13:13). As James E. Talmage explains, He “changed from direct exposition to parables” to withhold the mysteries of the kingdom of heaven from those “who were unreceptive and unprepared.”5 A second reason is that while Heavenly Father does not want to deliberately prevent His children from repenting, He also does not want the unrighteous and unworthy to think that they know everything or that they can heal themselves. Sometimes the Lord allows the wicked to continue in their ignorant, evil ways in order to provide less accountability and thus ensure fair, loving justice in the final judgment. In a like manner, the Lord counsels Isaiah to essentially conceal his message, making it understandable only to those worthy of its message of salvation. This is one important reason why it is so important to study Isaiah’s words carefully and with the Spirit.
 

In verse 11, Isaiah asks the Lord how long he must conceal his message from the wicked Israelites. The Lord responds, describing a time when the world will be desolate and the children of Israel will be scattered. The “great forsaking” (v. 12) prophesied by Isaiah possibly refers to the apostasy that occurred following Christ’s ministry. Isaiah then, in verse 13, foretells of a fraction of the Israelites returning, symbolic of a tithing to the Lord, as well as the literally small number of latter-day descendants of Israel who would become righteous. These returning Saints will be “eaten,” or grafted back to the tree of their inheritance. Because of their righteousness, they will bear sweet fruit once again (Jacob 5). We as Latter-day Saints are part of this grafting process, especially as we invite others to partake of the gospel and believe in the Savior. If we remain righteous and keep our ears and eyes open to the message of the Lord, we will be counted as one of the “holy seed” (v. 13).
 

Clearly, 2 Nephi 16 contains important insights for the people of Isaiah’s time, Nephi’s period, the Savior’s era, and today. It is important to remember that this material was included in the Book of Mormon because of its pertinence both to Nephi’s people and to later readers of the text. Nephi, like Isaiah, faced a challenging audience who would not always follow his words. Indeed, in his audience were loyal disciples and prophets of Christ (such as Jacob and Enos) as well as bitter enemies who would reject his prophetic calling and want him put to death. Isaiah and Jesus faced similar diversified audiences as they delivered their messages—trying to reach each listener (or reader) at his or her individual level of spiritual accountability. Isaiah spoke in symbolic poetry while Jesus spoke in simple parables as each, in his unique way, delivered God’s word to the righteous and the wicked.
 

Nephi spent much time laboriously copying the words of Isaiah into the accounts of his people to teach them (and his future audience) of Isaiah’s calling and challenges as a prophet. In addition, he probably wanted to highlight the importance of personal and diligent scripture study necessary to understand Isaiah’s deliberately difficult teachings. Isaiah purposely veiled his message in clouds of symbolism, poetry, and complex terminology to make his themes universally applicable and to ensure that his readers relied on the Spirit. With that Spirit, we, like Isaiah, can appreciate the Lord’s holiness and glory as we humble ourselves before Him. We can accept our callings and trials in life with an understanding that our good words and works will eventually be accepted by God. We, like Isaiah, can fulfill our destiny on earth as we follow the Lord’s instructions with faith and diligence.
