# Isaiah the Symbolic Poet

### Isaiah the Symbolic Poet 
Victor L. Ludlow, Unlocking Isaiah in the Book of Mormon (Salt Lake City 2003)
 
 

Isaiah the Symbolic Poet 
 

Isaiah was a prophet, seer, and revelator. As a prophet, he taught the Israelites important gospel principles and testified of the Lord and His words and works. As a seer, he saw great visions and knew of God’s plans and works through the ages to the end of days. As a revelator, he shared significant prophecies, especially about events in the last days. The particular method through which he chose to reveal his prophetic insights was poetry—symbolic poetry written in a style now known as parallelism.
 

In studying the words of Isaiah, it is important and helpful to know not just what Isaiah said but also how he said it. A study of his presentation style enriches our understanding of the cultural and literary context of his prophetic messages. Over 90 percent of Isaiah’s writings were written in Hebrew parallelisms. As you recognize this style prevalent in Isaiah’s words, you will overcome a literary obstacle that could otherwise restrict your comprehension of his profound words. In addition, appreciation of his poetic style enhances the meaning of his messages.
 

As the most prolific of the prophets who lived and taught toward the end of the eighth century b.c., Isaiah was a gifted poet and a master of the ancient Hebrew language. This chapter explains the poetic style of his prophetic writings. As you learn about how Isaiah presented God’s word, you will better understand both the context and the message of Isaiah’s writings. You will more easily see how Isaiah’s insights apply in your life. After studying this chapter, the writings of Isaiah will come alive for you.
 

Parallelism in Old Testament poetry and prophecy
 

Hebrew poetry comprises about one-third of the Old Testament, reaching beyond the so-called poetic books (Job, Psalms, Proverbs, and so on) into the rest of the scriptures. It is especially prominent in the prophetic books (such as Isaiah and Jeremiah). The ancient poets and prophets knew that their works were usually received and transmitted in oral, not written, form. Although written copies of their works (usually in the form of parchment scrolls) would have been available from generation to generation, most Israelites would not have had copies in their own homes. Temple or synagogue scrolls were usually not readily available, and in times of war or religious persecution, they would have been suppressed, banned, or destroyed. Rather than relying upon written records, the ancient Hebrews were trained to receive and remember long oral passages. The development of their oral retention allowed them to pass on religious records, poetry, psalms, family histories, and other important information.
 

The ancient poets, prophets, writers, and scribes assisted their audience by organizing their material into a more easily remembered form. Their messages were usually quite short and tightly structured like a song or a poem. They used memory devices or patterns that made the poems easier to remember and still allowed the author spontaneity of expression. Thus, there was less material to be memorized, and the arrangement facilitated its memorization. The poetic structures also focused attention on key elements of the prophet’s message. For example, Old Testament authors often used key phrases (like “thus saith the Lord”) or words (like “yea” or “verily”) as verbal flags to alert the listener to important passages that followed in their presentation. In addition, to emphasize key points, they repeated their ideas using slightly different terminology, or they placed the main idea in the focal point of a poetic pattern. These are just a few techniques Isaiah used to “package” his message to help his listeners and readers better remember and comprehend his words.
 

The most common trait of Hebrew poetry is the use of parallelism. In 1753, two thousand years after Hebrew had ceased to be a common spoken language, Bishop Robert Lowth of the Anglican Church rediscovered this poetic formula. Scholars since that time have expanded his ideas and have made major strides in understanding Old Testament poetry.
 

Parallelism is also the most significant and distinctive quality of Hebrew poetry. In parallelism, a thought, idea, or key word from the first line continues in the second line. This poetic parallelism is easily recognized in English because it is not a “sound rhythm” or a “grammar pattern” but rather a “theme rhyme” in which the meaning from one line is related to an idea of another line in a variety of parallel patterns. Think of Isaiah’s parallelisms as repetitions or amplifications of certain ideas or thoughts. He either repeats a particular idea in a slightly different context or approaches it with an alternate but parallel perspective to give further emphasis and insight.
 

Types of parallelisms
 

Seven types of parallelisms are found in Old Testament poetry. This section describes these seven basic types and provides examples from the Isaiah passages in the Book of Mormon.
 

Synonymous parallelism occurs when a theme in the first line repeats itself in the second line but in slightly different words. For example:
 

(a) he was wounded for our transgressions,
 

(a’) he was bruised for our iniquities (Mosiah 14:5/Isaiah 53:5).
 

Here’s another:
 

(b) for my name’s sake will I defer mine anger, and
 

(b’) for my praise will I refrain from [cutting] thee . . . off (1 Nephi 20:9/Isaiah 48:9).
 

This is by far the most common and recognizable form of parallelism in Isaiah (and in the most beloved poetic book in the Bible, Psalms). Although close together, the repeated ideas reinforce each other and provide a more complete perspective of the major concept.
 

Many doctrinal themes and messages are repeated throughout the scriptures. Repetition is a necessary educational process, whether in learning a new vocabulary word or in understanding complex religious doctrines. As President Hinckley reminded Church leaders, “repetition is the law of learning” (Priesthood Leaders Satellite Broadcast, 21 June 2003). Using synonymous parallelism, Isaiah could repeat his messages while reinforcing the learning and memory processes in his listeners.
 

Antithetic parallelism results when a thought in the second part of a couplet contrasts with a theme in the first. For example:
 

(a) I will ascend above the heights of the clouds; I will be like the Most High.
 

(b) Yet thou shalt be brought down to hell, to the sides of the pit (2 Nephi 24:14–15/Isaiah 14:14–15).
 

Here’s another:
 

(a) Say unto the righteous that it is well with them; for they shall eat the fruit of their doings.
 

(b) Wo unto the wicked, for they shall perish; for the reward of their hands shall be upon them! (2 Nephi 13:10–11/Isaiah 3:10–11).
 

Here’s a noted example found in Isaiah but not in the Book of Mormon:
 

(a) If you are willing and obedient, you will eat the good things of the earth:
 

(b) but if you refuse and disobey, you will be devoured by the sword (Isaiah 1:19–20).
 

This form is very common in Proverbs, where the use of opposites clarifies both extremes, such as contrasting the righteous with the wicked, the obedient with the disobedient, the wise with the foolish, and so on. It brings one idea into sharp focus as it is distinguished against an opposite concept.
 

Through antithetic parallelism, the poet reflects the opposition of all things (compare Ecclesiastes 3:1–8). As in life, where sensitivity to ugliness and suffering can lead one to better appreciate beauty and goodness, so also in poetry the sharp contrast of opposites brings the desired idea or message into sharper focus. Although Isaiah does not frequently use antithetic parallelism between two lines, it can be found between the two halves of a single verse (2 Nephi 21:4/Isaiah 11:4) or when comparing groups of verses. Thus, clusters of verses within the same chapter might contrast destruction with restoration, scattering with gathering, wickedness with righteousness, or curses with blessings. For example, the first verses of 2 Nephi 24/Isaiah 14 highlight the restoration of Israel and her people while the later verses foretell the downfall of Babylon and her king.
 

Emblematic parallelism is when the ideas or concepts from two halves of one line or from two different lines are compared by means of a simile or metaphor. For example (with emblematic parallels shown in italics and comparative prepositions in bold):
 

(a - a’) All we, like sheep, have gone astray (Mosiah 14:6/Isaiah 53:6).
 

Another example:
 

(a - a’) Then had thy peace been as a river,
 

(a - a’) and thy righteousness as the waves of the sea (1 Nephi 20:18/Isaiah 48:18).
 

Symbolic, emblematic parallelism, like a parable or an allegory, can be either clear and distinct or hazy and vague.
 

The listener’s past experiences and the new subtle imagery enrich the understanding of any emblematic parallelism. Like parables, they allow each listener to comprehend the parallelism according to his or her own background and insights. Thus, every listener can immediately relate to the material and yet still be challenged to develop a new perspective of possible additional meanings. Isaiah’s poetic writings are full of symbolism, and he uses emblematic parallelisms to help us identify some of the rich spiritual meanings of the symbols.
 

Note how these first three types of parallelism are combined together in a single verse from one of the most famous passages in Isaiah:
 

(a - a’) though your sins be (red) as scarlet,
 

(b - b’) they shall be white as snow;
 

(a - a’) though they be red as dyed wool, 
 

(b - b’) they shall be (white) as fleece (Isaiah 1:18).
 

 
 

It is not uncommon to have more than one type of parallelism within the same verse. As in the example above, all the types work together to enrich and reinforce the messages of that particular passage.
 

Synthetic parallelism is a more subtle form in which the second element or line completes or complements the first in a variety of possible combinations.
 

For example, the first element might ask a question that the second element then answers. Or, the first line might make a proposition with the second line stating a conclusion. Another example would be to have a bold statement followed by an explanation. In essence, the first part of the synthetic parallelism “sets us up” and gets us thinking and desiring more information. Then the second part provides the details, resolves our concern, or answers our question. For example:
 

(a) Surely he has borne our griefs, and carried our sorrows;
 

(b) yet we did esteem him stricken, smitten of God, and afflicted (Mosiah 14:4/Isaiah 53:4).
 

Here, the first line portrays what He has done for us, but without the second line we might wonder, “So, what have we done?” or “What does He now expect of us?” or similar questions. The second line indicates that we tend to miss the significance of His suffering for us because we just assume that it is God’s will that He suffer.
 

Here’s another example:
 

(a) The Lord God hath given me the tongue of the learned,
 

(b) that I should know how to speak a word in season unto thee (2 Nephi 7:4/Isaiah:50:4).
 

The first line causes us to wonder why God has given Isaiah a learned tongue, and the second line explains that it is so he will know how to speak to us.
 

Note the connection between the two lines of the verse as the second line continues or completes the thought of the first. Thus, a synthetic parallelism joins or blends two ideas in any of several possible relations, depending upon how the “set-up line” and the “punchline” are connected.
 

Sometimes difficult to identify, synthetic parallelism encompasses good educational psychology as it generates and answers questions, completes statements, and amplifies ideas. If the first line of a verse seems incomplete, or if it causes you to want to know how or why that statement is true (asking such questions as “Why has He carried our sorrows?” or “What does Isaiah do with his prophetic tongue?”) then this line probably begins a synthetic parallelism. We then look for the answer or resolution in the second line so we can know the direction in which the message is supposed to take us.
 

Composite parallelism is when three or more ideas develop a theme by amplifying a concept or defining a precept. For example:
 

(a) Enlarge the place of thy tent, and
 

(b) let them stretch forth the curtains of thy habitations;
 

(c) spare not, lengthen thy cords and
 

(d) strengthen thy stakes;
 

For thou shalt break forth on the right hand and on the left (3 Nephi 22:2–3/Isaiah 54:2–3).
 

Another example:
 

(a) Awake, awake again, . . . 
 

(b) put on thy beautiful garments, O Jerusalem, . . . 
 

(c) Shake thyself from the dust;
 

(d) arise, sit down, O Jerusalem;
 

Loose thyself from the bands of thy neck (3 Nephi 20:36–37/ Isaiah 52:1–2).
 

By presenting a variety of ideas that radiate around a central theme, this parallelism is often an advanced combination of synonymous and synthetic parallelisms as they combine to provide a complete message. Sometimes the central idea (or hub) of these ideas is expressly stated, as in the first example above (the erecting of Zion’s tent). Other times, no central theme is mentioned, or else a vague general theme or summary might be stated, as with the second example, and the listener must still organize the component parts within a general framework and complete the model (Israel gaining God’s power as she returns to the Lord; compare D&C 113:7–10).
 

In both examples above, notice how the poet provides additional aids to memory. In the first, the sequence of raising a tent is natural and easy to picture and remember—preparing the ground, laying out the walls, stretching the ropes, and anchoring the stakes. In the second, Jerusalem’s activities follow her through the day—wake up, get dressed, get out and about your daily business, and achieve success.
 

Through composite parallelism, the poet expands an idea beyond its simple meaning into its varied component parts. Complex issues are presented in as many facets as the poet wants to develop. He provides the ideas, definitions, and interpretations to give the listeners a more complete understanding of the central theme. In a composite parallelism, Isaiah paints a multifaceted picture, as Paul did in his epistle to the Ephesians when he exhorted them to “put on the whole armour of God” (Ephesians 6:11–18).
 

Climactic parallelism is when part of one line (an introductory word or phrase) is literally repeated in the second and subsequent lines until a key pattern or theme is developed.
 

The crowning message, or climax, of the parallelism is expressed at the end of the repetitive lines. Occasionally, the theme statement is given first and then followed by the repeated term with its attached phrases. Often however, the listener or reader must determine the essential message from the context of these steplike, repetitive lines within the chapter. A climactic parallelism culminates with a main idea or statement, whether that key idea is expressed or implied. For example:
 

And it shall come to pass,
 

(a) instead of sweet smell there shall be stink; and
 

(b) instead of a girdle (sash), a rent (rope); and
 

(c) instead of well set hair, baldness; and
 

(d) instead of a stomacher (fine clothing), a girding of sackcloth;
 

(e) instead of beauty, burning (branding) (2 Nephi 13:24/Isaiah 3:24).
 

Note the repeated words at the beginning of the inner lines of the poem above. Once those lines have been identified, then one looks for the climactic message. In the example above, the central theme is found by looking at the verses before and after the poem. Specifically, the lack of courageous, compassionate men (vv. 1–15) and the vanity of haughty, vain women (vv. 16–23) demonstrates the moral weakness of the people that leads to the reversal of their health and abundance (v. 24 above), a reversal resulting from war and devastation (vv. 25–26). The transition from the evil, worldly old days to the humble, deprived future era is highlighted in the climactic parallelism.
 

Here is a classic, long example:
 

For the day of the Lord of Hosts soon cometh
 

(a) upon all nations, yea, upon every one; yea, . . .
 

(b) upon every one who is lifted up, and he shall be brought low.
 

Yea, and the day of the Lord shall come
 

(c) upon all the cedars of Lebanon, . . . , and 
 

 upon all the oaks of Bashan, and
 

 upon all the high mountains, and
 

 upon all the hills, and
 

 upon all the nations . . . , and
 

 upon every people; and
 

 upon every high tower, and
 

 upon every fenced wall; and
 

 upon all the ships of the sea, and
 

 upon all the ships of Tarshish, and
 

 upon all pleasant pictures.
 

 And the loftiness of man shall be bowed down,
 

 and the haughtiness of men shall be made low;
 

 and the Lord alone shall be exalted in that day (2 Nephi 12:12–17/Isaiah 2:12–17).
 

In the second example above, the major idea is not just that the day of the Lord is coming (see the first two bold lines). The climactic message is that He alone will be exalted (see the last bold line), partially because the “high” men and objects will be brought low before His power and glory (see the many lines beginning with “upon”). Thus, after a long series of connected statements, the final statement is the capstone of the whole message.
 

This progressive model of parallelism leads to, descends from, or evolves around a main point. This complicated form of parallelism is made by combining similar ideas in a recognizable, repetitive pattern. The most profound example of climactic parallelism in the Bible is in the wonderful beatitudes at the beginning of the Sermon on the Mount (3 Nephi 12:1–12/Matthew 5:3–12). To help distinguish climactic parallelism from composite parallelism, look for a word or phrase that is repeated in each central line of the poem.
 

By using a number of close, successive steps, the poet channels the listener’s attention toward a culminating point. This climax could be a summary of the earlier mentioned ideas, or it could be a new idea derived from the context of the earlier ones. In any case, the poet uses climactic parallelism to lead the listeners toward a major theme or idea. Most of Isaiah’s parallelisms are just two lines or ideas in length. When he uses three or more lines in a composite or climactic pattern, he provides extra emphasis on a key concept or idea. Look for the larger message when you recognize these longer patterns.
 

Introverted parallelism is present when a pattern of words or ideas is stated and then repeated but in a reverse order. This parallelism is also called chiasmus. For example:
 

(a) Ephraim shall not envy
 

(b) Judah,
 

(b’) and Judah
 

(a’) shall not harass Ephraim (2 Nephi 21:13/Isaiah 11:13).
 

Here is an example of a longer pattern:
 

(a) Make the heart of this people fat,
 

(b) and make their ears heavy,
 

(c) and shut their eyes—
 

(c’) lest they see with their eyes,
 

(b’) and hear with their ears,
 

(a’) and understand with their heart,
 

 and be converted (return) and be healed (heal themselves) (2 Nephi 16:10/Isaiah 6:10).
 

Here is a more complex pattern:
 

(abc) Come . . . to the house of the God of Jacob, . . . and we will walk in his paths; . . . 
 

(d) and he shall judge among the nations, . . . 
 

(ef) and they shall beat their swords into plow-shares,
 

(e’f’) and their spears into pruning-hooks—
 

(d’) nation shall not lift up sword against nation. . . . 
 

(a’c’b’) O house of Jacob, . . . let us walk in the light of the Lord (2 Nephi 12:3–5/Isaiah 2:3–5).
 

Chiastic patterns can be expanded to include many verses, whole chapters, and even (according to some authorities) groups of chapters. With the more elaborate examples, a main theme or pivotal message is usually stressed in the center of the chiasmus. The separate halves of the chiasmus can also be in parallel patterns to each other (synonymous, antithetic, synthetic, and so on), so this becomes a very sophisticated style of Hebrew poetry. For example, the first half of a chiastic poem might portray our relationships with each other, and the second half might highlight our relationships with God in a synonymous pattern. Or the first half of a chiasm might warn of God’s judgments and punishments while the second half promises His mercy and blessings in an antithetic pattern. The possibilities seem endless and complicated, but patience and careful study will open up these parallelisms as you read the Isaiah passages in the Book of Mormon.
 

Chiastic parallelism is a common literary and public communications style used by Israelite poets and prophets. Just as contemporary students in public speaking classes are taught to organize their talks with (1) an introduction, (2) major ideas or illustrations, and (3) a conclusion, so ancient Israelite poets would use chiasmus along with other forms of parallelism to present their messages. Hebrew poetic parallelisms were their primary means of communicating to their audience.
 

Introverted parallelism is found in much of the inspired prophetic literature,1 suggesting the possibility that revelation was received by the prophets in this structured form. Thus, parallelisms were not just a cultural practice of the Hebrew prophets but also may have been the pattern of God’s communication to His prophets. Chiasmus (and the other parallelisms it incorporates) transmitted sublime, divine teachings, most of which are relevant for God’s children upon the earth today.
 

Why parallelisms?
 

For the people of Old Testament times, parallelism served not only as an oral memory device; it also enriched the messages as it provided new dimensions of meaning. Today, an awareness of parallelism helps readers understand vague and repetitive biblical passages. This potential is stressed in the following helpful quotations:
 

Categorizing two lines of poetry according to this or that type of parallelism should not be done rigidly and mechanically. Categorization is useful because it points up relationships and verbal associations for the reader. It can aid in understanding how a theme develops within a poem, what elements are repeated, and how new aspects of a theme are related to those introduced earlier. Parallelism is more than rhetorical embellishment. When, for example, in Psalm 1 “the knowledge of the Lord” is contrasted with the “perishing of the wicked,” something is learned about the knowledge of the Lord. Nor is parallelism simply repetitious: “almost invariably something is added . . . and it is precisely the combination of what is repeated and what is added that makes of parallelism the artistic form that it is.”2
 

Hebrew poetry may appear repetitious upon first reading, but parallelism may be clearly distinguished from repetition as a literary device, by noting that the idea is repeated, not the words or phrases. There are many cases of repetition in Hebrew prose, usually for the sake of stressing a point. Avoiding this confusion of literary devices is essential in appreciating the beauty and emotional strength of Hebrew poetry. Much of the Old Testament is in poetry, and a firm understanding of parallelism will make clear a great deal that is otherwise obscure.3
 

Any chapter of poetic or prophetic material in the Old Testament probably contains numerous examples of parallelism; even single verses often have two or more forms combined within them. By understanding this style of writing and prophesying among the Jews, scripture readers can not only comprehend spiritual themes, but they can also appreciate the prophets’ creative skills in their composition.
 

The holy scriptures are among the greatest works of literature, and their inspired writings can uplift any reader who will study their contents, ponder on them, and seek for spiritual enlightenment. As we read Isaiah in the Book of Mormon, we can feel the power of his prophetic words and appreciate the beauty of his inspired poetry.
