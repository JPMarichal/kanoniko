# Punishment and Redemption in the Latter Days

### Punishment and Redemption in the Latter Days
Victor L. Ludlow, Unlocking Isaiah in the Book of Mormon (Salt Lake City 2003)
 
 

Punishment and Redemption in the Latter Days
 

2 Nephi 13, 14/Isaiah 3, 4
 

Judah and Jerusalem shall be punished for their disobedience— The Lord pleads for and judges his people—The daughters of Zion are cursed and tormented for their worldliness—Zion and her daughters shall be redeemed and cleansed in the millennial day.
 

Second Nephi 13 encompasses some of Isaiah’s grimmest writings, prophesying misery and destruction that will befall the wicked Israelites of his day. However, his message speaks to more than the sinners of his time; it is a warning to all the unrighteous—ancient and modern, Jew and Gentile, Latter-day Saints and members of other faiths. Yet, Isaiah’s words in 2 Nephi 14 include some of Isaiah’s grandest images, foretelling redemption and divine protection that will bless the righteous Israelites in the latter days. This message is ultimately one of comfort, for he promises that Zion will be protected and her inhabitants redeemed in the millennial day. From these combined messages, we are assured that the Lord will justly and completely reward the disobedient and the obedient.
 

2 Nephi 13:1–8a: “The people shall be oppressed, every one by another”
 

To help us appreciate Isaiah’s messages in this chapter, we will highlight various poetic structures he uses to present his words. For example, the first section of chapter 13 creates a chiasmus that outlines the punishment of the unrighteous. Isaiah highlights a condemnation of the people of Jerusalem and Judah, the loss of their physical sustenance and defense, and the absence of leadership and justice. The chiastic structure pivots in verse 5 when Isaiah explains that the people, among themselves, initiate their own oppression and destruction. He then repeats his warning to the wicked, again highlighting their want of food, lack of protection, and absence of leaders in Jerusalem and Judah. The chiasmus follows this pattern:
 

A Jerusalem and Judah are addressed (v. 1).
 

B Bread and water are taken away (v. 1).
 

C Society’s leaders are listed (vv. 2–3).
 

D Children rule over princes (v. 4).
 

E People oppress each other (v. 5a).
 

D’ Youth rebel against the elders (v. 5b).
 

C’ Family leaders are called upon (v. 6).
 

B’ Lack of bread and leadership (v. 7).
 

A’ Jerusalem and Judah are ruined (v. 8a).
 

This poetic chiasmus is indeed an urgent message for a wicked society.
 

Isaiah begins his warning with a symbolic image, describing the Lord’s removal of the “stay of bread” and the “stay of water” from Jerusalem and Judah. Bread and water are essential for bodily health, and Isaiah suggests that the Lord, in His vengeance, will remove physical sustenance from the earth, leaving the wicked Israelites in want. The stay of bread and water also symbolizes another kind of nourishment—spiritual food—that will be sadly lacking among the disobedient children of Israel. This particular symbol of spiritual malnutrition was common during Isaiah’s day. Elder James E. Talmage comments that in ancient Israel, “‘the whole stay of bread,’ w[as] explained by the rabbis as referring to their own teaching, and they laid it down as a rule, that wherever . . . allusion was made to food or drink, it meant study of the law, and the practice of good works.”1 Unrighteous Israelites should have understood Isaiah’s allusion to their lack of spirituality. The Nephites who read Isaiah’s words from the plates of Laban would have understood this image, since “the bread and the waters of life” are described in Book of Mormon text as the food that would bring everlasting peace and joy (Alma 5:34). Today, the bread and water are spiritual symbols that we partake of weekly in remembrance of our sacred covenants. Isaiah’s image thus suggests that the unrighteous will be unworthy to participate in the sacramental covenants and will be shut off from the saving ordinances of the Atonement.
 

Isaiah uses a composite parallelism to describe the loss of both secular and spiritual leadership in the latter days. The rulers, or “the stay and the staff” (representing symbols of leadership), who will be removed during times of wickedness will include a variety of military, political, social, and cultural leaders (vv. 2–3). As an antithetic parallel, in their place, “children” and “babes” (v. 4) will rule over the elders, quite contrary to the Semitic tradition of family and tribal government.
 

Without proper leadership, the whole society disintegrates into chaos, and the people oppress each other. Instead of love between neighbors, there is fear. This ironic twist of events displays the reversal of moral and social values that will also occur in the latter days. Good becomes evil (2 Nephi 15:20/Isaiah 5:20), children “behave . . . proudly” to the elderly, and the base defame the honorable (v. 5). Truly, Satan’s influence perverts the natural truth and order of earthly life. In his hands, items of darkness are made to seem as light, words of deceit are presented as truth, and ways of wickedness are accepted as behavioral standards. We can trust that both Satan and Christ will be consistent in their dogmas—Christ is a man of truth, while Satan is the father of lies. To discern true light, truth, and values, one must follow the light of Christ, or one’s conscience, with faith in His teachings, which are always ennobling, right, and eternal.
 

Isaiah continues to describe the scarcity of leadership that faces the Israelites in ancient and latter days, explaining that men will evade leadership and welfare responsibilities for their own family members. They plead, “In my house there is neither bread nor clothing; make me not a ruler” (v. 7). This not only portrays cowardice, but it also underscores an inability to accept moral responsibility because of selfishness or wickedness. As Isaiah describes this people, there are just too many cowardly, self-centered men among them, and thus the whole society suffers.
 

2 Nephi 13:8b-15: “They shall eat the fruit of their doings”
 

In verse 8, Isaiah starts a new chiastic poem that explains why social chaos will come among the inhabitants of Judah and how the Lord’s people will be spared. He describes their evil acts and highlights the rewards coming to the wicked and the righteous. In an introverted order, he then highlights the judgments coming to various people and their oppressive acts. The chiasmus follows a clear pattern:
 

A Evil acts against the Lord (vv. 8b–9a).
 

 - Wo to the evil ones (v. 9b).
 

B Rewards + It is well with the righteous (v. 10).
 

 - Wo unto the wicked (v. 11).
 

 - People oppressed by children (youths; v. 12).
 

B’ Judgments + The Lord will plead for the people (v. 13).
 

 - “Ancients” (leaders) brought into judgment (v. 14a).
 

A’ Evil acts against the poor (vv. 14b–15).
 

Although the message is generally one of doom and gloom, promises of divine blessings and pleadings are pivotal elements in the poem.
 

In verse 8, the people first provoke the Lord with their “tongues” (what they say) and “doings” (what they do). This display of the Israelite’s external actions is coupled with the Lord’s vision of their interior thoughts, or the “show of their countenance” (v. 9). Thus, the Lord can see both the internal and external wickedness of the people, and He judges them fairly. It is fundamentally important that we watch not only what we say and do but also what we think. King Benjamin explained to his people, “If ye do not watch yourselves, and your thoughts, and your words, and your deeds, . . . ye must perish” (Mosiah 4:30). Our minds as well as our words and deeds must be pure before the Lord.
 

Isaiah again uses symbolic imagery, an emblematic parallel, to compare the openly visible sins of the wicked to the depravity of the city of Sodom. This wicked city described in Genesis 19 was utterly destroyed because of the people’s depravity. Isaiah explains that the fate of his own people will be the same. He cries out for their souls, foreseeing destruction similar to that described in Lamentations: “The punishment of the iniquity of the daughter of my people is greater than the punishment of the sin of Sodom, that was overthrown as in a moment, and no hands stayed on her” (Lamentations 4:6). Thus, in the Lord’s time, the wicked among Israel will be destroyed just as Sodom was. It is important to note that Isaiah also often compares the wickedness of his people to Babylon, another evil city. Both of these wicked lands—Sodom and Babylon—represent Satan’s kingdom on the earth. Their ultimate destruction foretells Satan’s inevitable defeat.
 

Isaiah shifts the focus of his writings in verse 10, looking to the righteous, who will not be destroyed in the Lord’s vengeance. He uses beautiful images of bounty to describe the spiritual fruit that will provide for the righteous both temporally and spiritually. What a contrast from the wicked, who will reap harvests of emptiness and be left in want, as described in verse 1. Isaiah’s image is similar to Paul’s promises of fruitfulness to the righteous in his vineyard parable (1 Corinthians 9:7–12). It is also like the fruitfulness the Lord promises missionaries in D&C 52:34. Most profoundly, the fruit of the tree of life, “the greatest of all the gifts of God,” is offered to those “baptized unto repentance” (1 Nephi 15:36; Alma 5:62). In each of these beautiful images, the righteous are promised an abundance of spiritual food—their “soul[s] shall never hunger nor thirst, but shall be filled” (3 Nephi 20:8).
 

In verse 12, Isaiah prophesies of the Israelites’ utter shame by explaining that the patriarchal system of male rule will be replaced with an oppressive leadership led by youth and women. This complete reversal of societal order parallels the shift of the Israelites’ spiritual state as they turn from the Lord and revel in wickedness.
 

A powerful image of the Lord as the final judge is introduced in verses 13 and 14, with Isaiah describing the Lord’s “stand[ing] up to plead, and stand[ing up] to judge . . . enter[ing] into judgment with the ancients of his people.” The Lord’s standing action connotes His voluntary and sacrificial role as defender of the children of Israel. Although the Israelites have turned away from their God, the Lord still pleads for their return and offers forgiveness paid for by His eternal Atonement.
 

Isaiah soundly chastises the children of Israel in verse 15, speaking for the Lord as He asks, “What mean ye? Ye beat my people to pieces, and grind the faces of the poor.” The wicked during Isaiah’s time not only refused to give to the less fortunate, but they also cruelly punished them, beating them and grinding them down. As shocking and implausible as these accusations sound, they actually continue in our own day! President Gordon B. Hinckley explains, “We have recently been reminded that in this land there still persists, notwithstanding the protection of the law, a merciless exploitation of children in certain industries. And there are still those, not a few, who without compassion ‘grind the faces of the poor.’”2 If we do not willingly give of our substance, and if we coldly refuse to assist those in need, we are participating in cruelty against the poor. Clearly, the Lord wants His people to contemplate their actions toward those with fewer spiritual and temporal blessings. These prophetic warnings constitute the second half to the chiasmus of earlier verses, highlighting again the oppression of the people, the reversal of leadership positions, the social and spiritual turmoil, and the destruction of the wicked.
 

2 Nephi 13:16–26: “The daughters of Zion are haughty”
 

In one of his more famous passages, Isaiah condemns the wicked Israelite women for their vanity and excess. With a series of composite parallelisms, he verbally paints a vivid picture of richly dressed women of the night. He lists their many physical adornments and embellishments, stressing the overabundance of material goods valued by these spiritually blind women. They are preoccupied with necklaces, earrings, bracelets, hats, headbands, curlers, coats, and scarves of fine materials—expensive fashions that the world still adores.
 

With climactic parallelism, Isaiah then describes the Israelite women’s fate: their “sweet smell” become “stink,” their fancy clothes turn into rags, their “well set hair” changes to “baldness,” their fine clothing (“stomachers”) becomes “sackcloth,” and their beauty turns into scars (v. 24). The irony of this downfall climaxes in the last verses of the chapter as the once proud and haughty daughters of Zion sit upon the ground as slaves in desolation and poverty.
 

2 Nephi 14:1–2: “The branch of the Lord be beautiful and glorious”
 

Isaiah builds upon the imagery of the barren women in the following short chapter (2 Nephi 14/Isaiah 4). He describes women desperately searching for husbands (taking on a man’s name) and families (removing the reproach of childlessness). At least these women approach men desiring something valuable and lasting, unlike the earlier daughters of Zion who enticed the men to receive their wealth and favor. Isaiah’s image of the seven women taking hold of one man represents a chaotic state of social affairs. The availability of few men relative to the many women suggests that horrendous wars and other disastrous events will have killed many men (2 Nephi 13:25). And of the surviving males, relatively few are both willing and able to provide the physical, emotional, and spiritual support that the women desire. The women desiring marriage and family thus can find only a handful of men to become their husbands and the fathers of their children.3
 

Isaiah moves from a description of social chaos to a promise of blessings among those who “are escaped of Israel.” A contrasting image, or antithetic parallel, is found in verse 2 as the barrenness of the women is replaced with magnificent abundance. The Israelites’ prosperity is compared to a fruitful branch—linking their eventual victory with Jacob’s well-known parable of the vineyard. In his poetic allegory (Jacob 5), Jacob describes the grafting of the fruitful Gentile branches to the original olive trees (house of Israel). Those who come into the gospel and faithfully take on the Lord’s covenants are “grafted,” or added to the house of Israel. Because of the abundance of “young and tender” grafted branches (Jacob 5:9–11), symbols for righteous, covenant-making Gentile Saints, the bitter fruit of Israel is made sweet again.
 

Using imagery of the fruitful branch, Isaiah could also be alluding to his servant poem in Isaiah 11 (2 Nephi 21) in which a “branch” or “rod” is described. The rod represents a great servant of the Lord, perhaps depicting a latter-day leader who will prepare the world for the Lord’s second coming. The word branch or vine may also symbolize the Savior himself, who will bring spiritual and physical sustenance to His righteous followers when He returns.
 

Isaiah’s metaphor of the “fruit of the earth” (v. 2) parallels his promises of redemption and grace recorded in Isaiah 27. In that chapter, he describes the eventual triumph of the children of Israel, writing that they who “come of Jacob [shall] take root: Israel shall blossom and bud, and fill the face of the world with fruit [especially children]” (Isaiah 27:6). The promise of formerly barren women becoming fruitful is also taught in Isaiah 54, a chapter quoted by the Savior during His resurrection ministry in the Americas (discussion on 3 Nephi 22 later in this book).
 

2 Nephi 14:3–4: “The Lord shall have washed away the filth of the daughters of Zion”
 

Isaiah describes the remaining righteous who will be spared from the Lord’s punishments in the last days. Verses 3 and 4 praise those who are “left in Zion and remain in Jerusalem.” This describes both the chosen people of the Old World (in Jerusalem), and the people of the New World, living in Zion (the New Jerusalem). As members of the stakes of Zion, we are included in the Lord’s assurance of protection (D&C 68:26; 107:36; 109:59).
 

Contrasting images of cleansing (water) and burning (fire) are presented in verse 4 as the Savior “wash[es] away the filth” of the people in Jerusalem and purges them “by the spirit of burning.” Both actions denote a purification of the body, but one implies physical cleansing while the other suggests spiritual cleansing. Through the gift of the Atonement, the Savior cleanses those who come unto Him “by the spirit of judgment” and purges those worthy of His grace with “the spirit of burning” (v. 4). Isaiah’s description of cleansing and purging also alludes to the ancient Jewish sacrificial traditions in which burnt animal sacrifices were washed and purged of blood before they were offered to the Lord as a burnt offering (Leviticus 1:9, 13). Symbolic washing and cleansing from the blood and sins of this telestial life are also experienced in our temples as we prepare to enter God’s celestial realms.
 

2 Nephi 14:5–6: “There shall be a tabernacle . . . for a place of refuge”
 

In verses 5 and 6, Isaiah describes the sacred nature of the tabernacle, or temple, by calling it a “shadow in the daytime,” a “place of refuge,” and “a covert from storm and from rain” (v. 6.) These composite descriptions of latter-day temples illustrate the peace and security that come through temple attendance. Isaiah’s allusions to a cloud of smoke and a pillar of fire evoke images of similar phenomena that followed the ancient children of Israel on their travels in the wilderness. The Holy of Holies, the Israelites’ sacred tabernacle (or temple), was continuously shadowed by smoke and fire as a sign of the Lord’s protection. Thus, Isaiah promises that in the latter days, the righteous in their sacred places on mount Zion will be protected by the Lord’s “smoke” and “fire” (v. 5). This does not refer only to the latter-day temples. As we are taught in latter-day doctrine, all aspects of our lives are like temples—our homes, our churches, and our bodies. As we live worthily, we receive God’s guidance and protection.
 

Instead of being like the weak, selfish men and the vain, worldly women described by Isaiah in 2 Nephi 13, we should be like the pure, holy children of Zion prophesied about in 2 Nephi 14. As we become clean and sanctified, we become vessels in which the Holy Spirit can continually dwell. With that Spirit, God will instruct and guide us, and with His might, He will shield and protect us.
