# Israel’s Messianic Redemption

### Israel’s Messianic Redemption
Victor L. Ludlow, Unlocking Isaiah in the Book of Mormon (Salt Lake City 2003)
 
 

Israel’s Messianic Redemption
 

2 Nephi 7/Isaiah 50
 

Isaiah speaks Messianically— Messiah shall have the tongue of the learned— He shall give his back to the smiters—He shall not be confounded
 
 
 

Isaiah reveals many great truths about the Savior in this chapter, emphasizing the Lord’s covenant relationship with Israel, foreshadowing His future sacrifice, and outlining His noble qualities we should emulate. After quoting this chapter and Isaiah 51, the Book of Mormon prophet Jacob proclaims that he has “read these things that [we] might know concerning the covenants of the Lord that he has covenanted with all the house of Israel” (2 Nephi 9:1). In addition, several phrases not found in present Bible translations are recorded in Jacob’s quotation, more fully developing the ancient text. By carefully studying this chapter and then reading Jacob’s inspired commentary in 2 Nephi 9, we can develop a greater understanding and appreciation for Isaiah’s powerful messages.
 

2 Nephi 7:1–3: “Where is the bill of your mother’s divorcement?”
 

The first three verses of chapter 7 are written in an accusation-defense format. As in a courtroom proceeding, charges of abandonment are brought against the Lord by the plaintiff, Israel. Showing patience and wisdom, the Lord disproves each charge brought against Him and ultimately reveals the true identity of the guilty party.
 

Jacob’s quotation of Isaiah 50 begins with phrases unique to the Book of Mormon. While the King James Version of the Bible begins immediately with the Lord’s defense against Israel’s accusations (“Where is the bill of your mother’s divorcement?”), the Book of Mormon softens the abruptness of Isaiah’s opening words by expressing the Lord’s perpetual concern with Israel (“Have I . . . cast thee off forever?”). To understand the difference between these accounts, remember that Jacob’s version of Isaiah came from the brass plates of Laban, written shortly after Isaiah’s ministry, while all current Bible translations come from copies of manuscripts written many centuries after Isaiah lived and taught.
 

The opening rhetorical question (“Have I put thee away?”) evolves into a discussion of Israel’s first complaint against the Lord—that He has put them away like a man divorcing his wife. In response to their accusation, the Lord asks the children of Israel for proof of His supposed departure: “Where is the bill of your mother’s divorcement?” This is a reference to the law of divorcement given in Deuteronomy: “When a man hath taken a wife, and married her, and it come to pass that she find no favour in his eyes, because he hath found some uncleanness in her: then let him write her a bill of divorcement, and give it in her hand, and send her out of his house” (Deuteronomy 24:1). Despite the abundant “uncleanness” in Israel, the merciful Lord remains true to His covenant people. Although they have wandered off, He has not completely cast them out. In fact, they are separated, but they are not divorced.
 

Another complaint the Israelites bring against the Lord is that He has cast them off forever. In answer to this unqualified claim, the Lord again asks His covenant children for proof of His supposed abandonment. Verse 1 continues, “To whom have I put thee away, or to which of my creditors have I sold you? Yea, to whom have I sold you?” In ancient Israel, it was possible for a man in debt to sell his children as “temporary slaves.” After the man’s debt was paid off, the children and the debt would be released.1 In response to their accusation, the Lord asks twice if He has sold them, but the Israelite nation is again left without an answer. Since the Lord is without debts, He would not need or ever want to sell the children of Israel to anyone.
 

In the end of verse 1, the Lord explains to Israel why they have been estranged: “For your iniquities have ye sold yourselves, and for your transgressions is your mother put away.” The guilty party is not the Lord, it is Israel. Feeling distant from the Lord’s protective mercies and grace, they accused the Lord of deserting them, while it was the Israelites who separated themselves from Him through their sins and transgressions.
 

After pronouncing Israel’s guilt, the Lord explains that He has not only remained faithful to the house of Israel but has also attempted to bring them back into favor. Verse 2 says, “Wherefore, when I came, there was no man; when I called, yea, there was none to answer.” This declaration reveals much about our Father’s desire to bring each of us back into His presence. Like the shepherd who seeks fervently for his lost sheep, the Lord actively seeks out each of His wayward children (Luke 15:4).
 

The verse continues by emphasizing the eternal nature of the Lord’s power. Through Isaiah, the Lord asks the Israelites if they think His hand is “shortened.” Scriptures often refer to the hand and arm of the Lord as symbols of power, so the Lord is essentially asking if the Israelites think His power has diminished. As a reminder of His potency, the Savior gives two examples of His authority being used to deliver the covenant people.
 

The Lord’s first example can refer to events past and future. “Behold, at my rebuke I dry up the sea, I make their rivers a wilderness and their fish to stink because the waters are dried up” (v. 2) is a reference to the miracle of the parting of the Red Sea, which occurred centuries earlier at a time when the Israelites thought the Lord had forgotten them (Exodus 14:21–30). A similar parting of the waters occurred as the children of Israel entered the promised land, forty years later (Joshua 3:14–17). Before the Millennium, waters will part again in fulfillment of a prophecy Isaiah gives concerning the return of the tribes of Israel (2 Nephi 21:15/Isaiah 11:15).
 

The second example, “I clothe the heavens with blackness, and I make sackcloth their covering” (v. 3), also refers to events surrounding the delivery of Israel. The ninth plague given through Moses to Pharaoh was a plague of darkness that covered all the land, except for Goshen, the area inhabited by the Israelites (Exodus 10:21–23). Later, when Israel was preparing to cross through the Red Sea, a cloud of darkness overcame Pharaoh’s men in order to shield the Israelites (Exodus 14:19–20). The reference to darkness has additional New Testament fulfillment in conjunction with a greater spiritual deliverance. At the time of Christ’s crucifixion, great darkness covered the skies. In Jerusalem, “there was a darkness over all the earth until the ninth hour” (Luke 23:44). Symbolically, sackcloth, the traditional coarse robes of a mourner, was heaven’s covering, for the Son of God had died. An even greater covering of darkness occurred across the ocean with the descendants of Nephi: “There was thick darkness upon all the face of the land, insomuch that the inhabitants thereof who had not fallen could feel the vapor of darkness” (3 Nephi 8:20). Before the Second Coming, darkness will again cover the earth: “The day shall come that the earth shall rest, but before that day the heavens shall be darkened, and a veil of darkness shall cover the earth” (Moses 7:61). Indeed, the Lord’s power over the waters and the heavens provides periods of darkness in preparation for the light of a new day (compare 2 Nephi 19:2/Isaiah 9:2).
 

2 Nephi 7:4–9: “I gave my back to the smiter”
 

The next six verses quoted by Jacob contain one of Isaiah’s four servant songs. The other three are found in Isaiah 42, 49 (1 Nephi 21), and 52:13–53:12 (3 Nephi 20, Mosiah 14). The servant songs contain moving descriptions of an unidentified servant of God, each highlighting different aspects of the servant’s life, mission, and personality. Several theories exist concerning the identity of the servant, but only one truly fits the description: the Servant of servants, Jesus Christ. Although the primary identity may be Christ, all of us should study the servant songs, strive to understand the characteristics they teach, and work to become more like the servant they describe. In effect, the servant songs provide a guide and an outline on how we can become more like Christ.
 

This particular servant song begins with a powerful statement: “The Lord God hath given me the tongue of the learned, that I should know how to speak a word in season unto thee, O house of Israel” (v. 4). In His youth, Jesus learned His duty and important gospel insights from His Father in Heaven. Matthew reveals that before Christ’s ministry, “he served under his father, and he spake not as other men, neither could he be taught; for he needed not that any man should teach him” (JST, Matthew 3:25). Luke records that when Jesus was twelve years old, Mary and Joseph “found him in the temple, sitting in the midst of the doctors, and they were hearing him, and asking him questions. And all that heard him were astonished at his understanding and answers” (JST, Luke 2:46–47). As a lad, Jesus was already teaching the religious leaders of His time with “the tongue of the learned.” The Savior was taught by His Eternal Father so that he could “speak a word in season” to the Israelites. The Gospels of Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John each record the wisdom that Jesus shared with the Israelites in an attempt to lead them to truth.
 

The rest of verse 4 addresses Israel and highlights the Lord’s early efforts in their behalf: “When ye are weary he waketh morning by morning . . . to hear as the learned.” The Lord’s undeviating devotion to learn “morning by morning” so that He can help the wearied Israelites, who had become weary through sin, shows the love and commitment the Lord has in fulfilling His divine purposes on this earth. Even though His mission would be wrought with frustration, ridicule, and agony, Christ “was not rebellious, neither turned away back” (v. 5). Even in the early morning of the last day of His life, as He suffered immense pain, He was not rebellious and did not turn away from the bitter cup that was placed before Him.
 

In the final hours before His crucifixion, Christ was betrayed, beaten, mocked, and spat upon. In verses 6 and 7, Isaiah describes these events—750 years before they happened! The fact that the very events described in these verses occurred during the Savior’s final hours is one of the great evidences that the servant song refers to Christ. Interestingly, one of Isaiah’s prophecies seems to go unfilled in the life of Christ—“I gave . . . my cheeks to them that plucked off the hair.” Does this mean the servant song does not refer to the Savior’s suffering? Probably not. There are several possible explanations regarding this statement. First, the translation in the Bible may make the phrase unclear. The reference to giving His cheek to those who pluck off hair may be a reference to the many times that Christ was slapped and abused by the Jewish leaders during His “trial.” Second, in the Orient, plucking the hairs of the beard of an enemy is a sign of contempt and disgrace, and Isaiah may have used the phrase to describe the shame Christ would be subject to.2 Third, this single prophecy may have been fulfilled but not recorded. Perhaps Christ was subjected to having parts of His beard plucked out as one of His cruel punishments. This unique insight from Isaiah may give us some new information concerning the Savior’s sufferings.

In verse 8, the servant reveals the divine authority with which he will accomplish his mission: “The Lord is near, and he justifieth me.” A similar proclamation is in verses 7 and 9: “The Lord God will help me.” The Savior, even before the fulfillment of His calling, takes no glory for Himself, instead giving all credit to His Father. After revealing the divine source of power, the servant calls for supporters to join him in the war against the adversary: “Who will contend with me? Let us stand together. Who is mine adversary? Let him come near me, and I will smite him with the strength of my mouth” (v. 8). The phrase in italics is not found in the Bible translation, showing again that the brass plates contained a more correct and complete version of Isaiah. The restored phrase promises the authoritative might of Christ’s words and reveals the fate of those who choose to side with Satan—they will be smitten. And as the Lord symbolically foretells in verse 9, His enemies will eventually decay into nothingness: “All they who shall condemn me, behold, all they shall wax old as a garment, and the moth shall eat them up.”
 

As Jacob amplifies these verses in 2 Nephi 9, he offers additional confirmation that the servant song describes the suffering Messiah. After quoting this scripture to his people, Jacob delivers a sermon of depth and beauty, revealing much about the Fall, the need for the Atonement, the Savior’s role in the plan of salvation, and the resurrection of mankind (2 Nephi 9). During the night after teaching and testifying about the Holy One of Israel, Jacob received additional revelation concerning the Redeemer: “It must needs be expedient that Christ—for in the last night the angel spake unto me that this should be his name—should come among the Jews . . . and they shall crucify him” (2 Nephi 10:3). Approximately 550 years before the Savior was born, His divine title, the Greek rendition of Messiah, was revealed to Jacob. In fact, this is the first time the name Christ, meaning “the Anointed One,” is mentioned in the Book of Mormon.3 Just as Jacob’s study and use of Isaiah’s words led him to further inspiration, so our reading and pondering of Isaiah’s words, especially in their Book of Mormon and latter-day context, can lead to profound spiritual insights.
 

2 Nephi 7:10–11: “Who is among you that feareth the Lord?”
 

In verse 10, the Lord asks three important questions, each identifying different key traits of the Lord’s disciples. The first question, “Who is among you that feareth the Lord?” may confuse some readers. The word fear usually connotes negative feelings and images not normally associated with our relationship to Heavenly Father. Instead, remember that in the biblical context, “to fear” God usually means “to revere” or “to esteem” Him. Since the Lord is so mighty, glorious, and “awesome,” we should humble ourselves in awe before Him (Bible Dictionary, “Fear,” 672). Also, to fear the Lord means to obey Him in covenant righteousness (Deuteronomy 6:13; 31:12). Our fear should be that, in breaking His commandments, we will disappoint our loving Father in Heaven.
 

The second question, “Who is among you . . . that obeyeth the voice of his servant?” teaches the importance of following the prophets of God. In all the gospel dispensations from Adam until now, God has used prophets to teach eternal truths to His children on earth.
 

Third, Christ asks, “Who is among you . . . that walketh in darkness and hath no light?”
 

Although this may seem contrary to the common counsel to “walk in the light,” it teaches an important lesson about faith. The Lord is asking who has faith enough to follow Him in unlit paths. According to Elder Boyd K. Packer, “Somewhere in your quest for spiritual knowledge, there is that ‘leap of faith,’ as the philosophers call it. It is the moment when you have gone to the edge of the light and stepped into the darkness to discover that the way is lighted ahead for just a footstep or two.”4 There will be times in our lives where we will have to take steps into the dark and trust that the Lord will light the way before us.
 

The final verse of 2 Nephi 7 is a warning against those who choose not to exercise faith in the Lord but instead “compass [them]selves about with sparks, [and] walk in the light of [their] fire and in the sparks which [they] have kindled.” Only one path, the strait and narrow, will return us to our Father. This path is lit by the light of the Lord. Those who do not follow this path, and instead follow paths lit by the sparks of sin and selfishness, will have their reward—they will be like burnt-out ashes that “shall lie down in sorrow” as they regret their self-destructive acts.
 

Isaiah delivers covenant promises, messianic expectations, and words of warning within this chapter. Jacob shared them along with newly revealed insights with his brethren of the Nephite community. And we today are inspired by their messages: God remains close to us; Jesus, the great Servant, suffered for us; and it is only through fearing (or esteeming) the Lord, following His servants the prophets, and walking in faith that we avoid the fires of divine judgment. Isaiah’s words provide spiritual wisdom for all generations of God’s children.
