# A Song of Scorn over the Fallen King of Babylon

### A Song of Scorn over the Fallen King of Babylon
Victor L. Ludlow, Unlocking Isaiah in the Book of Mormon (Salt Lake City 2003)
 
 

A Song of Scorn over the Fallen King of Babylon
 

2 Nephi 24/Isaiah 14
 

Israel shall be gathered and shall enjoy millennial rest— Lucifer cast out of heaven for rebellion— Israel shall triumph over Babylon (the world)
 

This chapter envisions the peaceful period following the Babylon destruction that was prophesied in the previous chapter. It also foretells the ultimate fate of the king of Babylon. Isaiah’s prophecy has dual meaning—describing both Babylon the ancient city with her political rulers, and Babylon the symbol of worldliness, whose king is Lucifer. As the leader of an Israelite settlement on a new continent, Nephi included it as the last chapter in his block of Isaiah’s writings. He had witnessed Babylon’s powerful influence before his family left Jerusalem, and he wanted his people to avoid similar worldliness and evil among their descendants. This prophet also knew that Satan would constantly oppose God’s Zion society with an attractive but counterfeit Babylonian society. Nephi surely hoped his people would remember God’s promises of rest and peace to the righteous as well as of frustration and chaos for Babylon’s king and his subjects.
 

2 Nephi 24:1–3: “For the Lord will have mercy on Jacob”
 

In spite of problems that the children of Israel had in keeping their covenant with Jehovah, the Lord promises them that He will “yet choose Israel” (v. 1). This is in sharp contrast to the destruction of Babylon promised in the previous chapter. Whereas Babylon fades into oblivion, the Lord promises that, as a result of His mercy, Israel will “return to their lands of promise” (v. 2). This prophecy refers to the time after Babylon’s fall when Cyrus the Great, king of Persia, allowed the exiled Jews to return to Judah. It also refers to the latter days when remnants of all Israel will gather to their promised lands. Israel, however, won’t be alone. Isaiah tells us that “strangers shall be joined with them, and they shall cleave to the house of Jacob” (v. 1). The “strangers” are those that have been adopted into the house of Israel by accepting the gospel and making covenants with Jehovah.
 

Though Israel has been punished and scattered, the Lord will conquer her enemies. And, as Isaiah says in verse 3, “The Lord shall give thee rest, from thy sorrow, and from thy fear, and from the hard bondage wherein thou wast made to serve.” These are comforting words for any liberated souls. When the Lord’s mercy comes to the house of Israel, they will finally be able to “rest.” The rest came for ancient Israel when they were able to leave Babylon for their promised land. The rest will come for Israel in the latter days during the millennial era when the worldly Babylon is overthrown and Christ reigns as the Prince of Peace. Further, we can rest from “the hard bondage” that we serve when we rid ourselves of the Babylon within us. When we turn away from worldliness and rely on the Lord’s mercy, He gives us rest and peace.
 

2 Nephi 24:4–11: “How hath the oppressor ceased!”
 

After recounting the fall of Babylon, Israel sings a “taunt song” against its king. The song is written using a common Hebrew poetic form, composed of parallel couplets, which becomes more apparent when the passages are read aloud.
 

Amazed that the once-powerful empire has been toppled, Israel marvels, “How hath the oppressor ceased, the golden city ceased!” (v. 4). Ancient kings of Babylon were not only powerful, but they were also notorious for their arrogance and persecution against others. The Lord has caused this powerful kingdom to fall. He finally breaks their staff (power) and scepter (authority) as they are themselves persecuted and overcome (vv. 5–6). With the evil leaders gone, Isaiah tells us that “the whole earth is at rest, and is quiet” (v. 7), reminding us of the rest foretold in verse 3 when Israel is gathered through the mercy of the Lord. Even the firs and cedars (the educated and leading classes of society) rejoice now that the henchmen of the evil one no longer seek their destruction (v. 8).
 

Peace and quiet are not what await the king of Babylon, however. Isaiah promises that “hell from beneath” awaits him (v. 9). Joseph Smith comments that the Hebrew word used here for “hell” is sheol, or in Greek, hades. He teaches that both terms “mean a world of spirits. Hades, Sheol, paradise, spirits in prison, all are one: it is a world of spirits.”1 Babylon’s king is now a helpless spirit in the realm of the dead.
 

Other kings and rulers who have died rise to meet the new arrival, and they mock him: “Art thou also become weak as we? Art thou become like unto us?” (v. 10). All their riches and power were not able to save them in the world of spirits as their “pomp is brought down to the grave” (v. 11). This reflects the teachings of the Savior to the people at Bountiful: “Lay not up for yourselves treasures upon earth, where moth and rust doth corrupt, and thieves break through and steal; but lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven” (3 Nephi 13:19–20; compare Matthew 6:19–20; 2 Nephi 8:8/Isaiah 51:8). Worldly treasures and political power cannot follow us to the next life. Only spiritual gifts will bless and strengthen us and others as we experience death and enter the postmortal world.
 

2 Nephi 24:12–23: “How thou art fallen from heaven, O Lucifer”
 

In these next verses, Isaiah continues his Babylon motif by addressing the king of spiritual Babylon: “How thou art fallen from heaven, O Lucifer, son of the morning!” (v. 12). Lucifer’s pride and selfishness are what led to his rebellion and the war in heaven. Elder James E. Talmage explains, “Lucifer, already of exalted rank, sought to aggrandize himself without regard to the rights and agency of others.”2 Isaiah’s words echo this idea: “Thou hast said in thy heart: I will ascend into heaven, I will exalt my throne above the stars of God; . . . I will ascend above the heights of the clouds; I will be like the Most High” (vv. 13–14). Instead of rising to exalted heights, however, Lucifer will “be brought down to hell, to the sides [or flanks or depths] of the pit” (v. 15). The “pit” refers to spirit prison or a prisonlike dwelling: “Terming this place the pit lays special emphasis on its nature as a prison, a prison in ancient times commonly having been a pit.”3
 

Those that see him, Isaiah tells us, will not believe their eyes. Astonished, they ask three questions related to Lucifer’s powers, accomplishments, and dominion. First, concerning his physical powers, they ask, “Is this the man that made the earth to tremble?” (v. 16). This reminds us of Satan’s limited powers to literally shake the ground in his presence (Moses 1:21–23.) Also the Aramaic root of “Satan” is satanah, meaning “to shake.” Second, they ask about his achievements upon the earth: “Is this the man that . . . made the world as a wilderness?” (vv. 16–17). Earth was a paradisiacal garden until Lucifer turned it into a dreary wasteland. In sharp contrast, Isaiah promises that when the Messiah appears, He will turn the earth’s desert into a garden of roses (Isaiah 35:1). Thus, Lucifer makes deserts, but the Lord creates gardens. Third, they ask about Lucifer’s dominion in the realm of the dead: “Is this the man that . . . opened not the house of his prisoners?” (2 Nephi 24:16–17). The chains of sin with which Satan enslaves us on earth carry over into the spirit world, where we are turned over to his buffeting and torment.4 At the time of Isaiah, the devil had not yet released even one of the prisoners in his spirit prison. This liberation occurred over seven centuries later as Christ’s atonement and resurrection finally opened the gates of death and hell.
 

Isaiah next brings greater condemnation upon Lucifer than upon any king of ancient Babylon. In verses 18, he tells him, “All the kings of the nations, yea, all of them, lie in glory, every one of them in his own house.” Even the kings of Babylon and the dictators of nations may have done some good during their lives, and they will at least be resurrected with some degree of glory, but Satan, as a cast-out spirit being, will never enjoy association with others in any kingdom of glory. In verse 19, Isaiah informs him, “Thou art cast out of thy grave like an abominable branch.” The phrase “an abominable branch” can also be translated as “an abhorrent offshoot” or “a loathsome limb.” Isaiah’s image is of a branch or twig that is broken and twisted—never reaching its fruit-bearing potential. It remains as a gnarled, ugly scar on the tree trunk. Compare this to the fruit-bearing branches discussed in an earlier chapter, representing God’s servants under Christ’s leadership who prepare the earth and its inhabitants for the Millennium (2 Nephi 16:13/Isaiah 6:13). Lucifer was a spirit son of God with great potential, but he rebelled against the Father and was cast out of heaven. He temporarily reigns over this wicked world on earth while awaiting his final dishonorable destiny in outer darkness. He is denied even the dignity of a grave because he rebelled against God and will therefore never have a physical body. His future destiny in darkness is so far removed from his earlier heavenly potential that he will be like “a carcass trodden under feet” (v. 19).
 

Why is his punishment so great? Isaiah tells us in verse 20, “Thou shalt not be joined with them in burial, because thou hast destroyed thy land and slain thy people.” In contrast to the Lord, whose mercy was described at the beginning of the chapter, Lucifer does not provide for those who follow him. As Alma 30:60 teaches, “The devil will not support his children at the last day, but doth speedily drag them down to hell.” Left without help from their leader, those who follow Lucifer, the king of spiritual Babylon, will be subject to the Lord’s judgments. “I will rise up against them, saith the Lord of Hosts,” and they will be swept away with a broom of destruction (vv. 22–23). Satan’s condemnation is that he deliberately brought this terrible destruction upon his people.
 

2 Nephi 24:24–27: “Surely as I have thought, so shall it come to pass”
 

Following the pronouncement of Babylon’s fall and the ultimate defeat of the kingdom of the devil, Isaiah reminds us of the omniscience of the Lord. He quotes Him as saying, “Surely as I have thought, so shall it come to pass; and as I have purposed, so shall it stand.” The hand of God is in every event. Elder Neal A. Maxwell reminds us that world events and the destinies of nations, as well as our own lives, all occur under the watchful and directing eye of the Lord. “What we mortals encounter as the unforeseen, God has already seen.”5
 

In verse 25, Isaiah explains how Assyria, the fierce power of his own time, will be checked by the Lord. They will be trodden “under foot,” and the yoke they inflicted upon the Lord’s people will be taken from them “and his burden depart from off their shoulders.” In verse 26, Isaiah explains that this will be the eventual end to everyone who fights against the Lord’s people, reflecting back to Babylon’s fall as well: “This is the hand that is stretched out upon all nations.” As will be highlighted later in this book, Isaiah makes a similar statement echoed in 3 Nephi (3 Nephi 22:16–17/Isaiah 54:16–17). All those who rebel against God will fade into oblivion as they are eventually destroyed.
 

Isaiah contrasts the fate of the wicked by promising God’s remembrance of the righteous. The God who fathered the spirit of every person on this earth will always remember His faithful children. His hand is still stretched out, and no power can turn it back from redeeming Israel (v. 27; compare 3 Nephi 22:4–10/Isaiah 54:4–10). Our responsibility, as latter-day Israel, is to keep our covenants and pursue a steady course of obedience so we can enjoy the Lord’s blessing. President Gordon B. Hinckley teaches us: “You and I may fail as individuals and miss the blessing. But [the Lord’s] work cannot fail. There will always be those he will raise up to accomplish it.”6 Isaiah reinforces this idea, promising that no one can thwart what the Lord of Hosts has planned (v. 27). The battle between spiritual Babylon and spiritual Israel may still be going, but the end is known to the Lord, and the victorious side has already been decided.
 

2 Nephi 24:28–32: “The Lord hath founded Zion”
 

The last five verses of this chapter are a “burden” or prophecy to “whole Palestina,” or the Philistine peoples that bordered Judah to the west. Since the days of King David, the Philistines were subject to the military and economic power of Judah. Although they might rejoice that Judah was being attacked by Assyria and that “the rod of him [Judah] that smote thee is broken” (v. 29), even worse catastrophes were awaiting Palestina. Assyria later attacked the Philistines, and though they initially held off the Assyrian armies, eventually the Philistines were conquered and their nation was destroyed. Isaiah’s prophecy contrasts the fall of the Philistines with the safety and stability of Zion. He says, “Howl, O gate; cry, O city; thou, whole Palestina, art dissolved” (v. 31). In contrast to this destruction, the “messengers of the nations” will say, “The Lord hath founded Zion, and the poor of his people shall trust in it” (v. 32). The only kingdom that will stand the test of time is the one founded by the Lord of Hosts. Zion, where the “first-born of the poor shall feed, and the needy shall lie down in safety” (v. 30), is the stronghold where the righteous dwell.
 

As the spiritual and temporal leader of his Israelite colony, Nephi understood the inherent weakness of worldly powers, such as Babylon, and the substantive strength in the Lord’s kingdom, known as Zion. He surely wanted his people to be able to discern this contrast. He also knew that this knowledge would bless God’s children in the latter days. Indeed, helping his readers gain this knowledge was a primary reason he quoted Isaiah’s words: “I know that they shall be of great worth unto them in the last days; for in that day shall they understand them; wherefore, for their good have I written them” (2 Nephi 25:8, italics added). As those living in the last days, we should carefully study Isaiah’s prophecies concerning Israel’s eventual triumph over Babylon because his words have special value for us—they are for our good!
 

Nephi’s Prophetic Insights into Understanding Isaiah
 

This is the last chapter in the large block of thirteen Isaiah chapters that Nephi includes in his writings (2 Nephi 12–24). Nephi then adds his own prophetic commentary on Isaiah’s words (2 Nephi 25–32). Nephi’s inspired commentary provides wonderful insights as we study the words of Isaiah in the Book of Mormon. He not only helps us understand what Isaiah says, but he also teaches us why Isaiah is difficult yet understandable. From Nephi’s words we can also learn how to study and understand Isaiah’s teachings and prophecies.7
 

 

In 2 Nephi 25:1, Nephi admits that Isaiah’s words are hard for people to understand, “for they know not concerning the manner of prophesying among the Jews.” We need to understand certain key elements before we can unravel Isaiah’s “manner of prophesying.” First, we should examine his literary mode of communication, his poetic parallelisms and patterns. Knowing how he packages his messages helps us understand their meaning. Second, we need to contemplate the symbols he uses, such as special names, titles, acts, and descriptions. Any poetry is symbolic, and prophetic poetry is especially so. Taken together, these first two elements help us comprehend the technique or mechanics of his writings. Third, we must appreciate his personal teaching style as a profound and “deliberately difficult” prophet. As the Lord instructed Isaiah when he received his prophetic calling, his words were not to be easily understood (2 Nephi 16:9–10/Isaiah 6:9–10). Fourth, we should use scripture keys and tools to unlock Isaiah’s writings. Using footnotes, exploring the Topical Guide and Bible Dictionary entries, and reviewing relevant insights from latter-day prophets and apostles will open our understanding of Isaiah’s gospel messages. Ancient Israelites, such as Nephi’s brothers, understood Isaiah’s Hebrew literary techniques, but, since they were spiritually insensitive, they may have missed the correct interpretation. Today, we can interpret Isaiah messages with the help of prophetic commentaries and scripture aids but, since we may not appreciate his writing mechanics, we stumble over his style and symbols. As we both study his writing technique and learn how to interpret his doctrinal messages, we can master Isaiah’s “manner of prophesying” and understand God’s will for us as found in the profound words of Isaiah.
 

To help us in this process of searching Isaiah’s words, Nephi provides a key to unlocking them: “The words of Isaiah . . . are plain unto all those that are filled with the spirit of prophecy” (2 Nephi 25:4). The “spirit of prophecy” is the means by which the scriptures were originally written.8 The Book of Mormon contains twenty-three references to the “spirit of prophecy,” often in conjunction with the “spirit of revelation.”9 Ultimately, the spirit of prophecy is to have a testimony of Jesus Christ (Revelation 19:10). As we build upon our testimony of Christ and apply our spiritual gifts, we receive revelation through the Holy Spirit and come to understand the scriptures by the same means that they were written—the “spirit of prophecy.” Even the difficult words of Isaiah can become plain for us.
 

Nephi promises additional encouragement to the readers of Isaiah’s words in the last days: “In the days that the prophecies of Isaiah shall be fulfilled men shall know of a surety, at a times when they shall come to pass” (2 Nephi 25:7). We live in a time when many of Isaiah’s prophecies are literally being fulfilled. Even if we had lived contemporary with Isaiah and understood his Hebrew language and literary style, we would not have understood his messages. For example, what would we have imagined as he tried to describe jet planes transporting LDS missionaries to and from their fields of labor in distant, unknown lands?10 We in the latter days who witness the building of Christ’s kingdom in preparation for His millennial reign can surely see and understand how many of Isaiah’s prophecies are finally being fulfilled. We can “know of a surety” that every divine promise recorded by God’s prophet Isaiah will be accomplished. This certainty was promised by Christ Himself when He said that “all things” that Isaiah prophesied “have been and shall be, even according to the words which he spake” (3 Nephi 23:3). God and His prophet do not lie.
 

After providing prophetic insights into understanding Isaiah’s writings in 2 Nephi 25:1–8, Nephi prophesies concerning Jewish and Nephite events leading up to the last days (vv. 9–22) and witnesses of Christ: “We talk of Christ, we rejoice in Christ, we preach of Christ, we prophecy of Christ” (v. 26; see also vv. 23–29). He continues with further prophecies about his descendants and the Gentiles in America, leading up to the time when his records, the Book of Mormon, will come forth in latter times (2 Nephi 26:1–13). He then starts quoting again from Isaiah, from his 29th chapter, which foretells sacred records coming from the ground. These prophecies are discussed in the next chapter.
