# Israel’s Covenant Contract

### Israel’s Covenant Contract
Victor L. Ludlow, Unlocking Isaiah in the Book of Mormon (Salt Lake City 2003)
 
 

Israel’s Covenant Contract
 

1 Nephi 20/Isaiah 48
 

The Lord reveals his purposes to Israel—They have been chosen in the furnace of affliction and are to go forth from Babylon
 

In this first chapter of Isaiah to be quoted in the Book of Mormon, the Lord invites rebellious Israel to establish a new beginning as a covenant people. It provides a doctrinal framework for all people who desire to commit (or recommit) to a righteous course of action. Moses, who centuries earlier had prepared the children of Israel as a covenant people, first established this covenant framework. The Lord’s original covenant contract with the house of Israel is found in the book of Deuteronomy, where Moses addresses a new generation of Israelites ready to enter their promised land under Joshua’s leadership around 1250 b.c.1 Almost six centuries later, near 700 b.c., Isaiah speaks to the humble citizens of Judah who, through divine intervention, have barely survived a major Assyrian attack (Isaiah 36–37). The prophet highlights important conditions for the citizens of Judah who want to remain God’s holy people. Many listeners were refugees from the northern tribes and were new inhabitants in Judah, which was rebuilding after the Assyrian invasion. Included among the many refugees from the tribes of Manasseh and Ephraim were probably the ancestors of Lehi and Ishmael, the patriarchs of the later Book of Mormon community. More than a century after Isaiah delivered this sermon, Nephi quoted it in its entirety to teach his family, newly arrived in their promised land, about their new opportunity to become a faithful, covenant people.
 

1 Nephi 20:1–2: “Who are called by the name of Israel”
 

Isaiah begins the chapter by announcing his audience: the Lord’s chosen people in a variety of spiritual circumstances. He uses seven titles to identify precisely who must “hearken” to his words: the “house of Jacob,” including those from the northern kingdom “called by the name of Israel” and also from the southern kingdom “out of the waters of Judah”; those who “swear by the name of the Lord” and who “make mention of the God of Israel” (v. 1); and those who “call themselves of the holy city” even though they may not “stay themselves upon the God of Israel” (v. 2). Isaiah’s terminology may have been clear to those of his day, but it can be confusing to latter-day readers. So, whom do these seven titles describe?
 

1. “House of Jacob”—the community of the tribes of Israel. That is, the people who are born, married, or adopted into one of the thirteen tribes descended from Jacob’s twelve sons with the blessings promised in Genesis 48–49 and Deuteronomy 33. All of God’s children on earth are invited to become part of this covenant community.
 

2. “Called by the name of Israel”—members of the tribes of Israel, but more significantly, those who are truly “called” by Israel’s name. Translated from Hebrew, Yisrael means “one who prevails with God.” Thus, anyone who is “called by the name of Israel” is one whose faith, righteousness, and spiritual strength allow him or her to prevail with God.
 

3. “Out of the waters of Judah”—actual descendants of Judah, and more important, those who have made covenants with the Lord. Literally, Jews “come forth” as they are born from the “waters of Judah” (or amniotic birth fluid). The blessings of Israel are not offered just to those born of Israelite parents, however. Gentiles are “adopted” into the house of Israel through baptismal covenants. They also come out of the waters of Judah—“out of the waters of baptism.”
 

This metaphorical birth into the house of Israel through baptism is an important doctrinal message because many people believe that baptism by immersion is not taught in the Old Testament. Contrary to this misunderstanding, Joseph Smith added the phrase “or out of the waters of baptism” to this verse in the second English edition of the Book of Mormon. This revealed phrase, unique to the Book of Mormon, clarifies that the Old Testament phrase “out of the waters of Judah” is a symbolic reference to the baptismal ordinance.
 

Why is there no direct reference to baptism in the Old Testament? The Prophet Joseph Smith and President Joseph Fielding Smith both explain that baptism is a Greek, not a Hebrew, word.2 Thus, during Isaiah’s day, when people spoke and wrote in Hebrew, no documented word existed that described the act of baptism, or “immersion.” We know, however, that an ordinance of immersion was performed. For such ritual washing, there was a large “brazen sea,” or font, that rested on twelve oxen in the temple courtyard at Jerusalem during Isaiah’s day. In the Old Testament, the words washing and cleansing represent the covenant of purification through a water immersion. Indeed, modern converts to Orthodox Judaism must perform a ritual “washing” or “immersion” before they are considered full members of the Jewish community. Thus, Isaiah’s phrase “com[ing] forth out of the waters of Judah” is a metaphoric reference to baptism by immersion.
 

4. “Swear by the name of the Lord”—those people who have made sacred vows with their Lord. The Hebrew verb shava (to swear) is a specific reference to covenant making. It is not used any other way in the Old Testament. The Lord’s covenant people may invoke His name and claim that He will protect and deliver them, but His power in their behalf is conditional upon their righteousness, as seen in the end of verse 1. Unfortunately, their claim is not verified by their worthiness. Also, note in verse 11 that the Lord will not allow His name to be polluted or “profaned” with hypocritical statements.
 

5. “Make mention of the God of Israel”—those people who are bold enough to teach and testify of their God. The Hebrew word for “God” in this passage is Elohim, meaning “of the gods” or “the supreme God,” the sacred name of our Heavenly Father. The Israelites were living in the Fertile Crescent of the ancient Near East, where their teachings and example could have enlightened the peoples of Asia, Africa, and Europe. Instead, as we see in the example of the idolatrous northern kingdom of Israel, they often followed the gods of the pagan nations.
 

After identifying those who make covenants with the Lord and invoke God in their expressions, Isaiah proclaims that their words have not been truthful and their behavior has not been righteous. Their religious fervor is simply zealous hypocrisy. He highlights more of their claims in the next verse.
 

6. “Call themselves of the holy city”—the chosen people who are associated with Jerusalem, the holy city of Isaiah’s time. These are people who could connect with the holy city of Zion, the ideal community founded by Enoch (Moses 7:18–21, Psalm 60:14). In the Book of Mormon context, the phrase may also refer to another holy city, the New Jerusalem, which is associated with the events of the Second Coming and the gathering of the tribes of Israel. However, these people just “call themselves” as citizens of Zion. The Lord and His prophet do not identify them as such.
 

7. “Stay themselves upon the God of Israel”—those who should be relying upon their Heavenly Father but are not.3 That is, they do not endure in obedience to the end of their mortal probation. Instead of overcoming life’s temptations and tribulations, they follow the ways of the world. However, Isaiah prophesies that in the latter days, gathering remnants of Israel will indeed rest upon the Lord for support (2 Nephi 20:20/Isaiah 10:20).
 

At the end of verse 2, Isaiah testifies that the “Lord of hosts” is to be the God of all these groups. Ideally, they will now make and keep their solemn vows with Him.
 

In these two verses, Isaiah identifies various parties who should be honoring their vows with God. Because Isaiah speaks to both ancient and modern Israelites, to birth and adopted members of Jacob’s family, to rebellious apostates and faithful followers of the Lord, and especially to all who make vows with the Lord and want to share His gospel truths, Isaiah’s message is for all who desire to become covenant members of the Lord’s kingdom on earth. If we want to be called by any of these noble titles listed by Isaiah, we should study this chapter carefully! But remember, a title describing one’s close relationship with God must be verified by obedient righteousness and faithful service.
 

1 Nephi 20:3–8: “I have declared the former things from the beginning”
 

Isaiah next describes how the Lord Himself verifies His prophesied, covenant relationship with His people. In verses 3–8, the Lord reveals His pattern of dealing with His children: He foretells His works and then fulfills them. All too often, the “obstinate” and stubborn children of Israel do not recognize His hand in their affairs, even when He is fulfilling His promises (v. 4). The Lord’s ancient chastisement still applies: in 1844, the Prophet Joseph Smith gave a similar reprimand, stating that the Saints are frequently unprepared for challenges, even though God has promised He will help them.4 As children of a loving Heavenly Father, we must show faith in Him and His Son as they help us handle adversity.
 

These verses focus on Jehovah’s role as supreme ruler of heaven and earth. Isaiah emphasizes the Lord’s omniscient power, explaining that He has a complete knowledge of “things from the beginning” (v. 3). Indeed, all things past, present, and future are within His power. Through the ages, He has “declared the former things” (v. 3) and they “came to pass” (v. 5). In these verses, Isaiah speaks for the Lord, using synonymous parallelisms to explain that Jehovah has foretold many of His great acts so that His people would not be able to mistake His work for the work of their idols. The people, however, stubbornly refuse to see or understand the Lord’s divine efforts in their lives.
 

The people are so stubborn in their rejection of God that Isaiah compares them to metal elements unable to bend. Using emblamatic parallelisms, the prophet says that their necks are as “iron sinew,” suggesting their unwillingness to turn to face God, and their brows are as “brass,” alluding to their insensitivity to divine promptings (v. 4). Their wickedness and hardheadedness cause them to ignore the Lord’s prophecies. The imagery of iron and brass also alludes to their metal-crafted idols. Isaiah describes these material icons as “graven” and “molten” images (v. 5). By referring to both the idols and the people as metal objects, Isaiah is essentially saying that the children of Israel have become like the statues they worship—stiff, stubborn, and resistant to the prophecies of the Lord. If we idolize anything or anyone worldly, then we too could suffer the fate of these ancient idol worshipers as we become more and more like that which we adore.
 

In reviewing this historical relationship between the Lord and Israel, Isaiah establishes a doctrinal context from which God can invite Israel to repent and to come unto Him in a new covenant relationship. In a similar manner, Moses reviewed Israel’s wanderings and God’s miracles as he prepared the children of Israel to become a covenant people at Mt. Nebo (Deuteronomy 1–4). Nephi also established a historical foundation and a doctrinal purpose for his quoting Isaiah to his family in the new world (1 Nephi 18:23–25; 19:18–24). With this preparation, the reader is now ready to draw nearer to the Lord in a covenant relationship.
 

1 Nephi 20:9–11: “I have chosen thee in the furnace of affliction”
 

Although the children of Israel were (and still are) stubborn and unrighteous at times, the Lord explains that He will forgive them and eventually exalt them. As they repent and take His name upon them, they will experience His merciful love. Isaiah describes the Lord’s great mercy in verse 9, writing, “For my name’s sake will I defer mine anger.” What does that mean? Elder James E. Talmage quotes from a Bible scholar to explain:
 

“Name . . . in the scriptures not only [means] that by which a person is designated, but frequently [means] all that is known to belong to the person having this designation, and the person himself. Thus ‘the name of God’ or ‘of Jehovah,’ etc., indicates His authority (Deuteronomy 18:20; Matthew 21:9, etc.), His dignity and glory (Isaiah 48:9, etc.), His protection and favor (Proverbs 18:10, etc.), His character (Exodus 34:5, 14, compare 6–7, etc.), His divine attributes in general (Matthew 6:9, etc.), etc. The Lord is said to set or put His name where the revelation or manifestation of His perfections is made (Deuteronomy 12:5, 14:24, etc.).”5
 

Thus we see that the Lord says “for my name’s sake” to reveal His perfection. He lovingly forgives and redeems us because of His divine nature. Indeed, His attributes are best described by His most commonly used name—Jesus, meaning “Savior.” This name also assumes covenant significance as we “take His name upon us” when we are baptized and thereafter partake of the sacrament.
 

In verse 10, the Lord explains that He has chosen the children of Israel “in the furnace of affliction.” Valuable materials are usually put in a refiner’s fire—gold, silver, iron, brass, and so on. Earlier in verse 4, He compared Israel to iron and brass. Here, the children of Israel are again compared to precious ores and elements. The metals in the furnace undergo intense heat and softening during the refining process as they are molded into a new form. The chosen people must similarly go through difficult trials to become softened, pliable, and purified. This imagery applies directly to a covenant people because it describes the cleansing and molding power of the Holy Spirit. Refining experiences are uncomfortable, but the eventual purity obtained is worth the purging process. Our many mortal experiences serve as our furnace of affliction, and as we humble ourselves and repent of our weaknesses, Christ truly serves as our Refiner. Elder Bruce R. McConkie explains how Christ serves as this purifier:
 

Christ is the Refiner (Isaiah 48:10; Zechariah 13:9; 1 Nephi 20:10). His mission is to cleanse, purify, and refine the human soul so that it can return to his Father’s kingdom in purity, free from dross (3 Nephi 27:19–21). His cleansing power “is like a refiner’s fire, And he shall sit as a refiner and purifier of silver” in that great day when he comes to judge the world (Malachi 3:2–3; 3 Nephi 24:2–3; D&C 128:24).6
 

It is a choice blessing to experience the cleansing power of our Refiner while we pass through this probationary period.
 

We learn from President Hunter that the purging process in verse 10 applies to “regular” Saints, not just to prophets. He testifies that many prophets learn that God refines people “in the furnace of affliction” as they lead faithful lives even during extremely difficult times. He then says:
 

Today other biographies of faith are being written—Saints who, like Job, suffer physical pain, emotional sorrow, and even disloyalty from friends—yet remain faithful; Saints who, like Jacob, see sons and daughters not so valiant as they should be, but who bless them for their potential; Saints who, like Paul, endure great ridicule and endure to the end; Saints who, like Nephi, must separate themselves from family because of their commitment to the gospel. There are those who know pain and sorrow because of loss of loved ones; who know spiritual sorrow because children go astray; who experience loss of health, financial reverses, and emotional distress, and yet, like Job, resolve, “When he hath tried me, I shall come forth as gold” (Job 23:10).7
 

We each have the potential to endure our earthly hardships and purifying with the patience of Job and the other prophets.
 

In verse 11, the Lord continues His description of His power and glory. He teaches the chosen people that He will not tolerate His “name to be polluted,” or dishonored. In other words, we must be careful about how we use the name of the Lord. We who have taken His name upon us defile it by misrepresenting Him and pollute it by disobeying His commandments. Elder Dallin H. Oaks uses this scripture to explain that we must not take the sacred names of God the Father or His Son, Jesus Christ, in vain. He teaches that the Lord has given this commandment to all His children: ancient Israel (Exodus 20:7), the Book of Mormon people (Mosiah 13:5), and us today (D&C 136:21).8
 

1 Nephi 20:12–17: “Come ye near unto me”
 

In the next verses, the Lord strongly requests that Isaiah’s listeners and readers hear and understand His words. Phrases like “hearken unto me” (v. 12), “all ye, assemble yourselves, and hear” (v. 14), “come ye near unto me” (v. 16), and “thus saith the Lord” (v. 17) direct our attention toward Isaiah’s important messages of peace, eternal covenants, and exaltation.
 

In verse 12, the Lord describes His role as the “first” and the “last.” Christ is the “first” because He was both the first to attain a spiritual body (as the Firstborn of Heavenly Father), as well as the first to receive His godhood (by becoming a God in the beginning). Jesus likewise represents the “last” because of His continuing role as a God, which will last “to all eternity, everlasting, without end.”9 For a more in-depth look at the role of Christ as an eternal being, Elder Talmage suggests reading Revelation 1:11, 17; 2:8; 22:13; and Isaiah 41:4; 44:6.10 These passages and verse 12 highlight the eternal nature of God. As such, the citation of this verse in 1 Nephi helps fulfill a major purpose of the Book of Mormon, which is to convince Jew and Gentile that Jesus is the “Eternal God” (title page, second paragraph).
 

Verses 13–17 emphasize a variety of roles that the Lord God, Jesus, fulfills. In verse 13, Isaiah speaks for the Creator as he portrays Jehovah’s hand laying “the foundation of the earth” and “spann[ing] the heavens.” Typical of Isaiah’s writings, there is symbolic meaning in even the simplest of images. The “right hand” in this verse is especially emblematic and depicts some fundamental characteristics of the Lord. Other scriptural texts enrich the possible meaning of Jehovah’s “right hand.” Exodus 15:6 reveals that it is the right hand of God that “is become glorious in power,” and His right hand that “hath dashed in pieces the enemy.” Moses similarly depicts the symbolic power of the Lord’s right hand, declaring to his people, “The Lord came from Sinai and, . . . from his right hand went a fiery law” (Deuteronomy 33:2). In these contexts, the right hand symbolizes our Master’s powerful creative abilities. Other scriptures refer to the Lord’s right hand as a place of exaltation, glory, and rest. Alma explains that all those who “rejoice and exult in the hope” of the gospel will someday “dwell at the right hand of God, in a state of never-ending happiness” (Alma 28:12). Thus, the Lord’s right hand extends a promise of strength and support.
 

Isaiah’s imagery of the hand may also allude to the covenant state of the children of Israel. Traditionally, the right hand was used in the making and sealing of sacred covenants (like the modern-day handshake used to “seal a deal”). Thus, in calling to the children of Israel with His right hand, God is referring to the sacred covenants that He enters into with us if we are righteous. God is “shaking hands” or raising His hand “to the square” with us, promising to keep His end of the bargain if we keep ours! As Christ promises, “I, the Lord, am bound when ye do what I say; but when ye do not what I say, ye have no promise” (D&C 82:10).
 

Isaiah next describes the Lord’s role as a spokesman of His own words. In verse 14 He asks, “Who among them hath declared these things?” meaning “Who has declared (and who will continue to declare) the truth to those around them?” He does not directly answer the question but rather highlights further characteristics of the servant who will help fulfill God’s purposes (vv. 14–15). Through Isaiah’s description, we know that the servant is beloved of God, called of God, helped by God, and given power to wield over the unrighteous (such as the Babylonians during Isaiah’s time and the wicked during our own day). Verse 17 accents two additional roles of the Lord: He is to teach us the Father’s commandments and lead us on the way back to Heavenly Father.
 

Scholars wonder if the servant described in these verses could be an individual, such as Isaiah or King Cyrus of Persia (who liberated the Babylonian Jews), or if the servant could represent a community, such as the house of Israel or Christ’s church. These could be secondary designations of the servant, but the primary identification is with Jehovah—He who declares and executes the Father’s plan of salvation. Thus, in these few verses, Isaiah testifies of at least five valuable roles of this Eternal God:
 

• The Creator God of the Father’s works (v. 13).
 

• The Advocate God of the Father’s plan (v. 14).
 

• The Administrator God of the Father’s will (v. 15).
 

• The Teacher God of the Father’s commandments (v. 17).
 

• The Leader God of the Father’s way (v. 17).
 

A major challenge in reading verses 12–17 is recognizing how the Lord is often talking about Himself in the third person in these passages. Readers of this chapter are often frustrated in identifying who is speaking in the different passages and understanding the constant shifts between first, second, and third person. Is it Isaiah or the Lord (Jehovah) or God (Elohim) speaking, and to whom and about whom are they talking, especially when the Lord talks about Himself? In verse 1, Isaiah (or the Lord through Isaiah) addresses Israel in the second person, inviting them to hearken to the following message. But at the end of verse 1 and in the next verse, he shifts to the third person as he talks about the people of Israel and describes their separation from the Lord. Then the Lord Himself speaks to Israel in the first person as He recounts His challenges with them (vv. 3–8), His personal witness to them (vv. 9–11), and His invitation to them (vv. 12–17). Yet in this last passage, the Lord seems to address the whole world (“all ye”; v. 14) and talks to them about Israel, whom He describes in the third person plural (“them”).
 

Similar shifts of point of view occur as the Lord is described, even by Himself. For example, in verse 16, it seems that the Lord Jesus Christ is speaking and declaring that the Lord God (Heavenly Father) and His Spirit (the Holy Ghost) have sent Him. Yet in verses 15 and 17, although the Lord still seems to be talking, He is also being talked about in the third person. So here the Lord is describing Himself as though He were another person. In a special way, He is bearing personal witness of Himself. This combination of first and third person is epitomized in the Lord’s short yet encompassing testimony: “I am He!” (v. 12).
 

Although we may have a hard time following Isaiah’s description of the servant, we should recognize many of His qualities. We should then strive diligently to attain these attributes. We do this best as we enter into a covenant relationship with Heavenly Father in His holy house, the temple. Heavenly Father has a plan for each us, and we come to understand His purposes for us as we honor our temple covenants. To prepare ourselves for the temple, we must invite the Spirit into our daily lives and live according to Christ’s word. As we take His name upon us, remember Him, and keep His commandments, we honor our covenant relationship with Him. As we fulfill the stipulations of His expectations, we become heirs of His blessings, as promised in the last verses of this chapter.
 

1 Nephi 20:18–22: “With a voice of singing declare ye”
 

The remainder of the chapter emphasizes the joy that comes from spreading the gospel, and the blessings that accompany an acceptance of spiritual gifts and a rejection of worldly materialism. Just as the children of Israel are commanded to “go . . . forth of Babylon” (v. 20), so we too must leave the world behind us as we come unto the Lord.
 

In verses 18 and 19, Isaiah, speaking for the Lord, laments the wonderful blessings that the wicked Israelites are throwing away. He compares the peace of righteousness that the Israelites could obtain to an endlessly flowing river and to infinitely rolling waves of the sea. This particular imagery is significant because water is often used in the scriptures to symbolize the gospel, the teachings of Christ, the peace of the Holy Ghost, and the love of God. Lehi makes a similar analogy between righteousness and water in the Book of Mormon when he says to his son Laman, “O that thou mightest be like unto this river, continually running into the fountain of all righteousness!” (1 Nephi 2:9). Like Isaiah, Lehi sees a powerful parallel between the gentle yet powerful flow of a river and the calm, unyielding dedication to righteous obedience.
 

In verse 19, Isaiah uses another simile to describe the blessings withheld from the unrighteous. His comparison of endless offspring to the sands of the sea is similar to the Lord’s promise of eternal posterity to Abraham in Genesis 22:17. The promise of eternal posterity is offered to all who remember “His name,” or the name of Jesus Christ. Remembering His name does not simply mean that we should think of the words “Jesus Christ.” As discussed earlier, His “name” represents His perfection and all His attributes. Thus, we should do those things that help us remember His name and His Atonement and great love for us.
 

Isaiah next refers to the release of the Jews from their Babylonian captors in 538 b.c. and, symbolically, to our coming out from the wicked world (v. 20). He instructs the liberated children of the house of Israel to declare His redemption to the world. President Joseph Fielding Smith implored the Saints to preach the gospel, clarifying Isaiah’s words when he wrote, “‘Go ye out from Babylon,’ which is the world . . . [for] there are many righteous people scattered throughout the earth, and it is our duty to search them out and give unto them the gospel of Jesus Christ.”11 Isaiah’s request that Israel preach “with a voice of singing” suggests how we should share the gospel—with joy and happiness. Psalm 9:11 presents a similar appeal, calling on Israel to “sing praises to the Lord, which dwelleth in Zion: declare among the people his doings.” We should heed Isaiah’s counsel and declare the Lord’s redemption “with a voice of singing.”
 

In verse 21, Isaiah compares the trials of his own day to the trials the children of Israel suffered while journeying in the wilderness. Although the Israelites encountered many obstacles and hardships on their journey to the promised land, the Lord provided for them and sent them sustenance in the desert. Isaiah’s image of water flowing from a rock represents the Savior’s protective care and redeeming power. When the children of Israel began to murmur in the wilderness for lack of water, Moses hit a rock with his staff and water flowed forth (Exodus 17). This action symbolizes the role of the Savior, who is the Rock that provides spiritual water. Paul also said that the children of Israel “drank of that spiritual Rock that followed them: and that Rock was Christ” (1 Corinthians 10:4). Thus, in our travels through the spiritual deserts of the world, we can nourish our spirits through the waters of the Savior’s gospel. We each need to develop a testimony of Christ’s words to the woman of Samaria: “Whosoever drinketh of the water that I shall give him shall never thirst; but the water that I shall give him shall be in him a well of water springing up into everlasting life” (John 4:14).
 

The famous phrase from verse 22, “there is no peace, saith the Lord, unto the wicked,” may lead us to wonder about the definition of wicked. Who is denied peace? Elder Bruce R. McConkie helps us understand that “the wicked” are evil people whose behavior is not in line with divine standards. They live “a telestial law because they are doing the things which will assure them of an eventual telestial inheritance.” He continues,
 

Wicked acts contaminate both body and spirit (Mark 7:20–23). “There is no peace, saith the Lord, unto the wicked.” . . . They are “in the gall of bitterness and in the bonds of iniquity; they are without God in the world, and they have gone contrary to the nature of God; therefore, they are in a state contrary to the nature of happiness.” In effect they suffer hell on earth. “Wickedness never was happiness.” (Alma 41:10–11.)12
 

It is wise to evaluate our behavior each week, identifying things we do that obstruct the peace of the Spirit from coming into our hearts.
 

The Lord’s work will roll forward, despite the mistakes and weaknesses of His children. Even though we sometimes cannot understand why, the Lord has chosen a people through whom He will accomplish His purposes. Isaiah addresses this chapter to those who want to be a part of this chosen people, either by claims of lineage or by promises of covenant. As we prevail with God as true Israelites, we are heirs to wonderful blessings. As we obey His commandments, we will enjoy “peace . . . as a river” and “righteousness as the waves of the sea” (v. 18). Isaiah delivered this message to the citizens of Judah so they could reestablish themselves as God’s covenant people. Nephi wanted his people in their new land of covenant promise to enjoy such blessings and to “believe in the Lord their Redeemer,” so he quoted Isaiah’s words as found in 1 Nephi 20 (1 Nephi 19:23). Those who have come unto Christ need to remember the Lord’s desire to refine us, even through the furnaces of affliction. As we rely upon Him, He will purify and bless us. As we leave Babylon, we take His message of redemptive, merciful love to the world. As we invite others to enter into a covenant relationship with God, we build His kingdom on earth.
