# Prophecies of the Messiah

### Prophecies of the Messiah
Victor L. Ludlow, Unlocking Isaiah in the Book of Mormon (Salt Lake City 2003)
 
 

Prophecies of the Messiah
 

2 Nephi 19/Isaiah 9
 

Isaiah speaks Messianically—The people in darkness shall see a great Light—Unto us a Child is born— He shall be the Prince of Peace and reign on David’s throne
 

This chapter contains one of the most familiar passages in the large block of Isaiah writings found in 2 Nephi. It includes inspiring descriptions of the Savior that were popularized in Handel’s Messiah. Isaiah’s prophetic promise of a special child who would bring light into a darkened world and be the prince of peace provides comfort to readers of all generations.
 

2 Nephi 19:1–5: “The people that walked in darkness have seen a great light”
 

The beginning verses of this chapter provide a contrast to the dark and foreboding close of chapter 18.1 Isaiah describes the region around the Sea of Galilee and speaks of light and protection rather than darkness and damnation as he promises the children of Israel blessings in some unnamed future period. Isaiah scholars offer several different interpretations of this passage, including:
 

• A historical application: The darkness represents Assyria and her threat from the north to the people of Judah (after Assyria had destroyed the northern kingdom of Israel); the light represents the king (Hezekiah) who will protect his people from Assyrian imperialist expansion.
 

• A religious application: The darkness represents the wicked actions and consequential punishment of the northern tribes of Israel; the light represents the Israelites’ recognition of earlier sins and their attempt at religious reforms after the Assyrian invasions, as directed by Hezekiah and Isaiah.
 

• A messianic application: The darkness represents wickedness and apostasy, especially during the pagan Roman times; the light represents Christ, who would bring peace and redemption from sin to the earth.2
 

It may seem strange to have different explanations for the same scripture. How can certain passages mean more than one thing? Isaiah wrote in this seemingly complicated manner so that his readers would need to be spiritually in tune in order to understand his prophecies as they might be fulfilled in different contexts and time periods. Also, scriptures with multiple fulfillments are not only applicable to various time periods but they can also contain varied levels of meaning, such as historical, symbolic, messianic, and eschatological (having to do with the last days). Looking at a particular passage in different perspectives can help us “liken all scriptures unto us, that it might be for our profit and learning” (1 Nephi 19:23).
 

The third interpretation of these verses—that the darkness and light represent the falling away of the covenant people and the coming of the Savior—becomes even more significant when we realize that Christ did come to a land of darkness as He began His public ministry. Territories in northern Israel around the Sea of Galilee, such as Capernaum, were known as the “lands of darkness” because many wars had devastated the land. The territory was also literally dark because the local stone construction of the area was black basalt rock. Thus, the Galilean landscape, with its walls and buildings, actually has a constant dark shadow upon it. Symbolically, Galilee was considered a backward province with no great scholars or places of learning. Its inhabitants were not enlightened or advanced in their social and economic development. Christ’s presence in this land of darkness provided a light to its inhabitants—especially the light of the gospel, which brought freedom from the darkness of spiritual ignorance. This pattern of light coming out of darkness also parallels events in the last days. Latter-day prophets and the scriptures tell us that times of turmoil and punishment will occur just before the arrival of the Savior. Imagine not knowing anything about Jesus Christ and His restored gospel. Life would be dark and hopeless without His eternal truths and blessings. We would have no hope without the light this Man from Galilee brought to the earth.
 

In verse 3, Isaiah describes a great joy accompanying the arrival of the “great light” (v. 2). He compares the prosperity and gladness of the people to “the joy in harvest” and their rejoicing when dividing the spoil. Anciently, the harvest was a joyful time—a time of receiving abundance from the land. God not only provides abundance in an annual harvest, but He also shares eternal gifts.
 

On a spiritual level, Isaiah is likely referring to the Atonement’s blessings when he prophesies of the coming of the light. Christ’s great sacrifice gives all people the opportunity to receive the blessings of God’s love as promised in His plan of salvation. Isaiah’s allusion to the joy of the harvest is also symbolic of the gathering of righteous souls that occurs in the latter days. The Prophet Joseph Smith tells us to spread the gospel diligently, for, as the Lord taught him, “the field is white already to harvest; and lo, he that thrusteth in his sickle with his might, the same layeth up in store that he perisheth not, but bringeth salvation to his soul” (D&C 4:4; compare 3 Nephi 20:18). Are we reaping the joy of the harvest in these latter times? Isaiah is also speaking to our day, counseling us to share the gospel blessings and light with friends and family.
 

Isaiah prophesies that the appearance of the “great light” will also bring freedom from spiritual bondage—a liberation that occurs with the gift of the Atonement. In verse 4, he explains that Christ (“the light”) will break the yoke, the staff, and the rod as burdens and oppression are taken from us. Isaiah, in the biblical text, compares this great rescue with the miracle of Midian, when a tiny band of Israelites under Gideon’s leadership defeated the massive armies of the Midianites and their allies (Judges 7). Similarly, in the latter days, a relatively small number of righteous Saints will be able to withstand the wicked dominion of Satan and his legions (D&C 133:58; 3 Nephi 20:16). If we are faithful and loyal to the covenants we have made, He will carry our burdens and free us from our oppressors. What wonderful relief!
 

Isaiah again refers to bondage and captivity in verse 5, perhaps prophesying about the eventual battle of good and evil from which the righteous Saints will emerge as victors. Isaiah describes “garments rolled in blood,” alluding to the Second Coming when the Savior will arrive dressed in robes of “red . . . like him that treadeth in the winefat” (Isaiah 63:2). Isaiah also depicts the wicked as “burning and fuel of fire”—a possible reference to the final destruction of the unrighteous and the cleansing of the earth by fire.
 

2 Nephi 19:6–7: “Unto us a child is born”
 

And what will bring us this ultimate victory? Following the first five verses about light prevailing over darkness, the chapter moves into its most pertinent subject matter—the mission of the Savior. Isaiah provides more insight about the “great light” discussed in earlier verses, declaring, “Unto us a child is born / unto us a son is given.” These synonymous phrases of the Savior’s birth emphasize two things: the importance of His physical entry into an earthly existence and the gift of God’s Son with His special ministry. By twice repeating the words “unto us” in the opening of verse 6, Isaiah establishes a major point: it was for our benefit that the Savior came to earth. As we look closely at the first half of verse 6, we see a complete representation of Christ’s mission: it begins with the description of His birth (“unto us a child is born”) and ends with an account of His millennial reign (“the government shall be upon his shoulder”). This passage also focuses our attention on the fact that although the Savior was sent into the most humble earthly circumstances to serve us, He is, in reality, the Ruler of the universe.
 

The second half of verse 6 describes many profound aspects of the Savior’s role in the plan of salvation. Isaiah lists four sets of titles and modifiers to define Christ’s majestic traits. In the Hebrew pattern, they would appear like this: and His name shall be called: Counselor—wonderful, God—mighty, Father—everlasting, Prince—peaceful. These four titles of Wonderful Counselor, the Mighty God, the Everlasting Father, and the Prince of Peace reflect the wisdom, power, glory, and love of our Lord. They highlight different important dimensions of His mission among humankind:
 

Wonderful Counselor. In studying and discussing this passage, we identify many wonderful ways we receive counsel, direction, teachings, and guidance from Jesus. He reveals helpful gospel truths through the scriptures and the light of His Spirit. Also, He is our counselor or advocate with the Father; He represents us before God’s throne. Whom else would we want to be our Wonderful Counselor?
 

Mighty God. Long before He was born of Mary in Bethlehem, Jesus demonstrated the powers of Godhood. As the great Jehovah, he supported God the Father’s plan and volunteered to be its Savior. With the premortal Hebrew name of Yahweh, He was the Lord of the Old Testament, the God of Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and their righteous descendants. As witnessed in the New Testament, He became God in the flesh as He performed miracles and then brought about the great atoning sacrifice—which only God could do. He opened the gates of death and hell, and He continues to expand His reign as a mighty God of eternity. As the Book of Mormon testifies, He is an Eternal God (title page, second paragraph).
 

Everlasting Father. The “child” or “son” given unto us is not only our elder, protecting brother, but He is also our “Everlasting Father”! Elder Bruce R. McConkie describes this somewhat confusing relationship: “Christ is the Father in the sense that he is the Creator, the Maker, the Organizer of the heavens and of the earth, and all things that in them are (Isaiah 9:6; 2 Nephi 19:6; Mosiah 15:4; 16:15; Alma 11:38–39; Ether 4:7).”3 Jesus Christ is the Father of the earth because He is the Creator. He is also the Father of His gospel, church, and kingdom. He is the Father of salvation and redemption. He is the Father of our spiritual rebirth, and we can become His spiritually begotten children (Mosiah 5). And through the divine law of investiture, He fully represents our Father in Heaven in all the Father’s affairs with us. In many ways, Jesus serves as an Everlasting Father for us.
 

Prince of Peace. Any of us can enjoy the peace of Christ’s love as we follow Him in righteousness. Eventually the whole world will recognize and enjoy His peaceful millennial reign (Isaiah 45). And the ultimate, eternal destiny of this earth and its inhabitants will be to enjoy celestial peace. Although Christianity does not seem to have brought peace to this earth, eventually Jesus Christ and His true covenant followers will be synonymous with peace as He reigns upon the earth as the Prince of Peace.
 

In four short phrases, Isaiah highlights Christ’s roles as a righteous teacher and judge; as a mighty, royal son; as the father of life and salvation; and as the ruler and redeemer of the Earth. These titles provide rich opportunity for enlightening gospel discussion with family and friends as we appreciate how the Savior guides our lives to prepare ourselves and the world for His glorious return.
 

Verse 7 seems to describe the earth’s growth and progression during Christ’s reign. Governing from David’s throne, He will establish His kingdom on earth with order, judgment, and justice. Indeed, His power comes from God, His order provides stability, His judgments are absolute, His justice is faultless, and His mercy is infinite. With His zeal, He will do it all for us.
 

Although Isaiah wrote centuries before the coming of the Lord, his prophecies concerning the mission of the Savior are just as powerful as if he had written his account during or after Jesus’ life. His early witness is just as powerful and insightful as the later ones of Matthew, Mark, Luke, John, Nephi, Mormon, and Joseph Smith. In addition, Isaiah’s prophecies go beyond providing comfort to the people of his time; Nephi cites this passage to strengthen the people of early Nephite times. Isaiah’s description of the Savior as a Prince of Peace is also especially significant to members of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, because they not only face their own private tribulations but they also experience much turmoil and trouble in the time that precedes the second coming of the Savior before He establishes His millennial era of peace on earth.
 

Interestingly, many Jews misinterpreted Isaiah’s portrayal of the Prince of Peace, thinking that the Messiah’s first coming would bring political peace and prosperity to the earth. They thought that He would come as a majestic ruler to free the children of Israel from their political bondage under the Romans. When Jesus arrived as a meek and humble stepson of a carpenter, prepared to save mankind from spiritual bondage, many Jews rejected Him. This misconception, among others, led many Jews to reject Christ as their Savior. The same misconceptions exist today. Elder J. Reuben Clark explains this dilemma in powerful words:
 

Modern man sometimes vainly thinks that Jesus’ mission was to wipe out war; and scoffers have cried that since war still curses the earth, Christ’s mission has failed and Christianity is a blight. . . . Christ did proclaim a peace—the peace of everlasting righteousness, which is the eternal and mortal enemy of sin. Between righteousness and sin, in whatever form, there can only be unceasing war. . . . This war cannot end until sin is crushed and Christ brings all flesh under his dominion. Righteousness is peace wherever it abides; sin in itself is war wherever it is found.4
 

Sadly, many are still waiting for the Prince of Peace’s millennial mission, unaware that the peace and protection they seek is available through Christ’s gospel. Until the Savior comes again, the world will remain a site for senseless violence and war. However, with Christ as the Peacemaker of our souls, we can find peaceful joy. Isaiah’s visionary words concerning this child of light are vitally significant to us today!
 

2 Nephi 19:8–12: “In the pride and stoutness of heart”
 

In verse 8, Isaiah returns to a discussion about the Israelites in his own day, describing the Lord’s warning to the tribes of Jacob. This foreshadows the Assyrian destruction of the kingdom of Israel and the scattering of the northern ten tribes that began in 732 b.c. Isaiah illustrates the pride of the wicked northern Israelites, twice describing their scoffing at the possibility of invasion. When threatened with the destruction of their buildings (“bricks are fallen down”), the Israelites mock Isaiah, proudly declaring that they will rebuild the structures with more beautiful “hewn” stones. When warned that their sycamores will be destroyed, they claim they will replant with even better “cedar” trees (v. 10). What arrogance! Because the kingdom of Israel is overwhelmingly proud, the Lord sends Assyria (the “adversaries of Rezin” [v. 11]) against them. The northern ten tribes are thus scattered and driven into captivity. We know that similar punishment will come to those who ignore the Lord’s warnings in the latter days. The wicked will be destroyed or scattered as adversaries are sent against them.
 

Although God harshly punishes the Israelites, Isaiah describes in verse 12 that the Lord’s “anger is not turned away,” suggesting that further punishment is still possible. However, His hand is “stretched out still,” implying that repentance and redemption are also possible. This image of the Lord’s stretched-out hand is both a potential condemnation and a reassurance to the Israelites. If they reject His helping hand, they will be subject to further punishment. But, if they come unto Him, they can be rescued. This promise of continuing justice (anger not turned away) and mercy (hand still stretched out) is repeated two more times in this chapter (vv. 17, 21).
 

2 Nephi 19:13–17: “The leaders of this people cause them to err”
 

Isaiah describes the complete destruction of the wicked Israelites who refuse to turn to the merciful hand of the Lord. He explains that the “head” (the king and other leaders) is leading the Israelites in their wickedness. Isaiah then mockingly describes the false prophets (apostate priests and teachers) as the “tail” of the people, representing the lowest and most despicable of sinners (v. 15). We can relate this scripture to the wickedness of our own society today. Many of our political and business leaders set poor examples of moral behavior and integrity. Also, numerous religious and social leaders try to turn us away from the truth. By the power of the Holy Spirit, we can determine if we are being led astray. As we follow the words of God’s prophets, we will stay on the path to truth.
 

Isaiah teaches us that, because of their completely wicked state, the children of Israel would not receive the mercy of the Lord, and they would become so wicked that they would ignore even the fatherless and widowed. Isaiah then repeats his twofold promise that the Lord’s “anger is not turned away, but his hand is stretched out still” (v. 17) again suggesting that the Lord will destroy those who are unrighteous but have mercy on those who return to Him.
 

2 Nephi 19:18–21: “Wickedness burneth as the fire”
 

The final burning and destruction of the wicked Israelites is depicted in verse 18. As in most of Isaiah’s writings, this refers to both the people of Isaiah’s time (the unrighteous ten tribes) as well as the people in the latter days. Isaiah explains that the destruction will be so devastating that people will not help each other. Because of severe agricultural and economic distress, they will steal food from each other and eventually even eat the flesh of their own dead. These catastrophes did indeed occur during Isaiah’s time, and they will occur again in the latter days.
 

In verse 21, Isaiah describes the combined fury of the tribes of Joseph (Ephraim and Manasseh) against Judah. He then repeats his prophetic declaration that “for all this [the Lord’s] anger is not turned away, but his hand is stretched out still.” Even though the Israelites are facing complete destruction through their civil war and the impending Assyrian attack, the Lord still holds out an offering of mercy and pleads with them to repent of their wicked ways. Before our selfishness and wickedness carry us toward catastrophe, we must reach out to our Savior so He can pull us toward Him with His outstretched hand and welcome us with open arms.
 

As with many of Isaiah’s writings, this chapter foretells the Messiah’s power and compassion to audiences beyond the people of Isaiah’s time. Most pertinent of his revelations are Isaiah’s descriptive titles of the Savior, who was to come some seven centuries later. This chasm between Isaiah’s time and the time of the Savior does not weaken the prophet’s powerful vision of the King of the world. Elder James E. Talmage comments on Isaiah’s role as futuristic seer who “with the forcefulness of direct revelation told of the Virgin’s divine maternity, whereof Immanuel should be born. . . . Looking down through the ages the prophet saw the accomplishment of the divine purposes as if already achieved, and sang in triumph: [quotes Isaiah 9:6–7].” 5
 

Nephi’s contemporary audience in the Book of Mormon setting of this chapter still lived more than five centuries before the fulfillment of Isaiah’s prophecies. Isaiah’s words of comfort and promise strengthened them as they lived in faith and experienced the light and joy of His divinity.
 

We today can also recognize this child from Galilee who brings wise counsel, divine power, fatherly security, and lasting peace. He will bring adversaries against the wicked and cleanse the earth in preparation for His second coming. And today He still stretches out His hand for all to come unto Him.
