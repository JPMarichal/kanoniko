# Israel’s Gathering in Joy

### Israel’s Gathering in Joy
Victor L. Ludlow, Unlocking Isaiah in the Book of Mormon (Salt Lake City 2003)
 
 

Israel’s Gathering in Joy
 

2 Nephi 8/Isaiah 51, 52:1–2; 2 Nephi 9:50–51/Isaiah 55:1–2
 

In the last days, the Lord shall comfort Zion and gather Israel— The redeemed shall come to Zion amid great joy— Come and drink: Salvation is free
 

Jacob promises great blessings of joy and salvation in 2 Nephi 8 and 9 as he cites three different Isaiah chapters. Continuing his discourse after quoting Isaiah 50 in 2 Nephi 7, Jacob quotes Isaiah 51 and the first two verses of chapter 52.1 There are four main sections in 2 Nephi 8: a reminder to Israel of her heritage and the Lord’s work (vv. 1–8); a promise that the righteous will enjoy the blessings of Zion (vv. 9–16); a warning and promise to the Jews in the last days (vv. 17–23); and a brief vision of Zion and Jerusalem restored to their full priesthood power (vv. 24–25). After quoting these Isaiah passages, Jacob builds upon them and delivers his profound sermon on the Atonement (2 Nephi 9). Toward the end of the sermon, Jacob quotes Isaiah 55:1–2 (2 Nephi 9:50–51), which becomes his invitation for all to come and partake of the blessings of the Redeemer’s salvation.
 

2 Nephi 8:1–3: “Look unto Abraham, your father, and unto Sarah”
 

So that God’s promises can be fulfilled, Isaiah admonishes the children of Israel to remember their noble ancestors Abraham and Sarah and particularly God’s covenant promises to Abraham. He uses imagery of stone quarries and calls upon the chosen people to “look unto the rock from whence ye are hewn” (v. 1), pointing to Abraham and his wife Sarah as examples of solid, faithful followers of the Lord (v. 2). Indeed, we know that Abraham’s faith was counted as righteousness (Romans 4:3; James 2:23).
 

The Lord promises His people spiritual and material blessings if they accept Him as Abraham did. As they follow Abraham’s example, Zion (Jerusalem) will be comforted and her ruins rebuilt.2 The city will become like Eden, the garden of the Lord. To emphasize the great promises to the covenant members of the gospel, Isaiah uses several contrasting ideas (or antithetic parallels) in verse 3. He compares the “waste places” with the comfort of the Lord, the desolate wildernesses with the glory of Eden, and the barren deserts with “the garden of the Lord.” Clearly, those who keep their covenants with God will be blessed. Joy, gladness, and thanksgiving will abide among those who obey, instead of the sorrow, frustration, and tribulation that Israel usually experiences. The source of these blessing is the rock in verse 1, which refers to the Savior Himself, the overseer of God’s works and blessings. He stands as the cornerstone of the Father’s plan of salvation, the “sure foundation, a foundation whereon if men build they cannot fall” (Helaman 5:12).
 

These ideal conditions will prevail over all the earth only after Christ comes and begins His millennial reign, but the scriptures do not give us the precise sequence of events leading to His coming. Studying the prophecies of the last days can be frustrating because events are listed in quick succession, giving the impression that they will occur within a short time period when they may actually take place over centuries. Thus, Israel’s gathering and redemption in the last days will undoubtedly take more time than the verses imply.
 

Early Book of Mormon prophecies about the gathering and restoration of Israel
 

Careful study of the writings of Nephi and Jacob provide helpful insights into some key developments and sequences in the last days. The first three books of the Book of Mormon contain five important prophecies about the gathering and restoration of Israel in the last days:
 

1. Before any Isaiah chapters are quoted, Nephi records some important teachings of his father. Lehi speaks of all Israel when he says, “After the house of Israel should be scattered they should be gathered together again” (1 Nephi 10:14).
 

2. Just before citing some of Isaiah’s prophecies, Nephi quotes another prophet from the brass plates. The prophet Zenos talks specifically about the Jewish community of the house of Israel when he prophesies, “When that day cometh . . . that they no more turn aside their hearts against the Holy One of Israel, then will he remember the covenants which he made to their fathers. . . . And all the people who are of the house of Israel, will I gather in, saith the Lord” (1 Nephi 19:15–16, emphasis added). Zenos is saying that when there is a change of attitude among the Jews toward the Holy One of Israel (Jesus Christ)—that is, they are no longer against Him—He will gather them and others of the house of Israel home.
 

3. Just before quoting Isaiah 50 and 51, Jacob records an angelic vision of the Jews that he had received. He foretells, “After they are driven to and fro, . . . they shall be scattered, and smitten, and hated; nevertheless, the Lord will be merciful unto them, that when they shall come to the knowledge of their Redeemer, they shall be gathered together again to the lands of their inheritance” (2 Nephi 6:11, emphasis added). Jacob reveals that when there is a change of knowledge among the Jews as they begin to know more about Jesus and His teachings—as has developed in the world Jewish community in the past 150 years—He will “gather” them to the lands of their inheritance, which would be in the modern land of Israel. Today, a third of the world’s Jews have gathered from more than one hundred nations to the State of Israel, fulfilling the Lord’s promise to them.
 

4. After quoting Isaiah and speaking about Christ’s atonement, Jacob receives further revelation during the night. The next day, he says more about the Jews: “Thus saith the Lord God: When the day cometh that they shall believe in me, that I am Christ, then have I covenanted with their fathers that they shall be restored in the flesh, upon the earth, unto the lands of their inheritance” (2 Nephi 10:7, emphasis added). The Lord promises that when there is a change of belief among the Jews—they accept Him, Jesus, as their Messiah—He will “restore” them in the lands of their inheritance. “Restoration” implies additional spiritual powers and blessings beyond the physical “gathering” into their promised land (also Nephi’s prophecies in 2 Nephi 25:16–19; in addition, note how Jacob paraphrases Isaiah 49:22–23 in 2 Nephi 10:8–9).
 

5. Some years after delivering these teachings and prophecies recorded in 2 Nephi, Jacob has more to say about the scattering and gathering of the house of Israel. He quotes Zenos’s Allegory of the Olive Tree in what becomes the longest chapter in the Book of Mormon (Jacob 5).
 

Thus we see that the theme of the scattering, gathering, and restoration of Israel was dominant in the teachings of Nephi and Jacob. From prophetic teachings recorded by Nephi in 1 and 2 Nephi to Jacob’s own writings in Jacob 5, these two brothers provide major insights and revelations about the return of Israel, especially concerning key preconditions and promises to be fulfilled in the last days.
 

2 Nephi 8:4–8: “Hearken unto me, ye that know righteousness”
 

In these verses, the Lord voices His intention to return to the earth to rule and judge His people. He calls to “my people” and “my nation” (v. 4) to stress the idea that He has not forsaken them. Isaiah’s description of Jehovah’s judgment as a “light” is similar to imagery found in 1 Nephi 21:6/Isaiah 49:6, where the Savior is described as a “light to the Gentiles.” Indeed, Christ is the “light [that] shineth in darkness” and “the light and the life of the world” (D&C 88:49; Mosiah 16:9). By studying references to light in the scripture, we learn how Christ was able to make His pronouncement of rest and peace “a light for the people” (v. 4).3
 

The image of Jehovah’s arm in verse 5 illustrates Christ’s dual role as both Savior and final Judge. Although the Lord’s arm “shall judge the people,” His arms are also a place of comfort and trust (Isaiah 40:11). In verse 6, the Lord speaks of His second coming and its associated events. The first two lines of the verse form a contrast; the instruction to look both up to the heavens and down to the earth is an antithetic parallel that reflects the all-inclusive nature of the Second Coming. Both the heavens and the earth will be permanently altered; their passing away can be interpreted as the Lord’s pronouncement of the certainty of His word, counsel, and judgment in contrast to the transitory nature of the world.4 The Lord also promises the righteous that their salvation is eternal and does not depend on the world.
 

Isaiah next uses emblematic parallels, or comparisons between two ideas, in verses 6–8 to highlight the earth’s telestial state. He compares the earth to a garment that will “wax old,” and wicked men to clothing that the “moth shall eat . . . up.” This imagery, however, can be interpreted in another way: the priesthood powers associated with the sacred temple garments of righteous followers are not transitory. The covenants and ordinances performed while wearing the garments do not die away with the return of the Savior—they last throughout eternity. Even though on earth we are surrounded by worldly objects and philosophies that will eventually be forgotten, we can participate in ordinances that are eternal and permanent, such as baptisms and sealings, for ourselves and for others.
 

2 Nephi 8:9–16: “I am he; yea, I am he that comforteth you”
 

If Israel seeks the Lord, He will use His strength to bring joy, holiness, and comfort as He assists her in fulfilling her destiny. Man cannot accomplish the establishment of Zion among Israel alone; the Lord must exert His powers to bring it about. In verses 9–11, Israel cries unto the Lord, petitioning Him to awake and fulfill the promises of the last days. Using a climactic structure, in which ideas build upon each other until they reach a climax, Israel (through Isaiah) asks several questions that recall the Lord’s great miracles. The questions are, “Art thou not he that hath cut Rahab? . . . Art thou not he who hath dried the sea? . . . that hath made the depths of the sea a way for the ransomed to pass over?” Isaiah uses Rahab, a sea monster, to represent Satan and remind the people of the Lord’s victory over the adversary.
 

In verse 11, Isaiah promises that as the righteous return to Israel, they will sing with rejoicing because of the fulfillment of prophecy. Regarding this passage, Elder LeGrand Richards testified that when Isaiah saw our day, “he saw the redeemed of the Lord come up and sing in the heights of Zion (Isaiah 51:11). And where do you find anything in the world to fulfill that like the singing of the Tabernacle Choir?”5 In a broader application, our hymns unite the worldwide community of the Church and give us a beautiful way to express the joy of the great gospel gathering.
 

Starting in verse 12, Isaiah speaks for the Lord instead of Israel. Jehovah manifests His power and offers assurance to those who have forgotten Him by asking three of His own questions in verses 12–13, then answering them in verses 13–16. He asks: Why do you fear mortal man? Have you forgotten your creator? Why fear your oppressors? He then answers the questions in reverse order: You will be freed from your oppressors. I am the great creator. You are my people and I will teach and protect you.
 

To bring additional comfort to His people, the Lord compares the transitory powers of men to grass (v. 12) and reminds them of His own eternal creations (v. 13). He then assures the Israelites that they have no reason to fear the “fury of the oppressor,” whose prisoners will be freed from the pit (spirit prison) and enjoy abundant bread (immortal life as resurrected beings; vv. 13–14). As a final promise of comfort, the words of the prophets, God’s creations, and even the Lord Himself testify to the inhabitants of Zion, “Behold, thou art my people” (v. 16).
 

2 Nephi 8:17–25: “These two sons are come unto thee”
 

In these verses, Isaiah speaks of the time when the Lord’s covenants are fulfilled and the children of Israel are brought back home through their righteousness. They are first commanded to “awake,” or hearken to the voice of God. These verses contrast former punishments with the ultimate redemption of the Lord’s chosen people. To describe Jehovah’s punishment, Isaiah uses two phrases with similar meanings in verse 17 as he tells Israel that they have “drunk at the hand of the Lord the cup of his fury” and “drunken the dregs of the cup of trembling.” According to Elder James E. Talmage, the “cup” (vv. 17, 22) often has the same meaning in both the Old and New Testaments: it is “a symbolic expression for a bitter or poisonous potion typifying experiences of suffering.”6 The cup of trembling refers not only to the physical punishment of the children of Israel but also to the Savior’s proxy suffering as He took upon Himself the sins of the world. While praying in the garden of Gethsemane, the Lord said, “O my Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me: nevertheless not as I will, but as thou wilt” (Matthew 26:39).
 

Israel’s suffering for her sins is then contrasted with the punishment her enemies will experience. The Lord promises that He will give the “cup of trembling” to Israel’s tormentors, such as the Assyrians or Romans, who have forced the Israelites to lie on the ground while they walked upon them (v. 23), representing captivity or conquest. The Savior promises Israel that He will deliver them both physically and spiritually at His coming.
 

Between the passages that describe the “cup of trembling,” Isaiah also explains why the children of Israel are suffering and scattered: they have “none to guide her” (v. 18), meaning that they have no prophets or inspired leaders. Since Jerusalem has no guide from among her own sons, she must look elsewhere for inspired leadership. Isaiah foretells that “two sons” will go to Jerusalem to give courage and power to the people. An emblematic parallel is used to describe these two powerful latter-day servants who will appear on the streets in Jerusalem. They will be like “wild bull[s] in a net,” fighting with awesome fierceness against the wicked. And though powerful enemies will oppose them, they will be “full of the fury of the Lord” (v. 20). Indeed, John the Revelator describes their role in the latter days: they will be powerful prophets of God, “hav[ing] power to shut heaven” and “devour their enemies.” They will be the major factor in keeping enemy armies from totally defeating the Jews (Revelation 11:3–6). Joseph Smith prophesies of these two witnesses, or prophets, in D&C 77:15, and Elder Bruce R. McConkie suggests that they will most likely be members of the Quorum of the Twelve Apostles or of the First Presidency.7 By studying the various scriptures foretelling these two future prophets in Jerusalem, we learn that they show the gifts and powers demonstrated by three ancient prophets: Enoch, Moses, and Elijah. Like Enoch, they use priesthood power to hold the enemies of Zion at bay; like Moses, they call forth plagues upon the earth; like Elijah, they seal the heavens so that no rain falls for an extended period. These servants will manifest God’s power to protect and rescue His people, especially as the calamities of the last days unfold.
 

Further justification for Zion’s rescue is found in the fulfillment of God’s invitation to the children of Israel to “awake” and put on their true “strength.” Jerusalem is told to put on beautiful garments and loose her bands of bondage as she receives priesthood powers and gathers home from her scattered condition (vv. 24–25; compare D&C 113:7–10). These last two verses are Jacob’s quotation of Isaiah 52:1–2, which the Savior later quotes in His teachings to the Nephites (for further discussion of these verses, see the commentary on 3 Nephi 20:36–37 in this book).
 

2 Nephi 9:50–51: “Come my brethren, every one that thirsteth”
 

Later in this sermon to the Nephites, Jacob quotes from Isaiah 55:1–2 as he concludes his great teachings about the Atonement. He uses Isaiah’s poetic words to exhort his people to partake freely of the gospel and its promise of eternal salvation.
 

Isaiah uses four similar phrases to encourage the people to “come” unto Christ:
 

• “Come . . . every one that thirsteth.”
 

• “Come ye to the waters.”
 

• “Come buy and eat.”
 

• “Come buy wine and milk without money and without price.”
 

The word thirsteth immediately reminds us of the human necessity for liquids. The metaphor uses three common liquids that quench one’s thirst: water, wine, and milk. Water is always required and is usually available to satisfy thirst. Isaiah is inviting us to come to the waters, or to stand in holy places, which is a necessary step before we can “buy” the precious gifts of the gospel. Water also represents the love of God (1 Nephi 11:25) and the covenant waters of baptism. Wine is less plentiful but is available after some effort, and is good for satisfying thirst, especially in times of rejoicing. Wine is also symbolic of the sacrament covenants. Milk is common and important for strength, growth, and nourishment. God’s covenant people are promised lands of milk and honey. Thus we see that Isaiah is inviting the people to satisfy their physical thirst, but he is also promising them joy and strength as they spiritually drink what the Lord has to offer as they honor their covenants with Him.
 

The milk and wine are to be acquired “without money and without price” (v. 50). Isaiah pleads with us to not spend our money and efforts on temporal things that do not ultimately satisfy (v. 51). Christ’s gifts of eternal salvation and glory are free to all, even though they are priceless gifts. His gospel has no monetary price attached to it; one does not have to buy one’s Church membership or the blessing of resurrection. However, these gifts cannot be obtained without effort. The price is a pure heart and a contrite spirit. Without the Atonement, we would never be able to “afford” these gifts.
 

In this section of 2 Nephi, Jacob quotes Isaiah’s teachings about Israel’s gathering and rejoicing. We learn that the gospel of Jesus Christ empowers the Saints with peace and joy despite wickedness around them. We are promised that special prophets in Jerusalem will manifest God’s powers as final events of the last days unfold. In addition, we are invited to partake of the nourishing truths of the gospel. Rich blessings are in store for those who believe in and then live worthy to receive these words of comfort from the Lord.
