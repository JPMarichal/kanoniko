# Nations and Armies as Tools in God’s Hand

### Nations and Armies as Tools in God’s Hand
Victor L. Ludlow, Unlocking Isaiah in the Book of Mormon (Salt Lake City 2003)
 
 

Nations and Armies as Tools in God’s Hand
 

2 Nephi 20/Isaiah 10
 

Destruction of Assyria is a type of destruction of wicked at the Second Coming—Few people shall be left after the Lord comes again— Remnant of Jacob shall return in that day
 

In this chapter, Isaiah warns the Israelites of dire consequences for their neglect of the poor and needy. As a result of their wickedness, the Lord will use the Assyrian army as a tool to remind Israel of her broken covenants with God. Isaiah also describes the pride of the Assyrian king, which will be the cause of his empire’s eventual downfall. In the midst of prophesying judgment and destruction, Isaiah reveals that a remnant of the house of Israel will return. As is typical of Isaiah’s writings, the events described in this chapter have both ancient historical and future prophetic fulfillment.
 

2 Nephi 20:1–4: “Wo unto them that . . . turn away the needy”
 

2 Nephi 20 is a continuation of chapter 19, where Isaiah gives three warnings to Israel about pride, wicked leaders, and turning away from God. A fourth warning is given in the first four verses of chapter 20: “Wo unto them that decree unrighteous decrees, and that write grievousness” (v. 1). The “unrighteous decrees” and grievous writings symbolize the social attitudes and legal actions of the Israelites, who repeatedly turned away “the needy from judgment” and took away “the right from the poor . . . , that widows may be their prey, and that they may rob the fatherless!” (v. 2). This disregard for the less fortunate goes directly against counsel that the Lord had given centuries earlier to the tribes of Israel:
 

If there be among you a poor man . . . within any of thy gates in thy land which the Lord thy God giveth thee, thou shalt not harden thine heart, nor shut thine hand from thy poor brother. . . . 
 

For the poor shall never cease out of the land: therefore I command thee, saying, Thou shalt open thine hand wide unto thy brother, to thy poor, and to thy needy, in thy land (Deuteronomy 15:7, 11).
 

The Israelites unashamedly hardened their hearts against the poor and helpless, blatantly disobeying the Lord’s command.
 

The Lord gave similar commandments to the Nephites and to the Latter-day Saints. In his great final discourse, King Benjamin pled with his people, “Impart of your substance to the poor, every man according to that which he hath, such as feeding the hungry, clothing the naked, visiting the sick and administering to their relief” (Mosiah 4:26). At Winter Quarters, President Brigham Young received a revelation about caring for the needy during the trek to the Salt Lake Valley: “Let each company bear an equal proportion . . . in taking the poor, the widows, the fatherless, . . . that the cries of the widow and the fatherless come not up into the ears of the Lord against this people” (D&C 136:8). It is the responsibility of God’s children in every dispensation to exercise the Christlike attribute of giving so that the cries of the needy are fulfilled.
 

In verse 3, Isaiah questions those who have neglected their duty, asking what they will do when the “day of visitation” and “desolation” comes. Roles have switched, and now, ironically, the Lord will refuse to help those who refused to offer assistance to others; they will be judged in the same way they chose to judge (Matthew 7:1–2). Isaiah further asks, “To whom will ye flee for help? and where will ye leave your glory?” (v. 3). Without repentance, they will not be able to “flee” to the Lord, because He cannot support them in their wickedness.
 

Because of their abuse of the needy, the Lord removes His protective care from the children of Israel, and thus they will “bow down under the prisoners, and they shall fall under the slain” (v. 4). This prophecy was fulfilled when Israel was invaded and many were killed or taken captive by the Assyrian armies. This physical fulfillment is coupled with a spiritual fulfillment that continues to this day: all who sin and refuse to obey the commands of God are bowed down under the burdens of sin, and if they choose not to repent, they will become prisoners of the adversary.
 

As discussed in the last chapter, the summary statement that the Lord’s “anger is not turned away, but his hand is stretched out still” (v. 4) is one Isaiah uses frequently (2 Nephi 15:25). This twofold statement represents both a promise and a warning: the Lord’s anger is not “turned away” from the wicked, but “his hand is stretched out still” to all who choose to repent and forsake their evil ways.
 

2 Nephi 20:5–11: “O Assyrian, the rod of mine anger”
 

The next 15 verses of this chapter describe how the Lord uses Assyria as a tool to bring Israel to remembrance of their obligations. To better understand how these verses would have influenced both Isaiah’s audience and the Nephites, one should have a basic knowledge of the history of the Assyrian people.
 

Adad-Nirari II (909–899 b.c.) began the expansion of the Assyrian empire by invading neighboring lands. From 909–857 b.c., the Assyrian kings continued to enlarge the boundaries of their empire until they reached from the regions near the Black Sea to the Persian Gulf, and from Media (modern Iran) to Syria.1 For a period of about 100 years, expansion came to a halt, and the kings struggled to keep control of their lands. In 745 b.c., Tiglath-pileser III began to strengthen the struggling empire. It was during this time that Assyria expanded toward her greatest dominion. Syria and Israel united in an anti-Assyrian revolt to stop her expansion toward Egypt, and they tried to gain support from Judah. King Ahaz refused, and the Syrian-Israel coalition attacked Judah. Against Isaiah’s advice, Ahaz sent a “contribution” to Tiglath-pileser and requested aid against this Syria-Israeli threat. Tiglath responded, forcing Syria and Israel to withdraw from Judah and defend their lands—something they were unable to do against the Assyrian might. By 732 b.c., much of Israel and Syria were conquered, and many leaders and their families were deported or killed. With Tiglath-pileser’s death in 722 b.c. and further rebellion by Israelite leaders, Sargon II besieged the capital city of Samaria and destroyed the last vestiges of the Israelite nation. By 721 b.c., Samaria was destroyed, and remnants of the ten tribes were relocated throughout the Assyrian empire as Israel’s exile among the nations began.
 

In verses 5–7, the Lord declares that He has allowed the Assyrian army to gain strength against the Israelites. Essentially, the Lord is using the army as a tool—“the rod of mine anger”—to bring His people, who had become a “hypocritical nation” (v. 6), to a remembrance of their broken covenants. The Lord, seeking to humble them through captivity, allowed the Assyrian armies to “take the spoil, and to take the prey, and to tread them like the mire of the streets.” The Lord “hold[s] the destinies of all the armies of the nations of the earth” (D&C 117:6), and He uses them as needed to accomplish His eternal plans.
 

Although it is the Lord who makes Assyria an indomitable force, the Assyrian king (Sargon II at the time) boasts that his great leadership and his army’s power have allowed great Assyrian expansion throughout the land. Because of this imperialist expansion, many nations had come under Assyria’s political control, and the king asserts that his “princes [are] altogether kings” (v. 8) over the huge territories. In verses 9–11, the king boasts about all the cities his forces have conquered so far. Each city had idols of gods that were supposed to protect them, but all were overcome and rendered powerless. The king believes that since Israelite Samaria and her idols had recently fallen, the Judean city of Jerusalem and the God of the Jews would fall as well.
 

2 Nephi 20:12–19: “The Lord of Hosts . . . shall kindle a burning”
 

Sargon’s boasting continues in the next few verses as he takes full credit for Assyria’s recent victories: “By the strength of my hand and by my wisdom I have done these things” (v. 13). He goes further by comparing his conquests to a man effortlessly gathering eggs from a bird’s nest: “My hand hath found as a nest the riches of the people; and as one gathereth eggs that are left have I gathered all the earth” (v. 14).
 

Isaiah re-enters the text as prophetic counselor in verse 15, revealing the illogicality and futility of the king’s thoughts. He compares the king’s proud attitude to an ax or saw claiming the ability to cut down a tree without anyone wielding it. Just as an ax is useless without a lumberjack behind it, Assyria has no power without the Lord’s permission. Isaiah also uses the terms “rod” and “staff” in his analogy, referring back to verse 5 where the Lord calls Assyria the “rod of [his] anger.”
 

Because of his wickedness, the king will be destroyed, but only after the Lord “hath performed his whole work upon Mount Zion and upon Jerusalem” (v. 12). Although we cannot fully comprehend the complexity and vastness of the Lord’s works, we can deduce that part of the Lord’s purpose for using Assyria was to humble the Israelites and to initiate the scattering of Israel toward all corners of the earth.
 

Using symbolic language, Isaiah further describes Assyria’s eventual downfall in verses 16–17: “Therefore shall the Lord . . . send among his fat ones, leanness; and under his glory he shall kindle a burning like the burning of a fire” (v. 16). The fire that will come against Assyria is revealed as “the light of Israel,” or the “Holy One” (v. 17). These terms refer to the Lord’s might and reveal that He will be the one to bring judgment against the proud nation. In fact, the Lord’s protection is so powerful that Isaiah says He “shall burn and shall devour his thorns and his briers in one day” (v. 17). While the Assyrian kings had taken centuries to gain their power, the Lord will effortlessly sweep them away like a fire consuming noxious thorns and thistles.
 

The symbolism of devastation continues in verses 18–19 as Isaiah likens the Lord’s destruction of the wicked to the leveling of a forest. The Assyrian army will be so completely demolished that a child will be able to count the number of soldiers left (v. 19). This prophecy was fulfilled several decades later when the Babylonians and Persians destroyed the Assyrian empire and the Assyrians ceased to be a nation. Indeed, “pride goeth before destruction, and an haughty spirit before a fall” (Proverbs 16:18).
 

Isaiah’s warning to wicked Israel in the 700s b.c. also applied to the Nephites in the 500s b.c. The Lamanite threat was very real to the Nephites of this time, and Nephi taught the lessons learned from this earlier historical period. He used these historical events (which had occurred only about 150 years previously) to remind his people about the price their Ephraimite and Manassehite ancestors paid for their wickedness. Just as the Lord used an enemy force, the Assyrians, to punish Israel and humble Judah, so He revealed to Nephi that the Nephite enemies, the Lamanites, “shall be a scourge unto thy seed, to stir them up in remembrance of me; and inasmuch as they will not remember me, and hearken unto my words, they shall scourge them even unto destruction” (2 Nephi 5:25). The Lord uses many tools, such as weather, disease, and war, to encourage His people to stay humble and obedient. People today can learn from this lesson of the past—if we neglect the poor and needy, we lose God’s protection and become vulnerable to foreign dominion and other punishments.
 

Although these verses refer to historical events, they are also symbolic of future events. In the latter days, the Lord continues to use different “tools” to bring His covenant people to humility and repentance as well as to bring judgments upon the wicked. Some of these tools may even be the proud and the wicked of the world. However, just as the Lord destroyed Assyria, so will He ultimately destroy the arrogant and wicked of the earth when He comes again, sweeping away the thorns and thistles of mankind and lifting the earth to its paradisiacal glory.
 

2 Nephi 20:20–27: “The remnant of Israel . . . shall return . . . unto the mighty God”
 

Switching from a voice of ominous warning, Isaiah now delivers a message of joyous tidings: after the destruction of the Assyrians, a remnant of Israel shall return. The doctrine of remnants of the house of Israel returning to their ancient lands of inheritance is common in Isaiah’s writings and is a thread of continual hope through Isaiah’s darkest prophecies.
 

Isaiah promises that those who are blessed to return will “no more . . . stay upon him that smote them, but shall stay upon the Lord” (v. 20). Instead of following the commands of wicked and brutal captors, the remnant will be able to return to Israel and live under the rule of their God. The return is not merely a physical escape from the arms of bondage but is also a spiritual release from the natural man’s sinful ways back to the righteous ways of the “mighty God” (v. 21).
 

When this remnant of Israel returns, their people will have multiplied greatly—described emblematically as being like “the sand of the sea” (v. 22). This simile alludes to the promise given to Abraham that his posterity would be “as the sand which is upon the sea shore” (Genesis 22:17). Isaiah’s prophecy not only refers to Old Testament times, when a remnant of the scattered Jews would return to their lands in Jerusalem, but also to the latter-day gathering of the ten tribes prior to the Savior’s return. In that day, righteous covenant followers of the Lord will assemble to usher in a millennial period of peace and prosperity.
 

These verses are the pivot point of a beautiful chiastic poem contained in verses 13–27. Beginning in verse 13, Isaiah portrays the king of Assyria boasting of his might, and in verses 25–27 the prophet testifies of the Lord’s great power. The following is an outline of the poem’s key ideas:
 

A Boastings of the king of Assyria’s “might” (vv. 13–14).
 

B A mortal king’s rod and staff (v. 15).
 

C Cleansing the wicked from the land (vv. 16–18).
 

D Only a few wicked remain (v. 19).
 

E A remnant of Israel will rely upon the Lord (v. 20).
 

E’ A remnant will return to their land (v. 21).
 

D’ Israel will become numerous as the sands of the sea (v. 22a).
 

C’ A cleansing of the earth (vv. 22b–23).
 

B’ A divine King’s rod and staff (v. 24).
 

A’ Demonstrations of the King of heaven’s might (vv. 25–27).
 

The poem’s two major messages are a witness and a promise. First, Isaiah testifies of God’s eternal power compared to man’s transitory might. Second, he records the Lord’s promise that He will bring back a numerous remnant of righteous Israel.
 

This joyous time of Israel’s return will also be a time of “consumption,” or depletion, for the wicked. Isaiah states, in verse 22, that “the consumption decreed shall overflow with righteousness.” Contrasted with the beginning of the chapter, which speaks of the obliteration of the Israelite nations, this consumption refers to the destruction of the wicked nations. This devastation could be referring to either Assyria’s historical destruction or the future removal of the political nations of the world when Christ returns to reign over His kingdom on earth.
 

In verse 24, Isaiah assures the children of Israel that they need not fear the Assyrians when they come under her dominion. He compares their upcoming captivity to the times of Egyptian bondage, centuries earlier. This not only reminds Israel of the upcoming trials and tribulations they will face but also of the miraculous deliverance that ultimately would occur. In verse 26, Isaiah refers to another miraculous delivery of the Israelites—the “slaughter of Midian at the rock of Oreb.” During this battle, Gideon, a righteous leader, defeated a great army of Midianites and allied warriors with a small but faithful band of 300 men (Judges 7:25).
 

Although times of prolonged captivity were ahead for all of Israel, including the Nephites and Lamanites, Isaiah assures that they will eventually be freed through the Lord’s power: “It shall come to pass in that day [the last days] that his burden shall be taken away from off thy shoulder, and his yoke from off thy neck, and the yoke shall be destroyed because of the anointing” (v. 27). This reference to anointing represents the “Anointed One,” or Christ. As seen earlier with the Isaiah chapters quoted by Nephi and Jacob in 1 and 2 Nephi, the Jews would miraculously be gathered and then restored after their attitudes, knowledge, and belief toward the Messiah changed. Jehovah’s power will also rescue the other remnants of Israel from political bondage and oppression as their attitudes, knowledge, and beliefs change. Indeed, Christ’s atoning power delivers all mankind from spiritual bondage and the yoke of sin. By following the Savior, we truly find rest, for His “yoke is easy, and [His] burden is light” (Matthew 11:30).
 

2 Nephi 20:28–34: “He shall shake his hand against . . . the hill of Jerusalem”
 

Verses 28–31 describe, with great accuracy, the advance of the Assyrian army as they make their way through the lands of Israel and Judah. In fact, the armies will come so close to Jerusalem that they will “shake [their] hand against the . . . hill of Jerusalem” (v. 32). Their advance was thwarted, however, by the power of “a mighty one” (v. 34), even the “Lord of Hosts” (v. 33). This rescue of the Jews did occur in 701 b.c., when the Assyrians came against the city of Jerusalem. Before the armies were able to attack the walls of the city, however, the Lord sent a debilitating plague throughout the army camps, essentially wiping out the Assyrian forces (Isaiah 37:36).
 

As alluded to in the chapter heading, the armies of the wicked will make a similar advance against the holy city of Jerusalem just before the Millennium. Then, too, they will be destroyed before they complete their conquest. This time it will not be sickness that stops them but the Lord Himself. He will appear on the Mount of Olives and will utterly destroy the two armies of armies (in Greek, the “two myriads of myriads” of soldiers) that have gathered against Jerusalem (Revelation 11:1–13; Zechariah 14:2–5; Joseph Smith— Matthew).
 

Verses 33–34 provide an apt summary of the whole chapter. Isaiah once again describes the destruction that will come upon the Assyrians (and the wicked people of the last days). Those who are lifted up in pride will be “hewn down” and “humbled” like trees in a forest (v. 33). The wicked leaders will be cast away, and the way will be prepared for a righteous remnant to return and assume leadership. Then, as prophesied in the next chapter, the Lord will raise up righteous servants to lead the world into the glory of His millennial reign.
 

The struggles between pride and humility and between submission and power remain the same: when the Lord uses Assyria as a military rod and a political staff against unfaithful Israel, the king of Assyria thinks himself more mighty than the Master. Then, the Master brings forth His own priesthood rod and gospel staff to destroy the wicked powers on earth as He restores His faithful rulers in Zion. As the nations of the earth rise to power and oppress and scatter the children of Israel, their arrogant rulers think themselves to be all-powerful. Then, with God’s help, the devoted remnants of Israel return and are restored to lasting power in their lands of ancient inheritance. Ultimately, only Christ and His humble, righteous followers rule the earth.
