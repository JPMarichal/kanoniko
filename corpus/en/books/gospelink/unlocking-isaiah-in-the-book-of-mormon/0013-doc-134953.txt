# The Immanuel Prophecy

### The Immanuel Prophecy
Victor L. Ludlow, Unlocking Isaiah in the Book of Mormon (Salt Lake City 2003)
 
 

The Immanuel Prophecy
 

2 Nephi 17/Isaiah 7
 

Ephraim and Syria wage war against Judah— Christ shall be born of a virgin
 

This historically significant and prophetically profound chapter describes the political power struggles between Judah and her neighbors. It also foretells the birth of a messianic child. These passages have deep layers of meaning not only for Isaiah’s time but also for Christ’s ministry and the latter days. This chapter is a third-person, historical, prophetic account, which sets it apart from Isaiah’s personal memoirs in the previous chapter (2 Nephi 16/Isaiah 6). Most importantly, this chapter contains prophetic insight into the Syrian-Israel war against Judah and significant prophecy about the coming Immanuel.
 

In order to understand the political conflict in that day (about 734 b.c.), one needs to review a little history of the house of Israel. Jacob, later known by the name of Israel, had twelve sons. Eventually, he and all his sons and their families resided in Egypt, where their descendants were later enslaved. At the time of Moses’ deliverance in the thirteenth century b.c., they were organized into thirteen distinct tribes because Joseph’s posterity was so large that they comprised two tribes—Manasseh and Ephraim. Under Josua’s leadership, twelve tribes received specified land territories while the tribe of Levi was scattered among the other tribes to provide priestly direction and assistance with the multiple obligations of the Mosaic laws. Some two centuries before Isaiah’s ministry, the tribes of Israel had divided into two separate kingdoms: Israel in the north (with about ten tribes) and Judah in the south (with about three tribes; the whole tribes of Judah and Simeon along with fractions of the tribes of Levi, Benjamin, and Dan). Shortly after Isaiah was called as a prophet in 740 b.c., Israel formed an alliance with Syria to unite their forces against the threat of an Assyrian invasion. They wanted all countries in the region to join their alliance, but Judah refused to do so. Israel and Syria both attacked Judah to force her compliance. This is the basic historical setting for the chapter. To appreciate the events in this chapter, we should become familiar with several names and titles and understand how they relate to the different kingdoms. There are three main countries involved, each known by a variety of place, tribal, and personal names:

Isaiah uses these names interchangeably, such as referring to the kingdom of Judah as Jerusalem or to the nation of Israel as Ephraim. Consulting this chart clarifies the historical passages in this chapter. Isaiah’s writings are much more enlightening when the reader has a general comprehension of their historical context. Also, referring to a Bible map helps one become familiar with how the relevant countries connect with each other. By creating a mental picture of Isaiah’s time and place, one obtains a fuller understanding of Isaiah’s contemporary message. The reader can then more easily appreciate how his words might be pertinent today.
 

2 Nephi 17:1–4: “Take heed, and be quiet; fear not”
 

Isaiah begins by introducing the key political figures in the chapter: Kings Ahaz, Rezin, and Pekah. It is interesting that Isaiah introduces two of them as “the son of _____,” devaluing their political positions to mere inheritances of their fathers. These men did not earn the right to be king; rather, it was given to them. Furthermore, Isaiah never mentions King Pekah’s name again, referring to him only as “the son of Remaliah.” Clearly, Isaiah did not respect these politically powerful leaders.
 

In verse 2, Isaiah compares the fear of King Ahaz and his people of Judah to “the trees of the wood . . . moved with the wind.” At this point, Ahaz knows that Syria and Israel want to take over his country and that his armies are already losing territory. However, Isaiah may be suggesting that the people of Judah are rather cowardly and easily terrified. In other words, their fears might be influenced by mere threats and rumors of danger rather than by actual peril. We too are sometimes overly anxious about our future. But we know that if we are prepared, we have nothing to fear. As individuals and as a covenant people, we should obey President Gordon B. Hinckley’s admonition to “stand a little taller.” He also counsels the Saints, “These are difficult times. The economy is struggling. There is conflict in the world. But the Almighty is keeping His promise that He will bless those who walk in faith and righteousness before Him.” He also promises that to the extent that we incorporate the gospel of Jesus Christ in our lives, we will be blessed and prosperous.1 Living righteously gives us hope instead of fear for the future.
 

In the next verse, the Lord sends Isaiah and his son to deliver a message to the king. The Lord wants Ahaz to be encouraged and to refuse an alliance with Syria and Israel. The presence of Isaiah’s son is significant because his name, Shear-jashub, means “the remnant shall return.” This phrase describes the literal and spiritual gathering of Israel that is to occur in the latter days. Thus, Shear-jashub’s presence signifies that although disaster may come to the house of Israel as their tribes are scattered, a reunion will eventually occur, and a portion of the house of Israel will return.
 

To emphasize and develop the Lord’s specific message for Ahaz, Isaiah repeats the same idea in different words in verse 4: “Take heed, and be quiet; fear not, neither be faint-hearted.” The Lord wants to strengthen Ahaz in the face of military adversity. This verse is similar to other scriptures that remind us to rely on the Lord’s protective powers. For example, before Joshua’s people went to war with the Amorites, he counseled them, “Fear not, nor be dismayed, be strong and of good courage: for thus shall the Lord do to all your enemies against whom ye fight” (Joshua 10:25). Similarly, David instructed his son, “Be strong and of good courage, and do it: fear not, nor be dismayed: for the Lord God, even my God, will be with thee; he will not fail thee, nor forsake thee, until thou hast finished all the work for the service of the house of the Lord” (1 Chronicles 28:20). Similar counsel is relevant in modern times of uncertainty. In his recent first presidency message “Be Not Afraid,” President James E. Faust admonished us to “be of good cheer, put our faith in God, and live worthy for Him to direct us.”2 We should be grateful that the Lord still sends messages of comfort and encouragement through His chosen prophets and leaders.
 

In the same verse, Isaiah uses symbolic imagery to illustrate the relative insignificance of the threats of Israel and Syria. He refers to them as “smoking firebrands”—little more than excessively smoky torches that threaten with fire but are really harmless. The Lord is trying to convince Ahaz to rely on His counsel and not his own understanding or the apparent power of man.3 In this and every case, the threats of man are inferior to God’s powers.
 

2 Nephi 17:5–9: “If ye will not believe surely ye shall not be established”
 

The goal of the Syrian-Israel alliance is revealed in verses 5 and 6: the two countries want to invade Judah and replace her king with someone who is willing to repel Assyria with them. Although this threat of conquest is frightening, Isaiah prophesies that it will not happen. The alliance “shall not stand, neither shall it come to pass,” and the Syrians and Israelites will suffer tragic destruction and scattering. We are fortunate because we can review ancient historical records that describe exactly how this prophecy was fulfilled: Assyria devastated the lands of Syria and Israel, destroyed both kingdoms, and scattered many Israelites.
 

When Nephi recorded this Isaiah passage on his metal plates, the fulfillment of the prophecies in these verses had occurred just a century and a half earlier. The political conflicts and figures were familiar to Nephi’s family, and the fulfillment of Isaiah’s words must have strengthened their testimonies. Perhaps Nephi hoped this chapter would teach his people to have more faith in the face of human adversity. Perhaps he wanted to emphasize that only an alliance with God will provide complete protection against one’s enemies. In addition, Nephi probably knew that latter-day readers would need to hear this message. The Lord truly reveals future events to His prophets, and if we listen to their words, we need not fear the turmoil of our times.
 

In verses 8 and 9, Isaiah builds toward his main idea: “If ye will not believe surely ye shall not be established.” Another way of saying this is, “If you do not stand firm in your faith, you will not stand at all.” Essentially, Isaiah is saying that since Syria (Damascus) is “headed” by king Rezin, and Ephraim (Israel) is “headed” by Remaliah’s son (Pekah), their political power will fail. The corrupt administration of these two kings will lead to Syria’s and Israel’s prophesied downfall. Isaiah warns Ahaz that Judah must remain firm in her faith in the Lord or else she too will fall. Unfortunately, Ahaz refused to heed this counsel and relied on man’s power instead, forming an alliance with the dreaded Assyrians. The Lord cannot help those who refuse His counsel, so He could not deliver Judah from a later attack by Assyria (Isaiah 36–37). We learn more about this principle from Jehoshaphat’s counsel to Judah, which resembles Isaiah’s counsel to Ahaz: “Believe in the Lord your God, so shall ye be established; believe his prophets, so shall ye prosper” (2 Chronicles 20:20). The scriptural records of God’s works among His children teach us that “there is no wisdom nor understanding nor counsel against the Lord” (Proverbs 21:30). When we do not follow the Lord’s counsel, He cannot lead us to victory.
 

2 Nephi 17:10–16: “Behold, a virgin shall conceive”
 

In verse 11, Isaiah, in the name of the Lord, gives Ahaz the opportunity to ask for a sign, a divine reassurance that the Lord will protect Judah. To describe the way Ahaz should ask, Isaiah uses contrasting words: “either in the depths, or in the heights above” (italics added). This commandment alludes to a scripture in Proverbs, in which a responsibility of Israelite kings is outlined: “It is the glory of God to conceal a thing: but the honour of kings is to search out a matter. The heaven for height, and the earth for depth, and the heart of kings is unsearchable” (Proverbs 25:2–3; italics added). Essentially, the Lord’s counsel to rulers is to pray and ponder consistently. Ahaz’s refusal to do so under the pretense of not tempting the Lord shows that he has no confidence in the Lord’s saving power (v. 12).
 

Verse 13 gives us the feeling that both Isaiah and the Lord are frustrated with the king’s lack of faith. They ignore Ahaz’s reluctance to ask for a sign and declare one anyway (v. 14). The prophecy is dualistic, which is typical of Isaiah. The Immanuel prophecy foretells Judah’s imminent freedom from the Syrian-Israel threat, but it also describes important elements about the Savior’s birth, which leads to a far greater deliverance. In this latter context, the passage is already familiar to most Latter-day Saints because we often quote it in referring to the Savior.
 

Isaiah declares that a virgin will conceive and bear a son who will be called Immanuel. It is important to note that this refers to two births: a particular birth that would occur during Isaiah’s time as well as the well-recognized birth of the Savior several hundred years later. This earlier, less-known birth is an important one. Isaiah essentially affirms that after a child meeting the stated characteristics was born, Ahaz and his people would see the destruction of Syria and Israel before the lad was old enough to know right from wrong. Thus, within eight to ten years, the king could know that the Lord would preserve Judah. Isaiah does not provide further historical details about the boy and his mother, but it appears to have been common knowledge among the Jews that Isaiah’s prophecy about the woman and her son was literally fulfilled, as Judah was indeed spared.
 

Isaiah prophesies that the child would be named Immanuel, meaning, “God is with us.” This name given to the child of Isaiah’s time reveals the means by which Judah would be preserved. God was truly “with” Judah when the powerful, wicked Syrians, Israelites, and Assyrians were defeated. It is also a clear allusion to the Savior’s earthly ministry. He was the God of creation, worshiped by the Old Testament patriarchs and prophets as the Lord, Yahweh (Jehovah), so when He would come to earth, He would literally be God dwelling “with” His people.
 

Isaiah’s Immanuel prophecy has been important in later dispensations. Before influencing anyone else, however, the prophecy had a strong effect on Isaiah himself. Elder James E. Talmage teaches that Isaiah’s prophecies are special because his calling as a prophet was “honored by the personal testimony of Christ and the apostles.” Nevertheless, his unique knowledge of the future regarding the Savior’s birth and earthly ministry was probably a weighty responsibility, or a “burden,” to him because he could see that many people would not accept their Redeemer. Elder Talmage also points out that in Matthew 1:21–23, the angel of the Lord repeated Isaiah’s prediction as he instructed Joseph.4 Isaiah’s words undoubtedly strengthened Joseph during the difficult times of supporting Mary and his family, especially during their sojourn in Egypt.
 

Latter-day prophets refer to this Immanuel prophecy because it contains important truths about our Savior and His divine yet human characteristics. During His ministry, Jesus was both mortal and immortal. Many scholars do not believe that is possible, and they think the young woman could not be a special virgin who would conceive a Son of God. Modern revelation provides helpful explanations. Elder Marion G. Romney explains that some modern translators of the Bible are mistaken when they say this verse should be translated “a young woman shall conceive” rather than “a virgin shall conceive” (italics added). They follow this translation because they “do not believe that Christ was divine,” or, in other words, they don’t believe that our Heavenly Father could literally be the Father of the mortal Jesus. They think Jesus must have had a mortal father. Refuting these modern theories, President Romney teaches that “when Isaiah used the word virgin, he was saying that a woman who had not known a man should bear a son.”5
 

Elder Bruce R. McConkie further explains how Christ was both mortal and immortal:
 

Our Lord is the only mortal person ever born to a virgin, because he is the only person who ever had an immortal Father. Mary, his mother, “was carried away in the Spirit” (1 Nephi 11:13–21), was “overshadowed” by the Holy Ghost, and the conception that took place “by the power of the Holy Ghost” resulted in the bringing forth of the literal and personal Son of God the Father (Alma 7:10; 2 Nephi 17:14; Isaiah 7:14; Matthew 1:18–25; Luke 1:26–38). Christ is not the Son of the Holy Ghost, but of the Father (Doctrines of Salvation vol. 1, pp. 18–20). Modernistic teachings denying the virgin birth are utterly and completely apostate and false.6
 

Mary was indeed a virgin who gave birth to Jesus as prophesied by Isaiah.
 

Micah also alludes to the birth of our Lord when he prophesies of a woman “who travaileth” in conjunction with the coming forth of the Messiah from Bethlehem (Micah 5:3).7 Joseph Smith also talks about a great “King Immanuel” who redeems the dead from spirit prison (D&C 128:22). From such passages, we learn that prophets both before and after Christ have referred to Isaiah’s prophecy of the virgin birth in their teachings.
 

Finally, President Gordon B. Hinckley instructs us to believe in the same Christ as the ancient apostles. He testifies that the prophets of the Old Testament, including Isaiah, spoke for Jehovah, whether they were rebuking or prophesying:
 

Believe in him who was the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, who was the source of inspiration of all the ancient prophets—as they spoke as they were moved upon by the Holy Ghost. They spoke for him when . . . as seers they looked forward to the coming of a promised Messiah, declaring by the power of revelation, “Therefore the Lord himself shall give you a sign; Behold, a virgin shall conceive, and bear a son, and shall call his name Immanuel” (Isaiah 7:14). . . . Doubt not, but believe that it was he who was born to earth in a manger when there was no room in the inn.8
 

Prophets continue to speak for Jesus Christ today. The ancient Israelites, including those in America, who believed Isaiah’s prophecies of the Savior’s birth to a virgin knew He was coming, and they understood some of the events surrounding His birth. We, too, can believe Isaiah’s words and know that He is coming again as we look forward to the prophesied events surrounding His Second Coming (Isaiah 63–66).
 

In verses 15 and 16, Isaiah describes the impoverished circumstances of the prophesied child. He tells us that the child will eat “butter and honey”—foods usually consumed by the poor. The child of Isaiah’s time would indeed consume these foods while Judah experienced war and devastation. Likewise, the Savior would be required to eat the fare of a plain people, as He was raised in very humble circumstances in Egypt and Galilee. This mighty God, who dwelt with us, chose to live as a common man.
 

2 Nephi 17:17–25: “The Lord shall bring upon thee . . . the king of Assyria”
 

Isaiah prophesies further of destruction in the form of the king of Assyria that will come to Judah. The Lord is described as “hiss[ing]” or whistling, for the “fly” and the “bee” that will swarm from Egypt and Assyria (v. 18). The stinging bees represent the massive armies of Assyria that would eventually destroy Israel and Syria and then later attack Judah. The pesky flies represent Egyptian armies that would also come into Judah as they counterattacked the Assyrians. The insects (or armies) would be so numerous that they would completely envelop the landscape, covering the thorns and bushes and even the holes of the rocks (v. 19).
 

In verse 20, Isaiah alludes to the captivity of many Jews as he describes how the Lord would shave Judah with a hired razor (Assyria). The Lord sometimes uses foreign nations to accomplish His work, and here He humbles Judah for her lack of faith because she formed an alliance with Assyria instead of relying upon His power. However, this alliance backfired as Assyria later turned against Judah and enslaved many. The Assyrians regularly shaved the heads of their captives to keep them clean and to make them easily recognizable in case they escaped. In addition, since the hair was a valued feature in Israelite culture, being shaved was an especially humiliating punishment for these Judean captives.9
 

Further desolation is described in verses 21–25. Isaiah depicts seemingly great abundance and plentiful times, but he is actually writing about a future time of great emptiness. Verse 21 describes a surplus of cows and sheep, but this excess livestock is not a result of prosperity. Each man owns cows and sheep because there are so few men left after the Assyrian invasion. Some livestock is lost during the invasion, but a much greater proportion of the men die, so the remaining livestock is more than enough to sustain the survivors.10 Isaiah explains that there will be great amounts of butter and honey because of the large number of milk-producing animals and nectar-producing blossoms compared to the number of people. Also, since these are foods eaten by commoners, this prophecy also reveals that the noble families would be removed from their estates. Their abandoned vineyards and farms would be overrun by blossoming weeds, especially briers and thorns, and once-fertile lands would become forests where livestock would graze and men would hunt for wild game. The repeated promise of “briers and thorns” also alludes to God’s affliction sent upon the land after Adam and Eve were expelled from the Garden of Eden. In a similar pattern, Israelites from the ten tribes and Judah were being cast from their promised lands of inheritance, which, in turn, would be covered with briers and thorns.
 

The prophecies and promises in this chapter strengthen our faith that Immanuel’s coming is near. More important than when and where He comes is the promise of His love and power that can bless throughout eternity as we prove faithful to receive His protective care. Unlike King Ahaz, let us trust in God’s prophets and recognize His signs and gifts unto us. We should recognize that “God is with us” in many times and places. As prophets teach about the Savior’s earthly mission and promise His divine protection, their message is that “God is with us.” When He comes to dwell in the midst of us during His millennial reign and when we return as resurrected beings to His celestial realms, “God is with us.” As we sense His radiant light and love, we can always know that “God is with us!”
