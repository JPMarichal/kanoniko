# Saints and Sinners Prepare to Meet Christ

### Saints and Sinners Prepare to Meet Christ
Victor L. Ludlow, Unlocking Isaiah in the Book of Mormon (Salt Lake City 2003)
 
 

Saints and Sinners Prepare to Meet Christ
 

2 Nephi 12/Isaiah 2
 

Isaiah sees the latter-day temple, gathering of Israel, and millennial judgment and peace— The proud and wicked shall be brought low at the Second Coming
 

Nephi’s citation of Isaiah 2 begins a block of thirteen Isaiah chapters quoted in 2 Nephi. It is usually in this section of the Book of Mormon where first-time readers bog down and stop because of their frustration in understanding these Isaiah passages. After readers have eventually worked their way through this Isaiah section and have read the whole Book of Mormon, they often bypass these chapters in their future readings. Unfortunately, by just grudgingly “plowing through” or randomly “skipping over” Isaiah’s teachings, Book of Mormon readers miss profound insights and key prophecies directed to covenant followers of the Lord in the last days. We will carefully discuss these chapters so that we can better understand and appreciate their contents.
 

The first chapter in this block, Isaiah 2, records a remarkable vision of latter-day temples, teaching the Israelites to turn from their wicked ways and “walk in the light of the Lord” (v. 5). Isaiah also delivers his oft-quoted prophecy that nations will “beat their swords into plow-shares” (v. 4) and the world will finally be free from war. He continues warning Israel of the superstitious, worldly influences of idolatrous neighboring nations. He then testifies that “the day of the Lord” (v. 12) will destroy the proud, and “the fear of the Lord” (v. 19) will terrify the idolatrous. The Book of Mormon version of this chapter includes insightful additions that clarify these powerful promises and warnings of the last days.
 

2 Nephi 12:1–4: “The mountain of the Lord’s house”
 

The first four verses of this chapter contain a profound revelation Isaiah received concerning the latter days. Verse 1 introduces the vision as “the word that Isaiah . . . saw concerning Judah and Jerusalem.” The word “saw” makes it unclear whether Isaiah viewed the events as a vision or read them from a scroll or book, like Lehi (1 Nephi 1:11). Interestingly, Micah, one of Isaiah’s younger contemporaries, records an almost identical revelation from the Lord (Micah 4:1–4).
 

In their writings, both prophets describe a “mountain of the Lord’s house”1 that will be established “in the last days” and will “be exalted above the hills.” The importance of the Lord’s house is apparent, for “all nations shall flow unto it” (v. 2) and it is here that God will “teach . . . of his ways” (vv. 2–3). Many prophets and apostles of this dispensation have shared important insights about these passages, providing various clarifications beginning with the identification of “the mountain of the Lord’s house” as a temple, especially the Salt Lake Temple. The Salt Lake Temple is literally a house of the Lord that has been established in the tops of the Rocky Mountains. This temple stands as a symbol of the Church’s determination and perseverance, and it is easily recognized throughout the world. Elder LeGrand Richards, in reference to the Salt Lake Temple, said:
 

This temple on this temple block is that house of the God of Jacob. . . . Isn’t it a glorious thing, one of the most beautiful buildings in the world? Those of us who filled missions in the early days know how literally every convert, as soon as they joined the Church, would want to sell everything they had, saving their money . . . by the nickels and dimes, until they could find enough to come to this land because of the drawing power of that temple, so that they could learn of his ways and walk in his paths.2
 

Thousands of early members of the Church, representing many nations, flocked to the Salt Lake Temple to learn of God and to be with other Saints. Currently, several million people visit Temple Square annually, and during the 2002 Winter Olympics, Isaiah’s prophecy was further fulfilled:
 

As Salt Lake City has hosted the 2002 Winter Olympic and Paralympic Games, we have seen a partial fulfillment of many prophecies. The nations of the earth and many of their leaders have come. . . . “The mountain of the house of the Lord,” with its brightly lit spires, has been witnessed by 3.5 billion people around the world.3
 

In a broader sense, the reference to the Lord’s house applies to all temples throughout the earth. In each of these holy temples, millions can leave the world behind them, saying, “Let us go up to the mountain of the Lord, to the house of the God of Jacob.” Temple worship is a way for Saints to rise above the secular teachings of the world, learn about the plan of salvation, and make covenants with God.
 

In addition, Latter-day Saints from throughout the world travel semiannually to Salt Lake City to receive instruction from the Lord’s prophets at general conference. In October 2000, President Gordon B. Hinckley quoted this Isaiah passage as he dedicated the LDS Conference Center:
 

As I contemplate this marvelous structure, adjacent to the temple, there comes to mind the great prophetic utterance of Isaiah: [quotes Isaiah 2:2–3, 5]. I believe that prophecy applies to the historic and wonderful Salt Lake Temple. But I believe also that it is related to this magnificent hall. For it is from this pulpit that the law of God shall go forth, together with the word and testimony of the Lord.4
 

In addition to temples and the Conference Center, future magnificent buildings might be a part of Isaiah’s vision. Elder Bruce R. McConkie, emphasizing the multiple fulfillment of this prophecy, reveals two additional interpretations of this scripture:
 

The day is yet future when the Lord’s house is to be built on that “Mount Zion” which is “the city of New Jerusalem” in Jackson County Missouri (D&C 84:2–4). Mount Zion, itself, will be the mountain of the Lord’s house in the day when that glorious temple is erected. [Also,] When the Jews flee unto Jerusalem, it will be “unto the mountains of the Lord’s house” (D&C 133:13), for a holy temple is to be built there also as part of the work of the great era of restoration (Ezekiel 37:24–28).5
 

Two great temples will be built in the last days, one in New Jerusalem and one in Old Jerusalem. According to Isaiah, these two cities will serve an important role in the last days, “for out of Zion shall go forth the law, and the word of the Lord from Jerusalem” (v. 3). Although many biblical scholars feel that this verse refers solely to “old” Jerusalem, President Joseph Fielding Smith has explained: “There are to be two great capitals. One Jerusalem of old and the other the City of Zion, or New Jerusalem. The latter is to be on this continent. The one will be the Lord’s headquarters for the people of Judah and Israel his companions; the other for Joseph and his companions.”6 The law and word of the Lord will go forth from these two cities to the ends of the earth.
 

In addition to spiritual law, political and social law may also come forth out of Zion in the last days. President Harold B. Lee relates an interesting insight concerning the phrase “out of Zion shall go forth the law”:
 

Years ago I went with the brethren to the Idaho Falls Temple, and I heard in that inspired prayer . . . a definition of the meaning of that term “out of Zion shall go forth the law.” Note what they said: “We thank thee that thou hast revealed to us that those who gave us our constitutional form of government were men wise in thy sight and that thou didst raise them up for the very purpose of putting forth that sacred document [the Constitution of the United States]. . . . 
 

“We pray that kings and rulers and the peoples of all nations under heaven may be persuaded of the blessings enjoyed by the people of this land by reason of their freedom and under thy guidance and be constrained to adopt similar governmental systems, thus to fulfill the ancient prophecy of Isaiah and Micah that ‘out of Zion shall go forth the law and the word of the Lord from Jerusalem.’”7
 

When the Lord’s Zion and Jerusalem have been established, men “shall beat their swords into plow-shares, and their spears into pruning-hooks” (v. 4). They will no longer need these weapons of warlike destruction and will use them as tools of peaceful production instead. President Hinckley petitioned for such a time in his masterful “War and Peace” talk given in the April 2003 general conference: “We can hope and pray for that glorious day foretold by the prophet Isaiah when men ‘shall beat their swords into plowshares, and . . . neither shall they learn war any more.’”8
 

Although the ultimate fulfillment of this prophecy may seem far distant, others have already been inspired by Isaiah’s vision, and it has often been quoted at the end of wars or as peace treaties have been signed. For example, at the end of the U.S. Civil War in 1865,
 

General Lee and General Grant met in Appomattox, near the famous apple tree, where General Grant wrote the terms of the surrender of the Confederate Army. He . . . magnanimously permitted the Confederates to retain their horses. He had a vision. The spirit of Isaiah rested upon him. Swords were to be beaten into plow shares. He beheld the battle-scarred fields of Dixie blooming in incomparable beauty with corn and cotton. . . . The great soldier decided to turn into plow horses those war horses, which had hauled the Confederates’ artillery to points where death and devastation could be poured into the Union ranks.9
 

And more recently, as the peace treaty between Egypt and Israel was signed in March 1979, Isaiah’s prophecy of turning swords into plowshares was quoted.10 The time will come when all people will unite in peaceful pursuits. “Nation shall not lift up sword against nation, neither shall they learn war any more,” for the “knowledge of the Lord” will cover the earth and inspire all humanity to embrace peace (v. 4; 2 Nephi 21:9/Isaiah 11:9).
 

2 Nephi 12:5–8: “They be replenished from the east”
 

In verse 5, Isaiah’s focus shifts from the future back to his own time. After witnessing this vision of a peaceful earth, Isaiah pleads with Israel to “walk in the light of the Lord.” The Book of Mormon contains an additional phrase that reveals why Isaiah is calling them to repentance: “Yea, come, for ye have all gone astray, every one to his wicked ways.” Because of the Israelites’ wickedness, Isaiah knows that they will be denied future blessings unless they turn from their evil ways and embrace the light. In consequence of their rebellious behavior, the Lord has forsaken His people (v. 6).
 

Isaiah reveals one reason for their wicked behavior in verse 6. To understand these allegations, one needs to review the history of the Israelites’ entry into their tribal lands. When Joshua led the children of Israel into the promised land, the Lord commanded them to cleanse the lands of the wicked people residing there. He then gave the Israelites a promise and a warning:
 

Be ye therefore very courageous to keep and to do all that is written in the book of the law of Moses, that ye turn not aside therefrom to the right hand or to the left; That ye come not among these nations, these that remain among you; neither make mention of the name of their gods, nor cause to swear by them, neither serve them, nor bow yourselves unto them. . . . Else if ye do in any wise go back, and cleave unto the remnant of these nations, even these that remain among you . . . ; Know for a certainty that the Lord your God will no more drive out any of these nations from before you (Joshua 23:6–7, 12–13).
 

The Lord had brought the Israelites into the midst of pagan nations, where the continents of Asia, Africa, and Europe come together, to set them up as an exemplary nation. Instead, as verse 6 indicates, the children of Israel began to follow the example given by the surrounding idolatrous inhabitants. The Israelites were being “replenished from the east,” a likely reference to the magicians of Babylon, “hearken[ing] unto soothsayers like the Philistines,” and “pleas[ing] themselves in the children of strangers [Gentiles]” (v. 6). Instead of influencing their neighbors by worshiping the one true God in a Zion-like society, they were being influenced by their neighbors’ gods of silver and gold and their wicked lifestyle. Israel was following after the treasures of man instead of leading toward the riches of eternal life.
 

2 Nephi 12:9–17: “For the day of the Lord of Hosts soon cometh”
 

The chosen people’s focus on their neighbors’ worldly items leads to pride, as described in verse 9. Instead of being meek and submissive, worshiping their God, the Israelites become proud as they turn to idols and follow the false teachings of their Gentile neighbors. It seems that forgiveness is no longer possible for them. In the King James Version, verse 9 reads, “The mean man boweth down, and the great man humbleth himself: therefore forgive them not.” In this translation, the Lord is seemingly refusing to forgive those who bow down and humble themselves before Him. The Savior does not work in this way, as the Book of Mormon clarifies: “The mean man boweth not down, and the great man humbleth himself not.” It is for the arrogant and proud who refuse to repent that the Lord will show no forgiveness.
 

Isaiah issues a warning in verse 10 to these wicked and proud individuals, proclaiming that they should “enter into the rock, and hide . . . in the dust, for the fear of the Lord and the glory of his majesty shall smite” them. While the righteous are making the journey up to the mountain of the Lord to seek His presence, the wicked will search for places to hide within the rocks and caves of the mountain, trying to escape His wrath! Nevertheless, their attempts to conceal themselves will fail as the fear and majesty of the Lord comes upon them.
 

Those who worship idols and are lifted up in pride will be held accountable, as verses 11 and 17 reveal. These two verses deliver essentially the same message: the “loftiness of man shall be bowed down . . . and the Lord alone shall be exalted in that day.” All those who refuse to bow down before God and are prideful will fall before the Lord at the last day, for “every knee shall bow, every tongue shall swear” that Jesus Christ is the Savior of this earth (Isaiah 45:23).
 

Between verses 11 and 17 is a description of several different status symbols of ancient times. Isaiah uses these familiar terms to teach his people about the fleeting nature of worldly items. “The cedars of Lebanon” were symbols of strength and glory, and the wood from these forests provided beautiful and fragrant wood for Israelite buildings, including Solomon’s palace and temple (1 Kings 6:20; 7:2–3). The “oaks of Bashan” were also used for building, as they were one of the few local sources of hardwood in the arid lands of Palestine. The “high mountains” and surrounding hills, and every “high tower” and “fenced wall” represent man’s trust in natural and man-made defenses. Eventually, “all the nations which are lifted up” and “every people” will be humbled.11
 

Isaiah uses another familiar symbol of industry and strength to teach the Israelites: ships. The ships of the sea are a general reference to commercial enterprises. Some of the most famous commercial ships were the ships of Tarshish, known for their large storage capacities and their ability to travel long distances. The “pleasant pictures,” translated in some versions of the Bible as “beautiful crafts,” may be a reference to the pleasure crafts or ships the wealthy traveled in throughout the Mediterranean. While verse 16 consists of three phrases—the ships of the sea, the ships of Tarshish, and the pleasant pictures, the ancient Greek (Septuagint) and Hebrew (KJV) texts each contain only two of the above phrases, as shown below:

The Book of Mormon is the only text of the three that retains all the phrases, again indicating that the brass plates contained a more correct and unedited version of the original Isaiah text. With the Book of Mormon additions and corrections, a more complete, accurate picture emerges concerning the “day of the Lord of Hosts.” All of these worldly symbols of greatness and strength will be brought low as the Lord and His kingdom are exalted in that day (v. 17).
 

2 Nephi 12:18–22: “They shall go into the holes of the rocks”
 

In verses 18–22, Isaiah more fully describes the fear and destruction that will come upon the proud idol-worshipers at the Lord’s second coming. These verses provide a stark contrast to the opening images of the chapter, which describe the righteous going up to the mountain of the Lord to learn of Christ and walk in His ways. The wicked will “go into the clefts of the rocks, and into the tops of the ragged rocks, for the fear of the Lord shall come upon them” (v. 21). At the time of Christ’s coming, these wicked people will know of their sins and strive to hide from the Lord in the rocks of the mountains, even “when [the Lord] ariseth to shake terribly the earth” (v. 19). This seems to be describing the terrible earthquake foretold by John the Revelator when “every mountain and island were moved out of their places.” John also sees the great and rich people fleeing into the rocks of the mountains as they fear the Lord’s coming. They flee into the mountains not to find refuge but to seek death (Revelation 6:14–16). Their fear of the Lord and their desire for death beneath the trembling mountains is echoed in the Book of Mormon. Amulek warns that if we have hardened our hearts against God’s word, we will proclaim, “We shall not dare to look up to our God; and we would fain be glad if we could command the rocks and the mountains to fall upon us to hide us from his presence” (Alma 12:14). What could be worse than a suffocating death in the depths of a mountain? For the wicked, it is meeting the Lord to face His sure, powerful judgments!
 

In their efforts to flee the wrath of God, the wicked will “cast his idols of silver, and his idols of gold . . . to the moles and to the bats” (v. 20). Those who have spent major time and effort to accumulate silver and gold for their idols will eventually cast these precious items to the seemingly blind inhabitants of the darkened world—moles and bats. Those who had gathered wealth and idols (vv. 7–8) were spiritually blinded by their pride (vv. 11–12), and they will throw their shining idols to those who never see in the physical light of day. Instead of providing service and resources to assist Heavenly Father’s work, these wicked children of God have devoted their energy and devotion to worldly objects, which will enrich only creatures hidden in the earth.
 

Isaiah ends this chapter with a blunt proclamation: “Cease ye from man, whose breath is in his nostrils; for wherein is he to be accounted of?” (v. 22). Isaiah pleads with the Israelites to stop seeking after the desire of the natural man, emphasizing that mankind is accountable before the Lord. Earth life is a probationary period for moral and spiritual development. God will not reward us according to how much wealth we have accumulated; instead, He will judge us on how we lived while we enjoyed the breath of life upon this earth.
 

We must each strive to “walk in the light of the Lord” so that we are not seeking to hide from Christ’s presence among the ragged rocks when He comes again. By walking in the light, we will be prepared to meet our Savior, walk in His paths, and live in the day when peace will cover the earth. As members of the Church, our goal should be to enter the “mountain of the Lord’s house” so that we can make and keep the covenants essential for eternal life. We should also devote our resources to building Christ’s kingdom so that we can prepare the earth and its inhabitants for the time when war will be no more.
