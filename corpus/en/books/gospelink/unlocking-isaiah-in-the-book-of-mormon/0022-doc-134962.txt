# The Great Suffering Servant Song

### The Great Suffering Servant Song
Victor L. Ludlow, Unlocking Isaiah in the Book of Mormon (Salt Lake City 2003)
 
 

The Great Suffering Servant Song
 

Mosiah 14/Isaiah 53
 

Isaiah speaks Messianically— Messiah’s humiliation and sufferings set forth— He makes his soul an offering for sin and makes intercession for transgressors
 

This chapter covers the most noteworthy event in Heavenly Father’s plan of salvation—the mission of Jesus Christ. Isaiah’s prophetic description foretells the suffering servant’s humble birth, mortal ministry, vicarious suffering, inglorious death, and eternal rewards. Through majestic poetry, Isaiah provides valuable insights about our Savior and His atonement. Almost six centuries later, the prophet Abinadi cites this passage as he teaches King Noah and the wicked priests. Because of the material’s doctrinal significance, the following discussion includes inspired commentary from several modern prophets. This profound chapter merits thoughtful study so we can better appreciate the suffering that God’s Son endured for us.
 

Mosiah 14:1: “Who hath believed our report?”
 

Isaiah begins by challenging the skeptics who refuse to believe that the literal Son of God would descend to a life of mortality. There are two main groups of skeptics: ancient Israelites and modern scholars. Although prophets had foretold the Messiah’s coming for hundreds of years, many Jews of Jesus’ time could not recognize Jesus of Nazareth as the embodiment of these Messianic prophecies. Now, centuries later, some Jewish and Christian scholars believe that Isaiah’s great messianic hymn refers to the house of Israel and not the Savior. Joseph Fielding Smith refutes this interpretation, stating:
 

Now Bible commentators will tell you that [Isaiah 53] has nothing to do with the life of Jesus Christ. To them this story is one concerning suffering Israel. I want to tell you that it is a story, a synopsis of the life of our Redeemer, revealed to Isaiah 700 years before the Lord was born. If you have the proper discernment you will discover this.1
 

Although Isaiah’s prophecy that a great Redeemer would come to save humankind from sin was fulfilled, many still fail to recognize Jesus as the Messiah portrayed in this prophetic servant song.
 

In verse 1, Isaiah asks two hypothetical questions that challenge first our convictions and then our commitments. When he asks, “Who hath believed our report?” we question ourselves by asking, “Do I believe Isaiah’s account?” By prompting us to contemplate our beliefs about the Savior, Isaiah prepares us for the powerful truths in the following verses. The second question examines our behavior. Isaiah asks, “To whom is the arm of the Lord revealed?” or in other words, who is the described person who receives the Lord’s saving power? The answer is those whose righteous actions show that they have partaken of Christ’s atoning sacrifice.
 

Isaiah and other prophets use imagery of the “arm” to symbolize the Lord’s immense power and authority. Ezekiel portrays the Lord’s commanding power, writing, “with a stretched out arm, and with fury poured out, will [the Lord God] rule over you” (Ezekiel 20:33). Jeremiah rejoices in the Lord’s creative abilities, saying, “Ah Lord God! behold, thou hast made the heaven and the earth by thy great power and stretched out arm, and there is nothing too hard for thee” (Jeremiah 32:17). Nephi prophesies of the Lord’s mercy, teaching, “I will be merciful unto them, saith the Lord God, if they will repent and come unto me; for mine arm is lengthened out all the day long” (2 Nephi 28:32). Joseph Smith describes the Lord’s might, visualizing His arm “fall upon the nations” at His Second Coming (D&C 45:47). Thus, the image of the Lord’s arm portrays the full range of divine powers—from creative to destructive and from painful justice to joyful mercy.
 

Mosiah 14:2–3: “A root out of dry ground”
 

Verses 2 and 3 outline the Savior’s mortal life. Isaiah first depicts the development and environment of the Savior, describing Him as a “tender plant” and “a root out of dry ground.” These images of plant life suggest He was to be born and raised like an ordinary child, and He would grow and progress just as all mortals do. He was to be neither an especially strong plant nor a unique plant, described only as “tender,” or yowneq—a Hebrew term defining a twig or limb sprouting from a felled tree.2 In addition, Isaiah tells us that this plant was to come out of dry ground, symbolic of both the spiritual famine present on the earth during Christ’s ministry and the literal aridity of His Middle Eastern homeland.
 

Elder Bruce R. McConkie elaborates on some possible interpretations of Isaiah’s description of Christ:
 

He shall grow up . . . “not like a stately tree, but like a lowly plant, struggling in arid soil. So the human life of the Messiah was one of obscurity and humility.” . . . Or: “Messiah grew silently and insensibly, as a sucker from an ancient stock, seemingly dead (viz., the house of David, then in a decayed state).” . . . Or: Perhaps better still, he grew up as a choice and favored plant whose strength and achievement did not come because of the arid social culture in which he dwelt; it was not poured into him by the erudition of Rabbinical teachers; but it came from the divine Source whence he sprang.3
 

Out of an impoverished political and religious background came forth the Son of God to fulfill His earthly ministry.
 

Verse 2 continues with Isaiah’s description of the Savior’s physical body having “no form nor comeliness” or special beauty to attract special attention. In other words, Christ was a mortal man, and He looked like other Jewish men from Galilee. Elder McConkie explains this passage and writes about the humanness of the Savior, writing that there was “no mystique” or “dynamic appearance” about Jesus. He walked, spoke, and dressed like other people, appearing as “a man among men.”4 The Savior’s humble birth and simple life are what led many to ignore or deny His divine identity. Those who were expecting a great and glorious king completely overlooked the physical Son of God, who appeared as the inconspicuous stepson of a carpenter from Nazareth.
 

People would do more than just look past Jesus, they would also despise Him. In verse 3, Isaiah describes their scorn: “He is despised and rejected of men; . . . he was despised, and we esteemed him not.” Isaiah’s description foretells people’s general rejection of the Savior. People could have despised and rejected Jesus for any number of reasons: they may have disapproved of His humble appearance; they may have scorned His provincial Galilean background; they may have disapproved the company of sinners and tax collectors with whom He associated; they may have expected a scholar or theologian with academic credentials; they may have disagreed with His doctrines and teachings; and particularly, they may have opposed His witness of His own divinity.
 

Isaiah’s statement that “we esteemed him not” (v. 3) makes the rejection of the Savior more personal, alleging that it is we who deny the Lord and “esteem him not.” Isaiah emphasizes our denial of Christ, charging, “We hid as it were our faces from him.”
 

Isaiah also explains the Lord’s tribulation, writing, “He is . . . a man of sorrows, and acquainted with grief” (v. 3). This synonymous account of Christ’s suffering highlights the great empathy and pain He experienced while on the earth, especially for those who experience disease, sorrow, and sin. He also endured personal pain. Throughout His ministry, the compassionate teacher of righteousness was subjected to the most inhumane treatment. He was ridiculed and spat upon, whipped and beaten, and nailed to a cross. Many of His friends and family left Him at His most difficult hour, and one of His closest associates betrayed Him. Indeed, the Savior was very well acquainted with grief. President Howard W. Hunter comments:
 

Jesus was not spared grief and pain and anguish and buffeting. No tongue can speak the unutterable burden he carried, nor have we the wisdom to understand the prophet Isaiah’s description of him as “a man of sorrows.” . . . His ship was tossed most of his life, and, at least to mortal eyes, it crashed fatally on the rocky coast of Calvary. We are asked not to look on his life with mortal eyes; with spiritual vision we know something quite different was happening upon the cross.
 

It seems ironic to the “mortal eye” that the Savior constantly preached peace when He lived a life of utter sorrow. Yet with our “spiritual vision,” we see that Jesus’ experience is one of ultimate peace and reassurance, giving us strength in our own trials. President Hunter continues:
 

Peace was on the lips and in the heart of the Savior no matter how fiercely the tempest was raging. May it so be with us—in our own hearts, in our own homes, in our nations of the world, and even in the buffetings faced from time to time by the Church. We should not expect to get through life individually or collectively without some opposition.5
 

Elder Jeffrey R. Holland also teaches about the adversities faced by the Savior. He reminds us that we cannot forget “the scars of sacrifice, the lesions of love” that Christ suffered for us. He is truly the “captain of our soul[s]” who has Himself experienced the roughest waters of life. Thus, He can lead us safely back to the harbor.6
 

Mosiah 14:4–5: “With his stripes we are healed”
 

In verses 4 and 5, Isaiah reports the intensity of Christ’s suffering and the magnitude of His sacrifice. He tells how Christ endured the consequences of our sins, writing, “He has borne our griefs, and carried our sorrows.” This phrase is especially symbolic. We, with any burden or scar of sin, can in no way enter the kingdom of God. The Savior takes our load of sin. He implores, “Come unto me, all ye that . . . are heavy laden, . . . for my yoke is easy, and my burden is light” (Matthew 11:28, 30). If we take advantage of the Atonement in our lives, the Savior will literally lighten the grief and sorrow caused by our sins.
 

Isaiah describes the Savior’s agony with five different words: “We did esteem him stricken, smitten of God, and afflicted. . . . He was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities” (vv. 4–5). Elder James E. Talmage witnesses of Christ’s tremendous suffering with the following words:
 

Christ’s agony in the garden is unfathomable by the finite mind, both as to intensity and cause. The thought that He suffered through fear of death is untenable. . . . He struggled and groaned under a burden such as no other being who has lived on earth might even conceive as possible. It was not physical pain, nor mental anguish alone, that caused Him to suffer such torture as to produce an extrusion of blood from every pore; but a spiritual agony of soul such as only God was capable of experiencing. No other man, however great his powers of physical or mental endurance could have suffered so; for his human organism would have succumbed, and syncope [anemia; a loss of blood or oxygen] would have produced unconsciousness and welcome oblivion. In that hour of anguish Christ met and overcame all the horrors that Satan, “the prince of this world” could inflict.7
 

The prophets Isaiah and Talmage help us appreciate Christ’s tribulation and the infinite Atonement required to satisfy both the demands of justice and also the expectations of mercy.
 

Isaiah uses antithetic parallels at the end of verse 5, contrasting “chastisement” (or punishment) with “peace,” and “stripes” (or wounds) with “healing.” The phrase “with his stripes we are healed” presents a profound paradox. Through the physical affliction of our Redeemer, the body and spirit are restored to wholeness—a state of purity and perfection that allow us as resurrected beings to return to Heavenly Father’s presence. As President Hunter testifies, “Without the Atonement there would be no hope of eternal life. . . . In its absence the whole plan of creation would come to naught.”8
 

Mosiah 14:6–7: “We, like sheep, have gone astray. . . . He is brought as a lamb to the slaughter”
 

Verses 6 and 7 use the image of sheep to contrast the natural rebelliousness of mankind with the submissive nature of the Savior. Isaiah describes people wandering away from God’s care and scattering among the nations like unruly flocks. Jesus, the perfect lamb, does not go astray but rather becomes “a patient and willing Sacrifice, silent under affliction, as a lamb brought to the slaughter.”9 Although Christ endured great suffering, He did not once complain of His difficult mission. His life is the ultimate example of endurance and charity.
 

Isaiah likens Christ’s silence before His accusers to a sheep who “before her shearers is dumb” (v. 6). During the last difficult hours of His life, the Lord “opened not his mouth,” and He silently suffered pain as only the Son of God could (v. 7). When brought before the Sanhedrin, the Savior “held his peace,” refusing to defend himself (Mark 14:61). In the presence of Pilate, He similarly “answered nothing” (Mark 15:2–5). In His final audience before Herod, Christ was mute yet again (Luke 23:8–9). Ignoring the requests of mortals, the Savior submitted His will to that of His Father. In the final moments before His atonement in the Garden of Gethsemane, Christ did not cry out against the impending torment from hell, but He simply declared to the Father, “Not my will, but thine, be done” (Luke 22:42).
 

Isaiah’s classic image of the lamb going to the slaughter has other parallels in the scriptures. Like Christ, Isaac allowed himself to become a sacrifice to God. Abraham, comparing his son’s sacrifice with that of a lamb, says, “God will provide himself a lamb for a burnt offering” (Genesis 22:8). Joseph Smith similarly depicts himself as a sacrificial offering, saying, “I am going like a lamb to the slaughter” (D&C 135:4).
 

Seeing ourselves as sheep helps us appreciate our dependence on the Lord. Just as sheep require a shepherd to watch over them and provide for their needs, so we need the Lord’s tender care. Who better to care for the sheep than the Good Shepherd? He came among the children of men to care for His Father’s flock. Isaiah describes the Lord’s role as a shepherd in a beautiful passage not cited in the Book of Mormon, explaining, “He shall feed his flock like a shepherd: he shall gather the lambs with his arm, and carry them in his bosom, and shall gently lead those that are with young” (Isaiah 40:11). With potent imagery, Isaiah portrays the Savior as both the sacrificial lamb and the compassionate shepherd.
 

Mosiah 14:8–9: “Who shall declare his generation?”
 

Isaiah outlines the crucifixion and subsequent death of the Savior in verses 8 and 9. The accuracy of Isaiah’s description as well as the verses’ specific focus illustrate that the life and death of Jesus Christ were indeed literal events fulfilled by God’s Son.
 

Isaiah asks an important open-ended question in verse 8. He asks each of us, “Who shall declare his generation?” The Hebrew word for generation, dowr, means a “revolution of time,” or an age. The question might be translated, “Who shall testify of His life mission?” Unfaithful children of God fail to “declare his generation” or testify of His life because of their inability to recognize and declare Christ as their Savior. We must “declare his generation,” or His life, mission, and gospel. President David O. McKay teaches that people who know in their heart that Heavenly Father and Jesus Christ restored the gospel to the earth have “the greatest possession that the human mind can possess.”10 As an answer to Isaiah’s question, Latter-day Saints with a testimony are the ones who can clearly and powerfully declare Jesus Christ and His gospel to the world (compare Mosiah 15:10–12).
 

Key insights about the Savior’s crucifixion, death, and burial are depicted in these two verses. Isaiah describes the imprisonment and trial of the Savior in verse 8, writing, “He was taken from prison and from judgment.” The New Testament records the arrest of Jesus and various judgment settings during His last hours.11 Isaiah next outlines the death and burial of the Savior, explaining that He would make “his grave with the wicked” and that he would be “with the rich in his death” (v. 9). Jesus was crucified between two common thieves, and a wealthy disciple, Joseph of Arimathea, provided a family tomb for His burial (Luke 23:32–33; Matthew 27:57–60). These accurate details of the Savior’s death show that Isaiah had intimate knowledge of his Savior’s earthly mission.
 

Verse 9 highlights the truth inherent in Christ’s teachings: “Neither was any deceit in his mouth.” The mouth often represents how God’s word is delivered. For example, when Isaiah was prepared for his prophetic calling, his mouth was purified so that he could stand in the presence of the Lord (2 Nephi 16:5–7/Isaiah 6:5–7). The Savior’s mouth symbolizes the importance of the words that He preached during His ministry. The teachings of Jesus, a “God of truth,” are powerful tools to counter the deception propagated by the devil, the “father of all lies” (Deuteronomy 32:4; Psalm 31:5; 2 Nephi 2:18).
 

Mosiah 14:10–12: “The pleasure of the Lord shall prosper” 
 

Moving from past to future tense, Isaiah describes the Messiah’s eventual victory over death. He also recounts the rewards to be bestowed upon the suffering servant because of His great sacrifice.
 

At the beginning of verse 10, the Lord is described as being “pleased” at the great servant’s affliction. This is an awkward phrase to translate from the Hebrew. It could also be translated, “Yet it was the Lord’s will to afflict him.”12 The “Lord’s will” could refer either to Christ Himself, who willingly submitted to the agony of Gethsemane, or to the Father, who desired that His Son provide the great atoning sacrifice. Of course, God the Father was not happy that His Son suffered so greatly during His mortal ministry. Elder Melvin J. Ballard portrays Heavenly Father as being “like the mother who bids farewell to her dying child, [and] has to be taken out of the room, so as not to look upon the last struggles. So he bowed his head, and hid in some part of his universe, his great heart almost breaking for the love that he had for his Son.” The Savior’s sufferings, however, are an integral part of the plan. As Elder Ballard continues, “Without His sacrifice, we would have remained [in sin], and we would never have come glorified into His presence.”13 By allowing His Only Begotten Son in the flesh to suffer for the sins of mankind, God made it possible for His spirit children to return to Him as purified, resurrected beings.
 

Later in verse 10, Isaiah outlines some specific blessings Christ will receive for His sacrifice: “He shall see his seed, he shall prolong his days, and the pleasure of the Lord shall prosper in his hand.” Because the Savior was faithful and obedient to the end, God will bless Him with everlasting life and a great posterity of His “seed.” After quoting this Isaiah passage, the prophet Abinadi explains that Christ’s seed are those who receive His word and then believe, obey, and share it (Mosiah 15:10–14; see also 4 Nephi 1:17; Mormon 9:26; Moroni 7:19). King Benjamin also teaches that Christ’s family consists of those who are “spiritually begotten” as His sons and daughters (Mosiah 5:7). Elder Bruce R. McConkie expounds upon the seed or family of Christ:
 

He shall see his seed! How aptly, in poetic and prophetic language, this reminds all who believe that they are the children of their Messiah. Seed is the progeny of the species. Among us men it is our children. The children of the Lord Jesus Christ are those who believe in him and obey his gospel, those who exercise the power given them to become his sons and his daughters, and who as a consequence are adopted into his family.14
 

We Latter-day Saints are some of the seed spoken of by Isaiah and Abinadi—the “heirs of the kingdom of God” (Mosiah 15:11).
 

Isaiah next describes blessings we receive as we join Christ’s kingdom and share His blessings from the Father. As part of His spiritual family, we are entitled to a special endowment or gift. Thus, when God divides “a portion with the great” and “the spoil with the strong” (v. 12), He shares all He has with His faithful children. We become “joint-heirs” (Romans 8:17) with Christ in this inheritance. Elder McConkie defines the term joint-heir:
 

A joint-heir is one who inherits equally with all other heirs including the Chief Heir who is the Son. Each joint-heir has an equal and an undivided portion of the whole of everything. . . . If the universe belongs to one, so it does equally to the total of all upon whom the joint inheritances are bestowed. Joint-heirs are possessors of all things (D&C 50:26–28). All things are theirs for they have exaltation (D&C 76:50–60). They are made “equal” with their Lord (D&C 88:107). They gain all power both in heaven and on earth and receive the fullness of the Father, and all knowledge and truth are theirs (D&C 93:15–30).15
 

As joint-heirs with Christ, we can be among the “noble and great ones who [are] chosen” to build His kingdom and receive the blessings of eternal life (D&C 138:55; Abraham 3:22).
 

Verse 11 proclaims that the righteous servant will “justify” those who will receive these blessings. Justification take place when the Savior vindicates our worthiness to come into God’s presence. Jesus fulfills this function when He serves as our Mediator and Advocate with the Father. President Joseph Fielding Smith teaches that Jesus “pleads our cause” and seeks to “bring us in agreement with God his Father. . . . That is part of his great mission.”16 As our Savior pleads for us to the Father, He justifies us as worthy to share celestial life with them. Indeed, He makes “intercession for the transgressors” (v. 12) as He mediates our status with God. Elder McConkie identifies the Savior’s great Intercessory Prayer as one of the best examples of His petitioning the Father in our behalf:
 

One of the great Messianic prophecies foretold that Christ would make “intercession for the transgressors.” (Isaiah 53:12; Mosiah 14:12.) His great Intercessory Prayer, excerpts of which are found in the 17th chapter of John, with some of the same petitions later being repeated on behalf of the Nephites (3 Nephi 19), is one of the chief illustrations of his pleadings on behalf of his brethren.17
 

Isaiah’s prophetic promise that the Messiah will intercede for sinners is demonstrated through His powerful prayers and works in our behalf.
 

Throughout verses 10–12, Isaiah describes the Savior’s atonement with a variety of phrases: “his soul [was] an offering for sin,” “[he experienced] travail of his soul,” “he shall bear their iniquities,” “he hath poured out his soul unto death,” “he bore the sins of many,” and “[he] made intercession for the transgressors.” These dimensions of Christ’s sacrifice show us that the Atonement is a complex and vital component of the plan of salvation. The Savior invites us to partake of His atoning sacrifice as we come unto Him and repent. Otherwise, we must suffer even as He suffered, which suffering caused him such intense pain and agony (D&C 19:15–19). This infinite suffering was the climax of the Savior’s mission and the central focus of Isaiah’s “Suffering Servant Song.”
 

When the prophet Abinadi quoted this Isaianic prophecy to wicked King Noah’s court, at least one young priest believed Abinadi’s teachings. Alma not only believed, but he also declared Christ’s message and mission to many others who came into the Shepherd’s fold. Numerous of God’s children through the ages have believed Isaiah’s powerful witness of the suffering servant. Learning from and acting upon Isaiah’s testimony and the testimony of others, such as the prophet Abinadi, they have come unto Christ to enjoy celestial blessings with Him, the Father, and others of God’s children. Let us do the same.
