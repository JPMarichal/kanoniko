# The More-Better Quandary

### The More-Better Quandary
S. Michael Wilcox, What Scriptures Teach Us about Prosperity (Salt Lake City 2010)
 
 

The More-Better Quandary

SHOULD I SEEK WEALTH?

The first
major teachings in the Book of Mormon about riches come to us from the prophet
Jacob. Since Jacob was told by his brother Nephi to only include on the small
plates those things he considered to be “most precious,” or “preaching which
was sacred, or revelation which was great” (Jacob 1:2, 4), his decision to
include his people’s flirtation with wealth, on the very limited space
available on the plates, shows what a critical feature of Book of Mormon
thought this is. It is something we in the modern world need to hear and must
not ignore or lightly pass over as necessary for others, but not
ourselves.

The problem
is introduced by the following narrative: “They . . . began to search much gold and silver, and began to be lifted up somewhat in pride” (Jacob 1:16; emphasis
added). There are some fascinating words to think about in this passage. Jacob,
like any discerning priesthood leader, wants to stop the problem before it gets
too large. His people are just beginning their intoxication with wealth. I
wonder about the word “much.” Is seeking some acceptable? How much and how will
I know when I have crossed the line of demarcation between safety and danger?
They have not reached full-blown pride as seen later in the Book of
Mormon. There is that intriguing “somewhat.” As we have seen, “somewhats” have
a tendency to grow and do so rapidly.

“Now behold,
my brethren, this is the word which I declare unto you, that many of you have
begun to search for gold, and for silver, and for all manner of precious ores,
in the which this land, which is a land of promise unto you and to your seed,
doth abound most plentifully” (Jacob 2:12). This is not a bad description of
our own day and time. The land still abounds in plenty, though most of us are
not literally mining the earth. We seek riches in other ways. The question we
must ask is, “Is the seeking of wealth permitted?” It seems that without
exception the scriptures answer with a resounding no! At first glance this
seems a point for debate and usually those who think it is all right to seek
wealth will inevitably turn to Jacob for support. We will examine closely that
verse shortly, but for the time being we must stick to our conclusion that
seeking wealth is not a scripturally permissible thing to do. There are simply
too many other things to seek more worthy of our attention.

We can seek
“for wisdom” (D&C 6:7; 11:7). We can “seek earnestly” (D&C 46:8) the
spiritual gifts enumerated in various places in scripture, including the
seventh article of faith (Articles of Faith 1:7). We can even “covet” these,
Paul said (see 
1 Corinthians 12:31). Paul told the citizens of Athens that “they should seek
the Lord, if haply they might feel after him, and find him, though he be not
far from every one of us” (Acts 17:27). We can seek for “the blessings of the
fathers, . . . the right [to be] ordained,” or receive our
“appointment unto the Priesthood” (Abraham 1:2, 4). We can “seek
. . . out of the best books words of wisdom; seek learning, even by
study and also by faith” (D&C 88:118). We can leave the ninety and nine to
seek the lost sheep (Matthew 18:12). We may “seek that [we] may excel to the
edifying of the church” (1 Corinthians 14:12). We can seek “the interest of
[our] neighbor” (D&C 82:19). We can seek the “power of the Holy Ghost” (1
Nephi 10:17). We can seek “forgiveness, with real intent” (Moroni 6:8). We can
“seek to bring forth and establish the cause of Zion” (D&C 11:6). We can
certainly “seek this Jesus of whom the prophets and apostles have written”
(Ether 12:41).

In fact, the
most common thing we are told to seek in the scriptures is to know the Lord and
his wisdom. “And seek the face of the Lord always” (D&C 101:38). But
nowhere are we told we ought to seek riches; in several of the preceding
scriptures the next verse strictly forbids the seeking of them. The Lord showed
this dramatically when speaking to the Saints trying to establish Zion in
Jackson County, Missouri. “Now, I, the Lord, am not well pleased with the
inhabitants of Zion, . . . they . . . seek not earnestly
the riches of eternity, but their eyes are full of greediness. These things
ought not to be, and must be done away from among them” (D&C
68:31–32). True, the woman in the parable sweeps her house seeking a lost
coin, but this is given to emphasize how much more important it is to seek the
prodigal or the lost soul.

Remember it
is God’s greatest desire to give us truth. It was to the poorer Zoramites,
“esteemed by their brethren as dross,” those who had been cast out because “of
the coarseness of their apparel” (Alma 32:2–3) that Alma turned to teach
one of the greatest scriptural discourses of the Book of Mormon. They could not
pray from the top of the Rameumptom, but they learned how to plant the seed of
the word so they could in time feast on the fruit of the tree of life. They
were schooled on how to gain truth, not the means to exchange their coarse
apparel for “fine-twined linen.”

Notice how
specifically the following verse is worded which describes the Nephites who were
caught up in the pursuit of material gain: “Satan had great power,
. . . puffing them [the people] up with pride, tempting them to
seek for power, and
authority, and riches, and the vain things of the world” (3 Nephi 6:15;
emphasis added). The temptation was not to use the riches inappropriately or to
obtain them dishonestly, or to kill or rob to get them. The temptation was
simply to seek them. Having them brings its own temptations, as we have
convincingly seen in the totality of the scriptural record, but it is a
temptation just to seek them. Benjamin even warned the poor of his own people
not to “covet that which ye have not received” (Mosiah 4:25).

“YE SUPPOSE YE ARE BETTER THAN THEY”

“The hand of
providence hath smiled upon you most pleasingly, that you have obtained many
riches; and because some of you have obtained more abundantly than that of your brethren
ye are lifted up in the pride of your hearts, and wear stiff necks and high
heads . . . because ye suppose that ye are better than they” (Jacob 2:13; emphasis
added).

I must admit,
this verse has been and continues to be for me one of the most powerful
scriptures dealing with our topic. I have pondered upon it again and again as I
examine my own life. It has applications beyond just riches. Pride is surely
one of the most 
difficult of all vices to eliminate from our lives. I have met very few people
who have accomplished this most desirable of victories. I certainly have not
achieved it, and I am sure evidence of it can be discovered in this very book,
though not intentionally placed there. Every difference between any two people
is a temptation to pride, to evaluate those dissimilarities in terms of better
or worse. The relationship of the words “more” and “better” in Jacob 2:13 is
deeply relevant. The quickest path to assuming superiority is having more of
some perceived desirable thing or quality than another. We can always find
differences with others; those disparities can easily lead to comparisons;
those comparisons lead to an assumption of better or best—and
therein pride is born and grows. It is such a human thing to seek
self-approval and to let that approval compete with that of
others.

Perhaps an
anecdote will be helpful in illustrating the problem. When I taught a class at
Brigham Young University several years ago, we discussed the “more-better
quandary” as presented by Jacob. We decided to conduct a little experiment with
pride. I asked the students to keep a few cards in their pockets or a small
notebook and write down every time a difference in some aspect of their lives
was a prompting to pride. I agreed I would do the same. In the next class
session after the week was over, we would share what we discovered about
ourselves. When we met again, everyone was a bit chagrined that the invitation
to give in to the more-better quandary was so very prevalent in their
day-to-day affairs both in opportunity and intensity. Here are
some of the areas they brought up; some are obviously very common,
especially in a college environment: The car you drive—“sporty” holding 
the highest place on the continuum; the apartment complex or dorm you live in
(some housing at BYU carried greater status or sophistication than others); the
clothing styles you wear or the physical appearance and attractiveness with
which you were born. How “good looking” you were was a particularly
difficult topic to bring up, especially for the girls, because it smacked of
vanity as well as pride, but the students were being totally honest with
themselves and each other. Body weight and image were brought up. The degree
you were pursuing ranked high in the pride category. Engineering or
pre-med or pre-law rated quite a bit higher than elementary
education, for example. Your home state was also classified in terms of higher
or lower in the urbanity (or the opposite) it offered. Californians admitted a
particular challenge in this area. Since I too hailed from California, I could
appreciate this temptation. Other temptations to pride were mentioned, but
these are sufficient to make the point.

As fate would
have it, that very week my uncle was moving to southern California, and his
very high-end clothing store was going out of business. He could not
sell a particular overcoat and would have no need of it in Palm Springs. He
gave it to me as a gift. It was extremely expensive, costing hundreds of
dollars. It was a beautiful light tan, New York pinstriped, fully lined,
bone-button camel hair coat. I put it on and walked across campus
looking at everyone wearing less-exclusive overcoats, old
missionary- style black London Fogs. I had to laugh at myself for
giving in so easily to the feelings that coat produced within me. I went home,
hung it in the closet, and put on my old one. I told my wife that I didn’t
think I had the humility to wear that coat, that it would drag me straight to
hell. To this day, my family still jokingly calls it
“the-going-to-hell” coat. I thought of giving it to Deseret
Industries, but I hesitated every time. “I wouldn’t want to tempt some poor
innocent person,” I told my wife when I hung it back in the closet. “Sure,” she
replied with a smile. I will probably never master New York pinstripes and bone
buttons.

GOPHERS IN THE BACKYARD

As a boy in
California, I had to deal with gophers in the backyard. No matter what we tried
to do to get rid of them, there never seemed to be an end to them. They made
such an eyesore of the lawn. I set traps, flooded their holes, and used
poisoned bait.  In my youthful optimism, I sometimes would sit quietly behind
a hole with a bat or stick waiting for a gopher to surface so I could have a
whack at it. But they were too sly and too quick. Maybe, I thought, if I
worried them enough with all my efforts, they would give up and move to the
neighbor’s lawn, where the energy to eradicate them was less enthusiastic than
mine. I never succeeded in persuading them to relocate, but our lawn would have
looked like a minefield if I had ceased my efforts.

Pride is like
a gopher in the backyard of our souls. It has a hundred different holes, and it
can pop up anywhere and at any time. “You are better because                           
,” it says. We can fill in the blank with the thousands of opportunities
that present themselves throughout our lives. Our only recourse is to beat it
down every time we see it.

I tried to do
this during the week of our experiment at BYU. I knocked every proud thought
out of my head. At the end of the week, I noticed they were coming less
frequently. I stood there with my bat surveying the backyard of my soul,
waiting for the gopher to peek out again and tempt me. He stayed underground,
and I found myself thinking, “By golly, I’ve conquered pride! There aren’t many
people who can conquer pride! I must be better than almost everyone else!” And
there, sitting on my bat, was the 
gopher, laughing at me for being proud of my humility. As we search for a way
through the needle’s eye we’ll have to keep a sharp eye out for the gopher. He
is persistent and has hundreds of holes from which to pop up unexpectedly.

Let us say we
can resist the temptation to build greater. We are not allowing wealth to build
gulfs and barriers, and we are not continually shopping for the dainties. We
are giving to the poor wisely and doing all we can to build up Zion. All of our
church obligations of tithes and offerings are current. We are not in debt.
Indeed, we have everything we need for passage through the needle’s eye. Yet we
still should pause and ask ourselves, can we handle the pride that might come
if we are successful in obtaining more of the world’s offerings? Can we handle
the temptation to think we are better because we have more?

OBTAINING A HOPE IN CHRIST

“Think of
your brethren like unto yourselves,” Jacob counsels, “and be familiar with all
and free with your substance, that they may be rich like unto you” (Jacob
2:17). Jacob takes the pride out of the more-better quandary with this advice.
I assume the “all” we are to be familiar with means other people regardless of
their status or station. “Freely ye have received, freely give,” Jesus said
(Matthew 10:8). This refers to all areas of life, not just the spiritual to
which the context points but also the temporal. “Ye were proud in your hearts,”
Jacob continued, “of the things which God hath given you” (Jacob 2:20). Jacob’s
call is not a call to poverty. In its purest sense it is a call for a
refocusing of energy, just as the rich young ruler’s call was one of
redirection. Jacob’s call is for the unity that comes from our familiarity with
all and our lifting of others “that they may be rich” also. A caution is needed
here that takes us back to the idea of proper dominion and stewardship as we
seek to raise other countries and peoples that they may be “like unto you.”

When I
discuss with my classes the probability of one being both worthy and wealthy,
rich and righteous, members of the class almost always quote Jacob
2:18–19, which states: “But before ye seek for riches, seek ye for the
kingdom of God. And after ye have obtained a hope in Christ ye shall obtain
riches, if ye seek them; and ye will seek them for the intent to do
good—to clothe the naked, and to feed the hungry, and to
liberate the captive, and administer relief to the sick and the afflicted.” Is
prophetic or godly permission being given here to seek riches? At first glance
it may appear to be so, but it is surrounded with directional advice. 

Number 1: We
must first seek the kingdom of God. Does “first” here mean chronologically or
as a priority? I think it is the latter. I do not think it means we get our
testimony, become grounded in our faith, find the Lord’s
kingdom—and now we can get on with the business of moving ahead
in the world. I think it means that at all times and in all circumstances and
in all periods of life the kingdom of God is first. It is the priority. Seeking
wealth will never replace it.

Number 2: We
aren’t even to consider seeking riches until we have obtained a hope in Christ.
How would we know when we have obtained it? There are two other places in the
book of Jacob where he speaks of obtaining a hope. We can go there for
clarification as to Jacob’s meaning. “We search the prophets, and we have many
revelations and the spirit of prophecy; and having all these witnesses we
obtain a hope, and our faith becometh unshaken” (Jacob 4:6; emphasis added).
Apparently to obtain a hope in Christ means one has searched scriptures,
received revelation, is filled with the spirit of prophecy, and has developed,
by virtue of so many witnesses, an unshakable faith. Jacob later adds to these
necessities the following words. “Wherefore, beloved brethren, be reconciled
unto him [God] through the atonement of Christ, his Only Begotten Son, and ye
may obtain a resurrection, according to the power of the resurrection which is
in Christ, and be presented as the first-fruits of Christ unto God,
having faith, and 
obtained a good hope of glory
in him” (Jacob 4:11; emphasis added). Do I have a calm assurance that a
celestial resurrection awaits me? Have I obtained that level of hope? Jacob
would say to us: “Why would you seek anything else until you had that
anticipation?”

Number 3: If
we look closely at Jacob’s words the individual is not in reality seeking
wealth. They are seeking to do good, which is entirely permissible. The desire
is not directed to the 
procurement of wealth but to the good it can do. There are many definitions and
ideas of what good can be done by riches. Jacob gives us a few examples. They
are the standard scriptural components of well-spent wealth. They
focus on the needy, the poor, the afflicted, in other words, the very people we
want to be rich like us or the very people to whom the rich felt superior
because they had “obtained more abundantly.” Notice the position of the word
“obtained” and how it is used by Jacob in both the “hope” and the “riches”
areas of life. Are we seeking to obtain the hope or the riches?

Let us assume
we are, in truth, seeking wealth for the good it can do for others. We must
still consider the danger seen earlier in the Book of
Mormon—that of holding true in the long term. There is the
error of stoicism to consider. This error consists in the belief that we can do
always what we can
do sometimes. The 
concern that our purely motivated quest to do good may become warped over time
should be ever present.

Jacob blesses
us with one last thought to keep in mind as we pass through the needle’s eye.
He reminds us that the creator of all flesh is not partial. “The one being is
as precious in his sight as the other. And all flesh is of the dust; and for
the selfsame end hath he created them, that they should keep his commandments
and glorify him forever” (Jacob 2:21). Can we remember that God does not think
in more-better terms? All are equally precious. All are pointed toward the same
finish line and that line is not an economic one. We are here to learn to keep
commandments and glorify God, not ourselves.

RACIAL OR CULTURAL AGGRAVATIONS

The problems
that Jacob strives to solve with his own people are aggravated by a racial and
cultural element. This is directed towards the Lamanites. Jacob does not mince
words. He tells the proud of his people that they “hate” the Lamanites “because
of their filthiness.” He commands them to “revile no more against them because
of the darkness of their skins; neither shall ye revile against them because of
their filthiness” (Jacob 3:5, 9). This is more than the animosity created by
Laman’s and Lemuel’s conflict with Nephi in the earlier generation. This has to
do with lifestyle, culture, and skin color. The Lamanites are described as
“dwelling in tents, and wandering about in the wilderness with a short skin
girdle about their loins and their heads shaven. . . . And many of
them did eat nothing save it was raw meat” (Enos 1:20). It is not hard to
imagine the cultured Nephites in their costly 
apparel speaking in rather disparaging tones about the poor, nomadic,
uncivilized state of their distant kin. Economic pride is so much more serious
in its ability to wound or alienate when racial or cultural assessments are attached.
This is frequently done, however, and will block as effectively as anything the
tiny passage through the needle’s eye.

When so
tempted, we should remember the words of another Book of Mormon prophet, King
Benjamin. “For behold, are we not all beggars? Do we not all depend upon the
same Being, even God, for all the substance which we have, for both food and
raiment, and for gold, and for silver, and for all the riches which we have of
every kind?” (Mosiah 4:19). A self-imposed assessment of our dependency
on God gives us a refreshing view of our own vulnerability and places us as
partakers of the common lot of man, regardless of race or station.
