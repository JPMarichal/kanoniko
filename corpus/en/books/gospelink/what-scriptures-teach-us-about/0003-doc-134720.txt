# Reception into Everlasting Habitations

### Reception into Everlasting Habitations
S. Michael Wilcox, What Scriptures Teach Us about Prosperity (Salt Lake City 2010)
 
 

Reception into Everlasting Habitations

“THE UNJUST STEWARD”

In Luke 16,
the Savior relates two parables which are interconnected and which follow
naturally the parable of the rich fool. There the focus was on which treasure
we were building and where we were storing it. Here, in both the parable of the
unjust steward and the parable of Lazarus and the rich man, a shift is made
from treasure in
heaven to friends
in heaven.

The steward
of “a certain rich man . . . was accused . . . that he had
wasted his goods” (Luke 16:1). When asked to prepare an accounting before being
dismissed, the steward took prompt action to secure his future. He can’t “dig”
(perform physical labor) and is ashamed “to beg.” “I am resolved what to do,
that, when I am put out of the stewardship, they may receive me into their
houses” (Luke 16:3–4). He quickly changed the amounts owed to his master,
reducing the master’s borrowers’ debt load so they would take care of him after
his discharge. What is problematic about this parable is in its ending, “And
the lord [the steward’s master] commended the unjust steward, because he had
done wisely: for the children of this world are in their generation wiser than
the children of light” (Luke 16:8).

Part of the
awkwardness of this ending is in the translation of “wisely.” It is more
properly rendered as “prudently,” “sensibly,” or “practically.” The master was
commending not his steward’s dishonesty but his foresight. He knew he needed
future friends, and he used his position to ensure he had them. Christ’s own
comments indicate the need for the “children of light” to be as equally focused
on the future as the “children of this world” are. But the friends they need to
make are heavenly ones. Yet sadly, most people are more concerned about their
future financial security than their future spiritual security. The Savior
turns the message of the parable to our priorities.

There is a
similar theme running through the Lord’s comments in Malachi. Here Jehovah is
displeased with the poor 
service and sacrifices of his people. “Offer it now unto thy governor; will he
be pleased with thee, or accept thy person?” (Malachi 1:8). We might apply this
question to ourselves by asking, for instance, “If I worked at my profession as
I do my Church calling, would I get a raise or be fired?” “If the Lord were my
employer [which he is], how much of a bonus would I receive?”

The Savior
does not conclude his lessons on the unjust steward with this single thought.
He expands it to include some very needful reflections, “And I say unto you,
Make to yourselves friends of the mammon of unrighteousness; that, when ye
fail, they may receive you into everlasting habitations” (Luke 16:9). “The mammon
of unrighteousness” is an expression meaning wealth. The phrase does not mean
wealth acquired unrighteously, but refers instead to its deceitfulness in
promising fulfillment which proves hollow. We can recast the sentence to grasp
the Savior’s meaning, “Use your wealth in proper ways to make angelic friends
that, when you die, will receive you in heaven.”

The parable,
then, is designed to show us the correct use of wealth. We should use what the
Lord has blessed us with in Christian, or charitable, ways to help others and
build the Lord’s kingdom. In this manner worldly wealth can advance our future
interests instead of hindering them. We must, as children of light, be as wise
as the children of this world. God will give us in abundance that we may do this.

The Savior,
in this parable, also places wealth in its true position as something
inconsequential—the least. “He that is faithful in that which is least is faithful
also in much: and he that is unjust in the least is unjust also in much. If
therefore ye have not been faithful in the unrighteous mammon, who will commit
to your trust the true riches?” (Luke 16:10–11). As we will see in other
scriptures, the true riches have to do with knowledge, revelation, spiritual
gifts, and in doing well.

In case we
think we can satisfy both demands, that of the kingdom of heaven and the
fulfillment of our own worldly desires which wealth can satisfy, the Savior
cautions, “No servant can serve two masters” (Luke 16:13). The two masters may
not be obvious. But with a little thought we come to realize that if one is the
Lord, the other is us. The other master is not specifically mammon. We can use wealth as the rich
fool used it, or we can use it the way the Lord hopes we will use
it—in this case, by making friends in heaven.

WHO’S WHO IN HEAVEN?

Which leads
us to the second parable in Luke 16—the rich man and Lazarus. In this
parable we are introduced to one of the friends we would like to have in heaven
and how to make his acquaintance both before and after we get there. The
parable of the rich man and Lazarus is one of the most skillfully crafted of
all the Savior’s stories. It requires some delicate reading to catch the
nuances that carry so much of the message. It is addressed to “the Pharisees
also, who were covetous,” who had listened to the previous parable and “derided
him” (Luke 16:14). As is human nature, if, like the Pharisees, you don’t like
the message because it strikes a little too close to home, then deride the
messenger. It’s a handy means of deflection.

The parable
begins with an introduction of the two leading characters, “There was a certain
rich man, which was clothed in purple and fine linen, and fared sumptuously
every day: And there was a certain beggar named Lazarus, which was laid at his
gate, full of sores, and desiring to be fed with the crumbs which fell from the
rich man’s table” (Luke 16:19–21).

The first
thing to observe in this parable is that one of the men receives a name and the
other does not. I think the Savior did this deliberately. We know the name of
the beggar but not that of the rich man. This is a reversal of what we would
expect. In our world, it is usually the wealthy and successful whose names are
readily identifiable, while the faceless, nameless poor are massed together in
obscurity. We sometimes refer to them as “the underprivileged,” “the homeless,”
“the poor.” But they all have names, and Jesus acknowledges this truth by
telling us the beggar’s name: Lazarus. He has an identity in the parable, not
the man clothed in purple (purple was a very expensive dye to make and obtain,
and therefore purple clothing was worn by very few). If we are to pass through
the needle’s eye, we must become cognizant of the truth that every person has
identity and worth.

In Isaiah’s
message to Tyre—the Wall Street of the ancient
world—he indicated the tendency of men to overvalue the
affluent because they have become successful at making money. He called Tyre
“the crowning city” (meaning the leading or ruling one), “whose merchants are princes,
whose traffickers are the honourable of the earth” (Isaiah 23:8). “Honorable”
can also be rendered “famous.” Without much thought we could produce a list of
today’s equivalent of Isaiah’s “princes.” Indeed, there are magazines that
dedicate whole issues to this topic. Wealth, if we are not careful, may lead us
to overvalue some individuals while undervaluing others. The astronomically
high compensation of many CEOs and celebrities is sufficient evidence for this
approach to the business of living.

Jesus’ theme
of humanizing the beggar while deflating the importance of the rich man
continues in his description of their deaths. Reading the next verse with the
right tone and emphasis is crucial. Notice the juxtaposition of the respective
phrases—two phrases for each man: “And it came to pass, that
the beggar died, and was carried by the angels into Abraham’s bosom” (read in
an elevated, almost poetic tone). “The rich man also died, and was buried”
(Luke 16:22). The second phrase should be read curtly, without emphasis or
emotion, and in a matter-of-fact tone.

As we saw
with the use of the main characters’ names, once again Christ reverses the
normal perception of value. It is the poor who are usually unceremoniously
buried in “potter’s fields,” or mass graves. The deed is done brusquely and
forgotten. The rich are entombed with solemn splendor, almost pageantry,
erecting marble mausoleums and monuments to mark their departure. We are to
remember them even after their deaths—their passage on earth being
so notable.

The rich man,
for his failure to help Lazarus (as indicated by Lazarus’s desiring what fell
from the table where the dogs “licked his sores”), ends “in torments.” As
anyone who owns one knows, dogs are perpetual visitors under the dinner table,
where they expectantly wait for something to drop to the floor. Lazarus, as we
have seen, was carried by the angels to Abraham’s bosom. The rich man saw
Lazarus and “cried and said, Father Abraham, have mercy on me, and send
Lazarus, that he may dip the tip of his finger in water, and cool my tongue;
for I am tormented in this flame” (Luke 16:24). The drop of water on the tip of
the finger is compared to the crumbs desired earlier by Lazarus. A complete
reversal of fortunes is implied. Lazarus has friends in heaven; the rich man
does not. Is the friend the rich man now needs God, or Abraham, or Lazarus, or
all three? Certainly all are implied, but the best answer for our purposes is
Lazarus. Had the rich man used just a meager portion of his sumptuous affluence
to alleviate the sufferings of a beggar, as the parable of the unjust steward
suggests he should have, that beggar could have pleaded his cause in
heaven.

“A GREAT GULF FIXED”

There is
another intriguing and rather subtle additional suggestion in this parable. It
is found in Abraham’s answer to the rich man’s request. He would very much like
to oblige, but there is a barrier. “Son, remember that thou in thy lifetime
receivedst thy good things, and likewise Lazarus evil things: but now he is comforted,
and thou art tormented” (Luke 16:25). The tone of Abraham’s comments is not
harsh. This is seen in his addressing the rich man as “son.” He is not saying,
“It serves you right.” This is not an unforgiving judgmental parable. There is
equal sympathy for the rich man in his present plight as for Lazarus when his
comforting companions were the rich man’s dogs. Abraham’s next comment should
be read with an ironic tone.

“And beside
all this, between us and you there is a great gulf fixed: so that they which
would pass from hence to you cannot; neither can they pass to us, that would
come from thence” (Luke 16:26). Before any commentary on this statement, we
must remember that this parable was not given to explain the doctrine of the
hereafter. It is a commentary on wealth. We may be diverted from the irony of
this passage by focusing on the spirit world and the divisions that exist there
between the righteous and the wicked. The purpose of the parable is not to
teach us about that. As suggested earlier, the parables were told by Jesus to
help create an emotional state in us, to feel things which would help us to
become better people. In this case, the emotional state should be sympathy,
which will translate into more generous behavior on our part.

Having said
that, think of what barriers, what fixed gulfs differing levels of wealth
create, and the irony will be readily seen. One need only read, for instance, a
novel by Dickens or Austen to perceive how strictly levels of income dominated
England’s social structure in the Victorian and earlier eras. Class
distinctions were also a continual problem for the Nephites. The downfall of
the reign of peace following the Savior’s visit was precipitated 
because those who were no longer Nephites or Lamanites “began to be divided
into classes” (4 Nephi 1:26). And the destruction just prior to the Savior’s
appearance was preceded by a similar 
situation, “the people began to be distinguished by ranks, according to their
riches and their chances for learning” (3 Nephi 6:12). Even in our own day, the
impediments and obstructions of class distinctions are so wide at times that
“they which would pass from hence to you cannot” (Luke 16:26). This is
augmented by our overvaluing those who wear purple and undervaluing those who desire
only crumbs. For those wishing to make the passage through the needle’s eye,
these great gulfs must be bridged in reality as well as in our
perceptions.

I have been
aware of these gulfs numerous times in my life. Sometimes they are marked
geographically within a city or community. We once lived in a small, mostly
Mormon town where the demarcation was measured by
elevation—those on the river bottoms with older, more modest
homes versus those on the hillsides with newer, more luxurious homes and nice
views. When we first moved to the Salt Lake Valley twenty-five years
ago, I was immediately made aware of a gulf that existed here. When buying our
home, we were assailed with counsel on where to live. There were some rather
strong feelings regarding the east or the west side of the valley. The gulf was
Interstate 15, and as an outsider moving in, I was somewhat surprised at how
wide a gulf the interstate represented in some minds. This particular gulf
really has nothing to do with the value of real estate and making wise
decisions about the future value of a home. Though we should consider those
things when purchasing a home, this has to do with our attitude and the way we
perceive people, particularly those with less income than ourselves.

As stated in
3 Nephi, gulfs can also be created by learning, which has a strong connection
to income. Wide gulfs are equally constructed by profession or vocation. We
speak of blue-collar or white-collar jobs, of trades and
professions, but the fact that most people work equally hard to maintain their
lives may not be valued as much as their position or the prestige that society
has placed on any particular occupation. The best man I ever knew was a janitor
with only a high school diploma. It is sad when gulfs like these are created
within the boundaries of a stake or ward. We must be careful to not let them
enter our thinking, let alone allowing them to become fixed between us.

“THOUGH ONE ROSE FROM THE DEAD”

Jesus adds to
this parable one final powerful reality. The rich man is not without sympathy.
He is learning from his torment to think of others. Because release from his
pains is not presently possible, he asks Abraham to send Lazarus to his five
brothers to warn them of their future situation if they do not change. Surely
an angelic visit from the beggar they used to ignore will turn the tide. (This
reminds us, of course, of Charles Dickens’s famous story of Ebenezer Scrooge
and the visit he receives from his old partner, Jacob Marley. In that tale,
Tiny Tim’s family plays the part of Lazarus.) Abraham’s response to the rich
man testifies to the supremacy of the scriptures as sufficient to get us
through the needle’s eye if we avail ourselves of their wisdom. “Abraham saith
unto him, They have Moses and the prophets; let them hear them. And he said,
Nay, father Abraham: but if one went unto them from the dead, they will repent.
And he said unto him, If they hear not Moses and the prophets, neither will
they be persuaded, though one rose from the dead” (Luke 16:29–31).

This
concluding statement is a double-edged sword. On the one hand, Jesus
is alluding to his own resurrection; on the other, he is suggesting how truly
difficult it is to overcome sumptuous living and purple robes in favor of
giving more than crumbs to beggars. If the authority of Moses and the prophets
cannot persuade a change in priorities, the return of one from the grave will
not accomplish it either. The scriptures remain our most dominant helper in
getting us through the eye of the needle!
