# Slippery Riches and Happiness

### Slippery Riches and Happiness
S. Michael Wilcox, What Scriptures Teach Us about Prosperity (Salt Lake City 2010)
 
 

Slippery Riches and Happiness

FROM THE WALLS OF ZARAHEMLA

Happiness is
a nebulous quality to define, and we all characterize it in myriad ways. There
are numerous places in the Book of Mormon where happiness is described. For
instance, while talking to his son Jacob, Lehi taught, “If there be no
righteousness there be no happiness” (2 Nephi 2:13). The fruit of the tree of
life in Lehi’s dream “was desirable to make one happy” (1 Nephi 8:10). When
recounting a life full of industry, study, and worship, Nephi said of his
people, “We lived after the manner of happiness” (2 Nephi 5:27). Alma assured
his son Corianton that “wickedness never was happiness” (Alma 41:10) and called
the plan of salvation “the great plan of happiness” (Alma 42:8; see also
42:16). We could go on and on. Considering the weight happiness receives in the
Book of Mormon, it is enlightening to compare the preceding verses to what many
other Book of Mormon people perceived as the grantor of happiness. In contrast
to Lehi and Nephi, we have Laman and Lemuel. A major cause of their murmuring
in the wilderness is revealed in the following telling statement: “These many
years we have suffered in the wilderness, which time we might have enjoyed our
possessions and the land of our inheritance; yea, and we might have been happy”
(1 Nephi 17:21). The enjoyment of possessions was the ultimate source of
happiness for these misguided brothers. We know from a statement made by Nephi
that their property “was exceedingly great” (1 Nephi 3:25). Perhaps that is a
major reason for the two older brothers beating Nephi and Sam when they lost
that property to the greedy Laban in an attempt to purchase the plates of
brass.

From the
walls of Zarahemla, Samuel the Lamanite chastened the people for their
preoccupation with wealth and the corruption of their laws, their government,
and their church as a result. Materialism fueled the growth of the Gadianton
robbers. “Ye have sought all the days of your lives for that which ye could not
obtain,” he told them. Their obsessive desire for increased possessions
paralleled the happiness formula of Laman and Lemuel. “Ye have sought for
happiness,” he continued, in a manner “contrary to the nature of that
righteousness which is in our great and Eternal Head” (Helaman 13:38). If the
major source of our happiness is wealth and possessions, what will we do when
they are gone? Samuel used a wonderful word to describe the false perception of
a happiness born of materialism—“slippery.” “The time cometh that
. . . your riches . . . become slippery, that ye cannot
hold them; and in the days of your poverty ye cannot retain them” (Helaman
13:31). It isn’t that they didn’t try to hang onto what they had obtained by
their industry and trade. They went to great lengths to preserve the wealth for
which they had labored. “We have hid up our treasures and they have slipped
away from us, because of the curse of the land. . . . All things are
become slippery, and we cannot hold them” (Helaman 13:35–36).

Slippery is such a fine word when used in this
context. There are so many examples we could turn to about how easily great
fortunes are lost. If we examine any financial crisis, the present one being a
grand illustration, we will see how quickly vast sums of money, investments,
401(k)s, real estate, stock and bond portfolios, and even apparently sound
businesses can slip into bankruptcy, insolvency, and total loss. Like you, I
contemplate where and how I can safely save for my future and that of my
children and grandchildren. As we ponder passing through the needle’s eye,
Samuel’s reminder of the slippery nature of wealth is good to remember. Mormon
also used this term while detailing the problems of his generation (see Mormon
1:18). If our sense of well-being and happiness are too dependent on
what we make, how much we have accumulated, and what we can buy, where will we
turn for joy, for security, for contentment, if these slip away? The loss of
wealth may, ironically, be a blessing from the Lord to give us an opportunity
to refocus our energy, priorities, and life values. Perhaps the slippery riches
of the Nephites were not a punishment from God but an invitation.

Samuel does
not leave us at a loss in our consideration of preserving assets. He reminds us
to consider some of the principles we discussed earlier. Prophesying to the
Nephites about how they will respond when their wealth has slipped away, Samuel
gives an answer for us to keep in mind as well. “O that we had remembered the
Lord our God in the day that he gave us our riches, and then they would not
have become slippery that we should lose them; for behold, our riches are gone
from us” (Helaman 13:33).

Many natural
safeguards are intertwined in the totality of scriptural counsel. They become,
when seen together, a very strong tether for maintaining the fruits of our
labors. Samuel particularly recommends gratitude, but “[remembering] the Lord
our God” could also refer to all the prophetic counsel contained in holy writ.
Quoting the Lord himself, Samuel reveals the way to “hide up . . .
treasure” so that it does not become slippery. “For I will, saith the Lord, that
they shall hide up their treasures unto me; . . . for none hideth up
their treasures unto me save it be the righteous” (Helaman 13:19). If our
genuine heart’s desire is to preserve wealth to further the Lord’s work, we
have his foresighted assistance in making our fiscal decisions. We will also be
less inclined to risk his resources in questionable or unbalanced financial
planning. Even trying to preserve assets as an inheritance for our posterity
may not be sufficient unless those plans are strongly motivated and guided by
spiritual objectives. In other words, we are “hiding up” treasure not for
personal motives but for the Lord’s needs. We might pray, “Lord let me preserve
and increase what I have been given, that thy work may progress as thou wouldst
have it.” In a financial sense this would be praying, “Thy kingdom come. Thy
will be done in earth, as it is in heaven” (Matthew 6:10).

THE FOURTH NEPHI SLIDE

“Surely there
could not be a happier people among all the people who had been created by the
hand of God,” Mormon wrote (4 Nephi 1:16). Though he does not spend a great
deal of space on the plates of gold to tell us about the Nephites during their
years of peace after the visit of Christ, he does give us a few details. “There
were no contentions and disputations among them, and every man did deal justly
one with another” (4 Nephi 1:2)—no lawsuits, wage disagreements, scams,
excessive profits or salaries. “And they had all things common among them;
therefore there were not rich and poor, bond and free, but they were all made
free, and partakers of the heavenly gift” (4 Nephi 1:3). They lived the law of
consecration, which we will examine in a following chapter. There were no
oppressive debts. No one basked in superabundant luxury while others faced demoralizing
poverty. “The love of God . . . did dwell in the hearts of the
people. And there were no envyings, nor strifes . . . nor lyings.
. . . There were no robbers . . . nor any manner of -ites;
but they were in one, the children of Christ” (4 Nephi 1:15–17). Love
motivated their interaction, not greed, fear, or legalisms. They did not buy
Korihor’s theory of social or economic Darwinism that “every man fared in this
life according to the management of the creature; therefore every man prospered
according to his genius, and that every man conquered according to his
strength” (Alma 30:17). They used their genius to bless others. After this
utopian description, Mormon sums everything up with one forceful sentence: “And
how blessed were they!” (4 Nephi 1:18).

There is deep
irony in the second half of 4 Nephi, where we read that the genuine happiness
enjoyed by the Nephites and Lamanites after Christ’s visit was destroyed by a
false happiness brought on by the riches that their peace and unity had
created. “And it came to pass that two hundred years had passed away; and the
second generation had all passed away save it were a few” 
(4 Nephi 1:22). Resistance to the lure of wealth continued through the children
of those who had witnessed the Savior’s appearance. But things would soon
change. We have seen how difficult it is for even one generation to hold true
over the long term. In 4 Nephi we see how difficult it is for one generation to
pass on its immunity to succeeding ones. “The people had multiplied . . .
and . . . they had become exceedingly rich, because of their
prosperity in Christ” (4 Nephi 1:23). We notice again that prosperity in and of
itself is not undesirable, but that prosperity must be anchored in Christ and
his teachings. For two generations the people had accomplished this, but all
was about to change.

“And now, in
this two hundred and first year there began to be among them those who were
lifted up in pride, such as the wearing of costly apparel, and all manner of
fine pearls, and of the fine things of the world” (4 Nephi 1:24). It is
difficult to objectively judge our own pride level. How can we know in the
earliest stages when wealth is beginning to negatively influence our thinking
and actions? In the Book of Mormon it was almost always signaled by the
“wearing of costly apparel.” This is a phrase we read often in the ancient
record. In the first chapters of Alma, when the Church began slipping down the
slide of riches, the change in their clothing was duly noted. Within a few
years they went from “they did not wear costly apparel, yet they were neat and
comely” (Alma 1:27) to “in all these things were they lifted up in the pride of
their eyes, for they began to wear very costly apparel” (Alma 4:6). Alma 1 also
speaks of “good homely cloth” (Alma 1:29). Notice how the word change indicates
a shift in attitude and desire. Earlier the concern focused on “neat and
comely” and “good homely cloth.” Later attention was paid to “costly” and
“fine.” The prominence placed on costly apparel as an early indicator of the
creeping growth of pride and materialism is seen in Jacob 2:13; in the
rejection of the poor Zoramites because of “the coarseness of their apparel”
(Alma 32:2); in Moroni’s assessment of the modern world (Mormon 8:36); in the
desires of Nehor (Alma 1:6); in short in all those assembled in the great and
spacious building where the clothing must be great also. “Their manner of dress
was exceedingly fine” (1 Nephi 8:27).

Costly
apparel seems to be a prime outward sign of an inner intruder. Alma calls our
attention to the Zoramites’ external display. “Behold, O my God, their costly
apparel, and their ringlets, and their bracelets, and their ornaments of gold,
and all their precious things which they are ornamented with” (Alma 31:28).
There is also an element of height involved here. The great and spacious
building “stood as it were in the air, high above the earth” 
(1 Nephi 8:26). Those praying on the Rameumptom are “standing . . .
high above the head,” on a space which “would only admit one person” (Alma
31:13). Isaiah spoke of the “lofty looks of man,” of “the cedars of Lebanon,
for they are high and lifted up” (2 Nephi 12:11, 13). He also castigated the
women of his generation for the “bravery” of their jewelry (2 Nephi 13:18),
clothes, perfumes, and hairstyles, which caused them to be “haughty, and walk
with stretched-forth necks” (2 Nephi 13:16). Bravery suggests a
superficial, self-satisfied feeling of self-worth because
of the right “look.”

COSTLY APPAREL OF THE MODERN WORLD

Where do we
find relevance for ourselves in this costly, fine, high emphasis? While today
some display or flaunt wealth in their clothing, it is normally not the
principal indicator of financial status. Some of us can walk down the street
and not be able to tell by the cut of a man’s suit or the style of a woman’s
dress what social level he belongs to or how much money she has. What then is
the modern equivalent of costly apparel? How can we more finely tune our
insight that we may notice one of the first indicators that we may be
succumbing to the fascination of wealth? 

I have asked
that question in at least a hundred different classes of every age group, and I
never fail to hear the same answer. Automobiles and houses are the “costly
apparel” of our era. Here is where we make our statements—perhaps not
consciously, but the result is often the same. We show wealth, position, and
affluence in the size, location, and décor of our homes and in the model,
accessories, and expense of our cars. Our choices in these two possessions
would be the surest way to examine ourselves to see if, perhaps, we are
beginning to be overwhelmed by the attractiveness of money and may be allowing
pride to settle in as our houseguest. It is worthy of repeating that in the
Book of Mormon, outward displays always signaled the commencement of inner
problems and the start of a downward slide.

Though the
words were not used of homes or automobiles, it is not hard to make a correct
application of what is appropriate in these areas by thinking of acceptable
clothing in the Book of Mormon. We remember Alma said their clothes were neat,
comely, good, and homely. If we borrow two words from the Doctrine and
Covenants we can add “plain” and “beautiful” to our list. “Thou shalt not be
proud in thy heart; let all thy garments be plain, and their beauty the beauty
of the work of thine own hands” (D&C 42:40). President Gordon B. Hinckley’s
counsel that we should purchase “modest” homes comes to mind.1 We
now have a series of adjectives to use when applying these scriptural truths to
our purchase of homes and automobiles. We need not live in a
broken-down shack driving a rusted-out car, but the message
of costly apparel as found in the Book of Mormon must hold a strong position in
our decisions in these areas. We do not pass through the needle’s eye in
“costly apparel,” whether it is fashioned with brick and shingles, wheels and
chrome, or fabric and thread.

“O YE FAIR ONES”

Once the
desire for “the fine things of the world” (4 Nephi 1:24) struck those Book of
Mormon people after Christ’s visit, their decline was quick and predictable.
Their happiness soon dissipated. “And from that time forth they did have their
goods and their substance no more common among them” (4 Nephi 1:25).
Consecration now became a barrier to their desire to possess more and more. The
inevitable breaking of a unified people into classes followed in the wake of
consecration’s demise. “And they began to be divided into classes” (4 Nephi
1:26). Since the teachings of the Church would have tried to counter this
downward spiral it was necessary for those who gave in to have their own
churches which would not condemn their excesses. “And they began to build up
churches unto themselves” (4 Nephi 1:26). 

This pattern
was not new to the Nephites. It is depicted earlier in the breakdown of the
righteous society just a few years previous to the three days of darkness and
destruction. Though all was well from the twenty-second to the
twenty-eighth year after the sign of Christ’s birth, in the
twenty-ninth year everything came apart. The culprit as usual was the
enticement of wealth. “Some were lifted up unto pride and boastings because of
their exceedingly great riches, . . . for there were many merchants
in the land, and also many lawyers, and many officers. And the people began to
be distinguished by ranks, according to their riches and their chances for
learning” (3 Nephi 6:10–12). This was certainly not the first time
educational attainment and wealth had been combined in the production of pride.
As early as Jacob’s time the people were warned that these two elements have a
propensity to nourish each other in creating an inflated sense of one’s worth
and congratulatory self-approval (see 2 Nephi 9:28–30). To use
Jacob’s words, a striving for these two things without due attention to the
things of God marks us as one who “wasteth the days of his probation” (2 Nephi
9:27).

As 4 Nephi
ends with division, war, and secret combinations, we wonder how quickly it all
disappeared. It is insightful, therefore, to read a trenchant comment by Mormon
which indicates a transition had taken place from God’s idea of happiness to
the false one they had created for themselves. As Mormon watched their misery
and suffering, he wrote: “Their sorrowing was not unto repentance, because of
the goodness of God; but . . . because the Lord would not always
suffer them to take happiness in sin” (Mormon 2:13). “O ye fair ones,” Mormon
at last lamented, “How could ye have departed from the ways of the Lord!”
(Mormon 6:17). We know the answer—it lies in a flawed
assessment of what brings happiness. Mormon had earlier concluded that man has
a self-delusional propensity to destroy his own happiness. “We may
see,” he wrote, “at the very time when [God] doth prosper his people, yea, in
the increase of their fields, their flocks and their herds, and in gold, and in
silver, and in all manner of precious things of every kind and art;
. . . in fine, doing all things for the welfare and happiness of his
people; yea, then is the time that they do . . . forget the Lord
their God, . . . yea, and this because of their ease, and their
exceedingly great prosperity” (Helaman 12:2). I think when we read 4 Nephi we
are being shown the manner in which the adversary will be loosed at the culmination
of the thousand-year reign of peace we call the Millennium. Periods
of righteousness seem to perpetually terminate in riches, pride, and the
dissipation of moral strength.

Wise use of
wealth can bring security and a sense of contentment and happiness, but only if
it is kept constantly in check. If we are not careful we may be wealthy in
goods but impoverished in living a joyful life. There are many edifying things
we can do with resources that we could not do if we lacked them. These activities
do generate happiness and in all probability are perfectly acceptable and
approved before God. But other things, such as relationships, service, faith,
continual learning, and charity cannot take the backseat. Wealth, and some of
the comforts and opportunities it can provide, may be a side dish to our
happiness. It must not be the main course. Jesus stated it perfectly when
teaching his disciples not to focus on “treasures upon earth.” To pass through
the needle’s eye will require sharp vision not dulled by the side glint of
coinage with its promise of happiness. “The light of the body is the eye: if
therefore thine eye be single, thy whole body shall be full of light” (Matthew
6:19, 22). In that light genuine and legitimate happiness is discovered and endures.
