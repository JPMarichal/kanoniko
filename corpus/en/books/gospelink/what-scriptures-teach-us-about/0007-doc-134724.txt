# Contentment versus the Dainties

### Contentment versus the Dainties
S. Michael Wilcox, What Scriptures Teach Us about Prosperity (Salt Lake City 2010)
 
 

Contentment versus the Dainties

“GODLINESS WITH CONTENTMENT”

Contentment
is an art that is difficult to master. Yet if we can triumph here, our passage
through the needle’s eye is almost accomplished. This perhaps above all other
things is the secret to slipping through that smallest of all corridors. Paul
mastered it through his grand passion of preaching the gospel to the Gentiles.
He simply found something that gave greater satisfaction than possessions or
the accumulation of wealth and with that fulfillment the demons of greed could
not touch him. If we can learn to focus our hearts and energies on other things
the lure of the material world diminishes.

Paul spoke of
contentment more than any other writer of the scriptures. Others allude to it.
For instance, John the Baptist who, when asked by some of his listeners what
they should do, answered, “Be content with your wages” (Luke 3:14). For Paul, 
however, contentment seemed to be a prime virtue. In his epistle to the Hebrews
he wrote: “Let your conversation [lifestyle or manner of conduct] be without
covetousness; and be content with such things as ye have: for he hath said, I will never leave
thee, nor forsake thee. So that we may boldly say, The Lord is my helper, and I
will not fear what man shall do unto me” (Hebrews 13:5–6; emphasis
added). The contentment Paul speaks of here is more than being happy with what
we have, it is also a peace born of knowing the Lord is aware of our needs,
that they are not excessive, and therefore we have promises of his help. The
context of this is a temporal one. It is equally true of spiritual needs, but
the counsel is given in the framework of covetousness and material
satisfaction.

While writing
to the Philippians from his imprisonment in Rome, Paul turned again to this
familiar theme, applying its calming element to his own life. He had been sent
funds from the Philippian Saints and expressed his gratitude for their care.
Then he wrote: “Not that I speak in respect of want: for I have learned, in
whatsoever state I am, therewith to be content. I know both how to be abased,
and I know how to abound: every where and in all things I am instructed both to
be full and to be hungry, both to abound and to suffer need. I can do all
things through Christ which strengtheneth me” (Philippians 4:11–13).

In this
epistle, as in that to the Hebrews, Paul indicates that his contentment comes
from his faith in the strengthening power of Christ. Life was for learning, and
instruction comes from many quarters. Here, however, Paul speaks of abounding
and being full. He did not espouse an ascetic life. We sense he was grateful
for any abounding times. But when in want or hunger his contentment was not
lessened. In whatever state he found himself, there would always be reason for
joy. This peace was consistently with him because material things did not weigh
heavily on his mind.

The phrase
“not that I speak in respect of want” is translated many ways. “Not that I
complain of want,” the Revised Standard Version renders the passage. “Not that
I am alluding to want,” the New English Bible reads. Today’s English Version
gives us “I am not saying this because I feel neglected.” The New International
Version translates the Greek as “I am not saying this because I am in need.”
Putting these different renderings of Paul’s words together we get a sense of
Paul’s mind. “I’m not complaining or hinting of want. I don’t feel neglected
and I’m not in need. I have learned to find contentment in the circumstances
that come whether I am rich or poor.” Or, as the hymn we often sing teaches,
“In ev’ry condition . . . in poverty’s vale or abounding in wealth.”1

We receive
Paul’s most detailed counsel on material contentment in his personal epistle to
Timothy. Timothy was like a son to Paul and the two epistles directed to him
radiate a fatherly love. Timothy was a church leader so Paul shared some of his
deepest thoughts on the challenges Timothy might face as he worked with the
members—the temptations of wealth being one of them. Apparently
there were in the early church some members “supposing that gain is godliness”
(1 Timothy 6:5). The general assumption on the meaning of this phrase is that
some wanted to use religion as a means of yielding profit. The general context
of this section of the epistle is advice directed toward masters and servants,
or as we would call them today, employers and employees.

This leads
Paul to give his most famous advice concerning wealth: “But godliness with
contentment is great gain” (1 Timothy 6:6). The purpose of religion is to teach
us contentment; herein we find the greatest gain. This goes hand in hand with
the Lord’s injunction that we seek wisdom, not riches. In the good news of the
gospel, in its truths, one discovers the greatest contentment. Paul then
continues: “For we brought nothing into this world, 
and it is certain we can carry nothing out. And having food and raiment let us
be therewith content” (1 Timothy 6:7–8). This 
rings with the spirit of the Lord’s Prayer, “Give us this day our 
daily bread” (Matthew 6:11). This most famous of Christian prayers is
introduced with the Savior’s assurance that “your Father knoweth what things ye
have need of, before ye ask him” (Matthew 6:8). What we need is daily bread,
that which covers the basic human needs of food, clothing, and shelter. Paul
would have us be content with these. Solomon was equally emphatic in his
assertion that “better is the sight of the eyes than the wandering of the
desire” (Ecclesiastes 6:9). Can we learn to be satisfied with what we
have?

“THE LOVE OF MONEY”

Unfortunately,
many are not satisfied with this rudimentary level of life. To these, which
undoubtedly include many of us, Paul turns his attention. “But they that will
be rich fall into temptation and a snare, and into many foolish and hurtful
lusts, which drown men in destruction. . . . For the love of money is
the root of all evil: which while some coveted after, they have erred from the
faith, and pierced themselves through with many sorrows. But thou, O man of
God, flee these things; and follow after righteousness, godliness, faith, love,
patience, meekness. Fight the good fight of faith, lay hold on eternal life” (1
Timothy 6:9–12).

These are
strong words. We must remember they are offered by a man who also “knew how to
abound,” yet had undoubtedly seen the snares, destruction, and sorrow of which
he spoke. He had seen the pursuit of wealth deter good men in the Church from
pursuing that which they could carry out of this world, those faithful
qualities listed by Paul. “A man should go where he won’t be tempted,” says Sir
Thomas More in Robert Bolt’s commanding play A Man for All Seasons.2 

“The love of
money is the root of all evil,” Paul said (1 Timothy 6:10). This love may not
always be manifested in a desire for the things money can buy, though that is
its main theme. There are those who can be content with a simpler lifestyle,
yet pursue wealth for the sheer accumulation of it, or for the sense of power
or security it seems to bestow, or for the stimulating sensation 
of having “made it.” So we are to “fight the good fight of faith” 
(1 Timothy 6:12). The alliteration of the phrase is memorable. There are other
battles that need to be fought that will take all our strength to fight.
Expending energy on battles that often end in disappointment, sorrow, or
destruction—both for the individual seeking riches or for
others who might stand in the way of his or her success—does
not lead to eternal life.

Paul then
leaves his topic to give Timothy some final parting words. Before ending the
epistle, however, he returns to the theme of contentment in the riches of God.
It is almost as if he felt he needed to stress his point once again. “Charge
them that are rich in this world that they be not high-minded, nor
trust in uncertain riches, but in the living God, who giveth us richly all
things to enjoy;
. . . that they be rich in good works, ready to distribute, willing to
communicate; laying up in store for themselves a good foundation against the
time to come, that they may lay hold on eternal life” (1 Timothy 6:17–19;
emphasis added).

Life is to be
enjoyed—richly
enjoyed, to use Paul’s specific word. If there is one theme in the scriptures
that is repeated over and over again it is the truth of that idea. Passage
through the needle’s eye will depend on whether we seek uncertain riches or
allow our Father in Heaven to give us those things he knows we can enjoy
without harm to ourselves and others. This usually means spiritual goods, but
it may and often does include more than the “food and raiment” spoken of by
Paul. The line of demarcation is thin between seeking those things and allowing
the Lord to grant them as he sees we are able to receive them. In the meantime,
let us be content lest, to use a Book of Mormon phrase, we “set [our] hearts
upon riches” (Alma 7:6).

“DOING SERVICE, AS TO THE LORD”

Paul also
taught that we should find contentment in our occupations, livelihoods, or
jobs. He lived in a world that tolerated slavery and demeaning labor. Both
masters and servants were accepting the teachings of the gospel. In light of
this, what counsel could he give to both classes? His words would, over time,
change the perception of work and create what we call the Puritan or American
work ethic:

“Servants, be
obedient to them that are your masters according to the flesh . . . ,
in singleness of your heart, as unto Christ; not with eyeservice, as
menpleasers; but as the servants of Christ, doing the will of God from the
heart; with good will doing service, as to the Lord, and not to men: knowing
that whatsoever good thing any man doeth, the same shall he receive of the
Lord, whether he be bond or free” (Ephesians 6:5–8). Similar advice was
given to the Colossians (see Colossians 3:22–24).

Though
toiling for our daily bread by the sweat of our brow has been an injunction
upon man from the time of Adam, there have been periods of the earth’s history
where labor was demeaned. During much of European history, for instance, the
upper classes did not work. If one had to labor it was an indication of his
lower status in society. The laboring man was looked down upon. During the
Reformation, however, John Calvin in particular struck upon Paul’s counsel to
elevate work. All work was work done for Christ; therefore, one should find
contentment in it and take pride in doing one’s best. God would reward you.
Solomon also repeatedly said in Ecclesiastes that a man should “enjoy good in his
labour” (Ecclesiastes 2:24). Work, then, had a spiritual dimension to it. We
see the effects of Paul’s emphasis in the modern work ethic. In today’s view
many who do not work are viewed negatively, but this attitude was not always
so. It is the exact reverse of many centuries of thought.

There was a
downside to this change. A subtle assumption crept into men’s thinking: The
reward for labor is normally given in financial assets; if we are working for
God and he rewards us, then those who have more financial resources may be more
pleasing to God, and those who have less may not be laboring satisfactorily.
Hence a judgmental attitude towards the poor began to creep into modern
thought, as well as an elevated appraisal of financial success. We must be
careful when evaluating these subtleties. For example, sometimes we use the
word “blessed,” a religious expression directly tied to God, when talking about

material prosperity. God does bless us in this manner, but the connection to
our deservedness may not always be as strong as we believe. Remember,
scripturally speaking, wealth is usually a temptation, or, at best, something
to be indifferent towards in our seeking of more desirable objectives. If there
is a blessing in wealth, it is in freeing up our time to concentrate on more
significant 
matters or in our opportunity to bless others in kind. Paul taught that a main
motive for working hard was found in the increased ability that added resources
give us to lift others and practice brotherly love: “Ye yourselves are taught
of God to love one another. . . . We beseech you, brethren, that ye
increase more and more; and that ye study to be quiet, and to do your own
business, and to work with your own hands, as we commanded you; that ye may
walk honestly toward them that are without, and that ye may have lack of
nothing” (1 Thessalonians 4:9–12).

Paul also
spoke to the masters. If the servants were to serve readily, as if working for
God himself, then the masters were to “give unto your servants that which is
just and equal; knowing that ye also have a Master in heaven” (Colossians 4:1).
In other words, be the same kind of Master as Christ is. Paul knew that the
pursuit of wealth often led to unjust and unequal treatment of the laborer.
This has not changed, as myriad examples can be found in the world today. To
the Ephesians, Paul gave a similar caution. “Masters, . . . [forbear]
threatening: knowing that your Master also is in heaven; neither is there
respect of persons with him” (Ephesians 6:9). The key for both laborer and
master, employer and employee, management and worker, white-collar
and blue-collar, stockholder and pipefitter lies in attaching their
individual attitudes about preeminence or profit not to each other but to God.
Thus, whether one is the master and one the servant, their efforts and behavior
toward the other would tell an observer a great deal about their attitude
toward the Lord.

DESIRE FOR THE DAINTIES

Leaving
Paul’s teachings for the moment, let us turn to those of John as found in the book
of Revelation. In the later chapters of this epistle, John describes three
great influences the adversary uses to hold sway over the affairs of men: the
political, the religious, and the economic. He rules with power, apostasy, and
money. Governments and the politics that shape them, differing religions, and
varying economic strategies are not inherently evil, but under the influence of
Lucifer they become twisted. John gives these three to us using symbolic
imagery. A predatory beast represents the political influence; for governments
and nations almost always use the law of the jungle when they build their
empires—the great devour the small. A harlot is the symbol for
the religious influence; for in their apostasy and man-made
doctrines, religions turn against their true bridegroom and husband, the Lord,
in favor of uniting with the powers of men. Thus in Revelation the harlot rides
the beast. The economic influence is represented by a great merchant city, a
trading center which draws in wares from all over the globe, a market of
nations. It is to this last symbol we now turn.

The main
appeal of the merchant city lies in what we might call the three Ds. Within the
walls of the city, “the merchants of the earth are waxed rich through the
abundance of her delicacies”
(Revelation 18:3; emphasis added). John uses several words to suggest the
luxuries of the city, “delicacies” being one of them. Later we read, “How much
she hath glorified herself, and lived deliciously” (Revelation 18:7; emphasis added). The
word “delicious” in this context means wantonly, in excess, wastefully. “All
things which were dainty and
goodly” (Revelation 18:14; emphasis added) are available in her markets. Above
all, her “costliness” is her defining feature (Revelation 18:19). What the city
offers are delicate, delicious dainties, which by their very nature are costly.
All three words suggest luxuries, as opposed to Paul’s “food and raiment,” with
which we are to be content. The great city’s success is based on the continual
production and consumption of what people do not really need, but are convinced
they do by clever advertising that appeals to the buyer’s vanity, or virility,
or femininity, or their longing for perpetual youth, and on and on and on.

Profit is the
essential motive that drives the great city onward. Since the pursuit of
delicious, dainty, costly delicacies seems to be eternal, the great merchant
city believes that her rule over the hearts, labors, and ambitions of men will
continue indefinitely: “I sit a queen, and am no widow, and shall see no
sorrow” (Revelation 18:7). These words are echoes of those put into the mouth
of “the lady of kingdoms” (Isaiah 47:5). “I shall be a lady for ever” (Isaiah
47:7). They come in the midst of chapters where the Lord repeatedly tells his
straying people, “I am God, and there is none else; I am God, and there is none
like me” (Isaiah 46:9), but the merchant city claims the same loyalties. “You
can have faith in me,” she claims. “I am consistent and constant. I will always
be here.” I am reminded sometimes of how often I have been told about the stock
market’s reliability over the long term. While it undoubtedly is
reliable—and I would not suggest we cease investing for future
retirement—it is the tone of near veneration I hear in its
defenders that gives me a slight twinge of unease.

So the
merchant city challenges the God of heaven to a duel for the hearts of men:
“Therefore hear now this, thou that art given to pleasures, that dwellest
carelessly, that sayest in thine heart, I am, and none else beside me; I shall
not sit as a widow, neither shall I know the loss of children” (Isaiah 47:8;
see also Zephaniah 2:15). The city’s children and husband are those who have
been deceived by “the multitude of [her] sorceries, and . . . the
great abundance of [her] enchantments” (Isaiah 47:9). There is nothing else
quite like wealth, with all that it can buy, to bewitch and mesmerize the human
soul. Thus the affections of men, their energy and focus—that
belong to God—are redirected to his chief rival. 

Sometimes we
take too much pride in the ability to make money or to possess the dainties
that are ever available in the markets of the great city. “Thy wisdom and thy
knowledge, it hath perverted thee,” the Lord tells the city, “and thou hast
said in thine heart, I am, and none else beside me” (Isaiah 47:10).

“AND SOULS OF MEN”

In the Old
Testament the great city is associated with the island of Tyre. Tyre was the
great seaport and marketplace of the ancient world. Located off the coast of
Lebanon, Tyre sent her ships throughout the Mediterranean, from Spain to Egypt.
Caravans of trade goods from inland cities were bought and sold in the markets
of Tyre. Ezekiel gives us a list of Tyre’s goods, which is duplicated by John
in Revelation with one sobering addition. These wares come from dozens of
different nations. All come to buy and sell and exchange their goods. Everyone
is there. Everything is for sale. The list of dainties includes silver, iron,
tin, lead, brass, slaves, horses, ivory, ebony, emeralds, purple and
embroidered work, fine linen, coral, agate, wheat, honey, oil, balm, wine,
white wool, spices, precious clothes for chariots, lambs, rams, goats, gems,
and cedar wood. Even the trading ships have oaken oars, embroidered sails,
ivory benches, and masts of cedar. Luxury is described down to the finest
detail (see Ezekiel 27).

Business is
good, profits are high, and everyone is happy, especially the governments whose
subjects are contented with their ease and assets and where taxes are rolling
in. “Thou filledst many people; thou didst enrich the kings of the earth with
the multitude of thy riches and of thy merchandise” (Ezekiel 27:33). Pulling
his inspiration from the writings of Isaiah and Ezekiel, John draws up his own
catalog of dainties. “The merchandise of gold, and silver, and precious stones,
and of pearls, and fine linen, and purple, and silk, and scarlet, and all
thyine wood, and all manner vessels of ivory, and all manner vessels of most
precious wood, and of brass, and iron, and marble, and cinnamon, and odours,
and ointments, and frankincense, and wine, and oil, and fine flour, and wheat,
and beasts, and sheep, and horses, and chariots, and slaves, and souls of
men” (Revelation
18:12–13; emphasis added).

Everything is
for sale in the city, even the human soul, and life is cheap. You can have
anything you want if you have enough money. Perhaps the greatest tragedy lies
in the knowledge that a soul can be offered only by the one who possesses it.
Notice how the list contains almost exclusively luxury items, not the necessary
things which sustain life and can do so comfortably. The Lord is not against
prosperity. He has said that the resources of the earth are for the use of man.
It is the opulence, the superfluity, the waste, the dishonesty, the disregard
of the poor, the inequalities of the city that make its wealth so
distasteful.

The grand
dilemma is that there is no end to the list. That is the message of both
Ezekiel’s and John’s extended enumeration of products. Solomon also discovered
this truth when he taught: “All the labour of man is for his mouth, and yet the
appetite is not filled” (Ecclesiastes 6:7). When is too much too much? How much
is enough? Will one ever be satisfied? The appetite grows with its own increased
consumption. The great city knows it cannot really offer true contentment, at
least not the quality of Zion. The only way she can keep the adoration of the
world is to continue to offer them new pleasures, new comforts, new dainties,
none of which have the ability to sustain happiness. False contentment will in
time wear off; then, lest the newly dissatisfied person realize the grand
deception, the great city displays the new product line, as delicious, delicate
dainties never seen before now dazzle the eyes. The enchantment can continue
only as long as new offerings are available in the city’s markets. The old must
therefore go out of fashion or wear out and be replaced by the newest model
with improved features.

IVORY CHOPSTICKS

A desire for
dainties can become almost addictive. One leads to another in a never-ending
spiral in which each new delicacy demands another for company. There is a story
told in China about the last emperor of the Shang Dynasty, which fell around
1000 b.c. Emperor Zhou wanted
ivory chopsticks. One of his ministers, a man named Jizi, made a prophetic
observation. Ivory chopsticks would not be used with earthen bowls; those made
from rhinoceros horn and jade would be required. Ivory and jade dinnerware
would certainly be filled with only the finest of wines and most rare of meats.
One who ate such food would not be content to wear clothes made of rough
material but of silk dyed in beautiful colors. A thatched cottage would not be
appropriate for such elegance; a palace would be obligatory. Jizi then
remarked, “I am fearful for his end. That is why the beginnings of lavish
living fill me with apprehension.”3 From time to time it might be
advisable to ask ourselves if we are buying ivory chopsticks. In China, the
question has become a proverb.

I believe the
Lord is not against our possession of some dainties—a few ivory
chopsticks—but the question is: Will we know when to stop? We
all have our little luxuries, and we need to be wise and careful in refraining
from judging each other in these matters. For some it might be an occasional
meal in a nice restaurant, for others a comfortable automobile, or tasteful
furniture, or travel, or clothing. We could design a list of our own based on
our modern world equal to the lists of Ezekiel and John. What items we put on
that list would not be the critical factor. Our main lesson lies in realizing
that we cannot have all the dainties—for they never end. There
must be a point when we say, “This is sufficient for my needs and contentment.”
Or, as Socrates once observed, “How many things there are that I do not want.”4
A proper understanding of our need to exercise responsible dominion will help
us. Producing and discarding all the ever-changing,
ever-arriving new dainties places greater and greater strains on the
natural resources of the world. We will explore this restraint in another
chapter.

Acquisitiveness
breeds discontent; indeed, it creates discontent in an effort to then satisfy
the very hunger it has produced. Much of modern advertising is directed to this
strategy. Depending on the product, marketers are in the business of promoting
a discontent which can only find satisfaction in new possessions. A too finely
tuned desire for the dainties may cause us to lose even the necessities of life
as feast turns to famine in the continual round of economic boom and bust. In
our desire for more, in the need to satisfy a created hunger we may generate
our own famine. The human soul was not designed to be run on the consumption of
dainties. Ultimately, if we make them our standard diet, we may starve in the
midst of plenty.

In
Revelation, as in Isaiah and Ezekiel, the great merchant city eventually falls,
brought down by its own excesses and disregard for more eternal truths and
values. “Alas, alas, that great city, that was clothed in fine linen, and
purple, and scarlet, and decked with gold, and precious stones, and pearls! For
in one hour so great riches is come to nought” (Revelation 18:16–17).
This is not the cry of repentance. It is not a cry of sorrow that they valued
the world’s wares more than their God. It is not a cry of agony that their
fellow men meant less to them than the latest costly dainty, or the profits
generated by their production and sale. It is a lament for the city herself. It
is a cry of grief that the list cannot go on forever, getting larger and larger
with each passing season. They still have not learned what has real worth.
Their source of happiness has vanished, and in their blindness they cannot
visualize any other world than the old one. They can find no answer to their
pitiable question, “What city is like unto this great city?” (Revelation
18:18). The answer is obvious: Zion will outshine the merchant city like the
sun outshines a distant star. Within Zion’s borders true contentment will be
enthroned in human hearts, and the lesser gratifications of the “great city”
will be forgotten.

NO GREATER MISFORTUNE

John tells us
that “the merchants of the earth shall weep and mourn over her; for no man
buyeth their merchandise any more” (Revelation 18:11). This passage can be
interpreted two ways. One—the whole structure has collapsed. It
is the ultimate crash; the buying and selling of delicacies ceases. “Who hath
taken this counsel against Tyre, the crowning city,” writes Isaiah, “whose
merchants are princes, whose traffickers are the honourable of the earth? The
Lord of hosts hath purposed it, to stain the pride of all glory, and to bring
into contempt all the honourable of the earth” (Isaiah 23:8–9). Honourable, as used in Isaiah, means successful,
influential, eminent, prosperous, admired for their ability to increase
profits. Consumption drove the economy. It mattered little what was consumed.
When I hear the continual cries that the consumer must buy and buy in order to
save failing or struggling economies, I feel a certain unease. Where will the
consuming economy end? How can it find the point of contentment when continual
growth depends upon greater and greater consumption fueled more and more by
borrowing? For in the merchant city money itself is for sale.

The other
reason for no one purchasing dainties anymore lies in the ultimate triumph of
Zion. Both Zion and the merchant city promise happiness. Both invite us to
bring our time, talents, and treasures to them and build them up. As the people
finally recognize the virtues of Zion and the soul-filling
contentment she offers—seeking to establish her
righteousness—the wares once longed for in the merchant city no
longer have their allure. The shopping spree is over; the dainty, delicate,
costly goods are no longer craved. The spirit of consecration, which we will
examine later, has humbled the power of possession.

Desire for
the dainties may be one of the greatest obstacles to our passage through the
needle’s eye. John indicates at the end of his prophecy about the merchant city
that her “merchants were the great men of the earth; for by thy sorceries were
all nations deceived” (Revelation 18:23). This is not an exclusively American
or even a “First World” problem. It is endemic for 
the human race, though America has given in to the sorcery the most. With this
in mind I have found it interesting to read 
the Chinese sages on the topic covered in this chapter. I think there is not a
people, over the centuries, who have learned the art of contentment quite like
the Chinese, although the westernization of the globe is wearing at their
resistance to such compulsions. In the words of one prominent scholar of
Chinese culture: “Like other human beings, they have sometimes been guilty of
greed and lust and overweening ambition. But most of them have shown an unusual
talent for happiness, even in the midst of poverty and suffering. They have
been able to find joy in things that many of us overlook. . . . The
Chinese have seen some things—and some things that we have
missed—with particular clarity, and the things they have said
about them are often helpful.”5 I therefore think it appropriate to
end this chapter with a passage from one of their greatest scriptural texts:

There is no disaster greater than
not being content;

There is no misfortune greater than
being covetous:

Hence in being content, one will
always have enough.6
