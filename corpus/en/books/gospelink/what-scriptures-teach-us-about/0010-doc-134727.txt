# Wisdom in Giving

### Wisdom in Giving
S. Michael Wilcox, What Scriptures Teach Us about Prosperity (Salt Lake City 2010)
 
 

Wisdom in Giving

GIVE AS WE ARE ABLE

One of the
decisions most of us struggle with deals with giving—how much
and to whom? The scriptures speak often of two types of giving. We give to the
poor, the widows and fatherless being particularly mentioned in the sacred
texts, or to organizations whose causes we value; and we give to God and his
kingdom in the form of tithes and offerings. Wisdom is a critical factor in
sharing that with which the Lord has blessed us. Giving alone is not
necessarily a virtue; although it may demonstrate an unselfish character,
wisdom in the gift is equally necessary. The scriptures indicate that we are
displaying good judgment and wisdom when we bestow our offerings to God because
of the promises that are rendered in return. We will look at some of those
promises. It would be foolish to disregard the Lord’s assurances in an effort
to manage our own financial affairs without taking into consideration his
words. Wisdom also needs to be demonstrated when giving to others, including
the poor. Moses taught, “Every man shall give as he is able, according to the
blessing of the Lord thy God which he hath given thee” (Deuteronomy 16:17).
Determining what we are able to give and how to do so is cause for serious
thought. What principles can help us in this area?

“BY THE SWEAT OF THY FACE”

Work has been
a commandment of God from the Garden of Eden. After the Fall, Adam was told he
would earn his bread by the sweat of his brow and that there would be
opposition to his labors, represented by thorns and thistles. All jobs have
their share of thorns and thistles unique to their specific labor. It is,
therefore, a fundamental principle of the gospel that we should work, and this
should be taken into consideration when helping others. God supplied the manna,
but the children of Israel were required to gather it daily. Under Old
Testament law, it was expected that the poor be adequately paid for their toil,
just as it was expected they would do something for what they received.

The law of
Moses allowed the poor to enter a field and gather food to sustain life. “When
thou comest into thy neighbour’s vineyard, then thou mayest eat grapes thy fill
at thine own pleasure; but thou shalt not put any in thy vessel. When thou
comest into the standing corn [grain] of thy neighbour, then thou mayest pluck
the ears with thine hand; but thou shalt not move a sickle unto thy neighbour’s
standing corn” (Deuteronomy 23:24–25). An example of this is found in the
New Testament, where we read that “Jesus went on the sabbath day through the
corn; and his disciples were an hungred, and began to pluck the ears of corn,
and to eat” (Matthew 12:1). One’s daily bread was to be supplied, yet there was
an element of labor required. The poor were not to go hungry, but the sweat of
their face was anticipated as part of the process.

Gleaning was
another solution to helping the poor while still honoring the idea of work.
“When thou cuttest down thine harvest in thy field, and hast forgot a sheaf in
the field, thou shalt not go again to fetch it: it shall be for the stranger,
for the fatherless, and for the widow: that the Lord thy God may bless thee in
all the work of thine hands. When thou beatest thine olive tree, thou shalt not
go over the boughs again: it shall be for the stranger, for the fatherless, and
for the widow. When thou gatherest the grapes of thy vineyard, thou shalt not
glean it afterward: it shall be for the stranger, for the fatherless, and for
the widow” (Deuteronomy 24:19–21). The repetition of field, olive
trees, vineyard, and of
stranger, fatherless,
and widow in this
passage gives it emphasis. Ruth and Naomi, being poor, would sustain their
lives by this principle. And Boaz gives us a wonderful example of helping the
poor without violating the requisite need for work that maintains the dignity
of the receiver. “At mealtime,” he told her, “come thou hither, and eat of the
bread, and dip thy morsel in the vinegar. . . . And she did eat, and
was sufficed” (Ruth 2:14). 

Boaz,
however, could help in another way. “Boaz commanded his young men, saying, Let
her glean even among the sheaves, and reproach her not: And let fall also some
of the handfuls of purpose for her, and leave them, that she may glean them,
and rebuke her not. So she gleaned in the field until even, and beat out that
she had gleaned: and it was about an ephah of barley” (Ruth 2:15–17).
Ruth had started gleaning “even from the morning” (Ruth 2:7). She had worked
throughout the long, hot day and gathered an ephah of barley, a poorer grain
than wheat. An ephah is less than a bushel, but that amount represented a good
day’s toil. This was hard work, and Boaz was trying to make it as light as he
could. It is a perfect example of balancing all the principles involved in
giving with wisdom.

Certain
sections of every field were to be left unharvested so the poor could gather
the grain or fruit and sustain their lives. “When ye reap the harvest of your
land, thou shalt not wholly reap the corners of thy field, neither shalt thou
gather the gleanings of thy harvest. And thou shalt not glean thy vineyard,
neither shalt thou gather every grape of thy vineyard; thou shalt leave them
for the poor” (Leviticus 19:9–10; see also 23:22). The
seventh-year fallowing of the land was also a boon for the poor.
Though the property owners were not to harvest, but live upon their savings,
the poor were welcome to the fields. “The seventh year thou shalt let it rest
and lie still; that the poor of thy people may eat” (Exodus 23:11). This was a
welfare system adapted to an agricultural society, but the application of the
principles to a more modern one is not difficult.

“HE TOOK HIM BY THE RIGHT HAND”

I recall a
conversation I had with one of our guides in Central America. She watched the
members of our tour group giving toys and dollars to dozens of children who had
gathered thick and fast when our group approached. The needs of children so
deeply touch our hearts. I was very close to this lovely woman. She watched the
charitable chaos of crowding children and said with a tone of sadness, “Please
don’t make beggars out of my people. Buy something they make, or better still
donate the money to that program I have heard you have established in poorer
countries to help them get educated.” A few questions from me established she
was talking of the Perpetual Education Fund. I don’t know how she knew of this
endeavor, but she was very impressed with it. There are wise ways to help.

Though not
directly related to our discussion, we might pause and consider some of the
miracles of the Savior. He met many people along the lanes and roads of Galilee
and Judea who, for one reason or another, begged for sustenance or for help.
Some were blind, some lame, all were reduced to pleading in the streets. Jesus
restored to them those things that would enable them to leave the streets and
return to a more normal life. He fed the five thousand for the day to relieve
their immediate needs, but when they returned the next morning expecting a
repeat of the miracle, insisting, “Our fathers did eat manna in the desert; as it
is written, He gave them bread from heaven to eat,” Jesus refused (John 6:31).
He tried to lift their sights to higher vistas than another free lunch. “Labour
not for the meat which perisheth, but for that meat which endureth unto
everlasting life, which the Son of man shall give unto you” (John 6:27).

Peter and
John entered the temple, “and a certain man lame from his mother’s womb was
carried, whom they laid daily at the gate of the temple which is called
Beautiful, to ask alms of them that entered into the temple.” When this man saw
the two Apostles, he “asked an alms. And Peter, fastening his eyes upon him
with John, said, Look on us. And he gave heed unto them, expecting to receive
something of them. Then Peter said, Silver and gold have I none; but such as I
have give I thee: In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth rise up and walk. And
he took him by the right hand, and lifted him up” (Acts 3:2–7).
Metaphorically speaking, this is what we want to do. The begging days so long
endured at the gate Beautiful were over. Had Peter given him alms he would have
been happy and his needs would have been met for the day, but Peter gave him
another life. There must be ways economically that we can do this for those in
need both as individuals and countries. We must ask the question, “How can
I take them by the hand and lift them up out of poverty, so they need no longer
sit at the gate to beg?”

“THOU SHALT NOT BE IDLE”

Very few
scriptures lay any condemnation upon the heads of the poor. One of the few we
find deals with wanting too much and being unwilling to work for it. “Wo unto
you poor men . . . whose bellies are not satisfied, and whose hands
are not stayed from laying hold upon other men’s goods, whose eyes are full of
greediness, and who will not labor with your own hands!” (D&C 56:17). Greed
and indolence can be experienced by the poor as well as by the rich. Work is a
key element of our mortal probation, regardless of our socioeconomic class. 

In another
place in the Doctrine and Covenants the Lord enjoined, “Thou shalt not be idle;
for he that is idle shall not eat the bread nor wear the garments of the
laborer” (D&C 42:42). We may be tempted to interpret this verse as a
judgment against the idle poor who want to be taken care of by the laboring
middle class or the wealthy, and we speak disparagingly of what is often called
the “dole.” However, another meaning of this verse is that the idle rich are
not to be supported in their laziness by the laboring poor. Viewing life
through a historical context shows us that it is not usually the poor who are
idle. In several other verses in the Doctrine and Covenants idleness is
mentioned with the same assumptions. The Lord was “not well pleased with the
inhabitants of Zion, for there are idlers among them” (D&C 68:31). This led
to later counsel, “Let every man be diligent in all things. And the idler shall
not have place in the church, except he repent and mend his ways” (D&C
75:29). Work is expected of all classes and economic stations. It is desirable
and necessary. To paraphrase a well-known adage: “Man shall not live
by interest alone.”

Few things
brought on the critical indignation of the prophets more than the unfair
treatment of the poor. “Ye shall not afflict any widow, or fatherless child,”
Moses commanded (Exodus 22:22). “Thou shalt not wrest the judgment of thy poor
in his cause” (Exodus 23:6). “Thou shalt not defraud thy neighbour
. . . the wages of him that is hired shall not abide with thee all
night until the morning” (Leviticus 19:13). Payment for labor was to be prompt
and honest. “Thou shalt not oppress an hired servant that is poor and needy
. . . At his day thou shalt give him his hire, neither shall the sun
go down upon it; for he is poor, and setteth his heart upon it” (Deuteronomy
24:14–15). Even the familiar “Thou shalt not muzzle the ox when he
treadeth out the corn,” applies here (Deuteronomy 25:4). As Paul said later,
assuring us that the Lord meant his people with this proverbial saying, “Doth
God take care for oxen? Or saith he it altogether for our sakes? For our sakes,
no doubt, this is written” (1 Corinthians 9:9–10).

We can best
help the poor by being fair with them; by not taking excessive profits at their
expense; by not feeling our own worth is greater than theirs and therefore
demanding higher and higher compensation. Isaiah lamented, “Ye have eaten up
the vineyard; the spoil of the poor is in your houses” (Isaiah 3:14). Solomon
spoke in a similar vein. “He that oppresseth the poor to increase his riches
. . . shall surely come to want” (Proverbs 22:16).

Amos particularly championed the cause of the poor who 

were
demoralized and marginalized by the extravagant lifestyles of the wealthy.
Present compensation packages for Fortune 500 CEOs may have relevancy here.
“They sold . . . the poor for a pair of shoes” and “pant after the
dust of the earth on the head of the poor” (Amos 2:6–7). Amos’s sarcastic
words refer to the desire for land. They even want the very dust on the head of
the poor. Their treatment of the laborer is necessary that they might have “the
winter house with the summer house; and the houses of ivory . . . and
the great houses” (Amos 3:15). The wives of the rich are equally to blame. To
support their lifestyle they “oppress the poor, which crush the needy” (Amos 4:1).
The wealthy “lie upon beds of ivory, and stretch themselves upon their couches,
and eat the lambs out of the flock, . . . chant to the sound of the
viol, . . . drink wine in bowls, and anoint themselves with the chief
ointments” (Amos 6:4–6). In their eagerness for profits they can’t wait
for the Sabbath or the holy day to end “that we may sell corn . . .
that we may set forth wheat, making the ephah small, and the shekel great
[charging more and more for less and less], . . . that we may buy the
poor for silver, and the needy for a pair of shoes; yea, and sell the refuse of
the wheat” (Amos 8:5–6). Amos sounds like a man who has experienced what
he speaks of. He was probably a poor man. As he says, “I was no prophet
. . . ; but I was an herdman, and a gatherer of sycomore fruit” (Amos
7:14). If we want an idea of the emotions of the poor who feel the weight of a
burdensome upper class, it is to Amos we turn. 

The Old
Testament prophets were consistent in their pleadings for the poor, and this is
seen equally in the New Testament. James wrote, “If a brother or sister be
naked, and destitute of daily food, and one of you say unto them, Depart in
peace, be ye warmed and filled; notwithstanding ye give them not those things
which are needful to the body; what doth it profit?” (James 2:15–16).
Even here the scriptural emphasis is on the basics, on those things “needful to
the body.” It may not be wisdom to give more. James also called our attention
to those “rich men” who “have heaped treasure together” but “the hire of the
labourers who have reaped down your fields . . . is of you kept back” (James
5:1, 3–4).

“AND HAVE NOT CHARITY”

In Paul’s
masterpiece of Christian thought, his definition of charity, he tells us the
attitude we need to cultivate when giving to the poor: “Though I bestow all my
goods to feed the poor . . . and have not charity, it profiteth me
nothing” (1 Corinthians 13:3). The heart is all! Help is to be given with
wisdom. It is to be received with graciousness and willingness to do one’s best,
but love must be at the center of all. Mencius, a Chinese sage of the centuries
before Christ, taught this truth more bluntly when he indicated that feeding a
man without showing him love is to treat him like an animal, and to love him
without showing him respect is to keep him like a pet.1 

This is a
narrow road to walk, but it is well worth the effort 
to both the giver and the receiver. When love is the prime mover, everything
else will fall into place. If the love is true and not 
just indulgent or guilt-induced, wisdom in giving will be
self-evident.

King Benjamin
highlighted the importance of the heart and our intent when he spoke of giving
to the beggar without judgment. We will not say, “The man has brought upon
himself his misery,” but will realize we are all beggars asking God for our
needs. Benjamin then addressed the poor, “Ye who have not and 

yet
have sufficient, that ye remain from day to day; I mean all you who deny the
beggar, because ye have not; I would that ye say in your hearts that: I give not
because I have not, but if I had I would give” (Mosiah 4:17, 24). Concern and
empathy are there even though the means of fulfilling it are not. 

We have all
probably had the experience of being approached by someone on the street asking
for money. There are times because of the situation or the place where giving
may not be the wisest thing to do. We inevitably feel guilty when these moments
come. Even when we do give something, we may feel we should have given or done
more. Guilt in such cases is not constructive. Love and sympathy are the
emotions we want to feel. 

As
Latter-day Saints we are deeply fortunate in our opportunity to give
to the Humanitarian Aid fund when we pay our tithes and offerings. More than
once I have been so grateful for this fund. Each Sabbath, we can donate to the
Church, knowing the money is used to alleviate suffering around the world. We
cannot help everyone in all situations, but we can avoid judging, feel
compassion, and then in wisdom do what we can. That may mean an increased fast
offering or an additional contribution to the Perpetual Education Fund. This is
done not to soothe a perplexed mind or a troubled conscience but to help
someone, somewhere, in the appropriate manner.

When my wife
and I took our youngest son to the highlands of Guatemala, he encountered real
poverty for the first time. We could see how deeply it affected him. It changed
his heart, and ever after he has felt and acted upon his deepest sympathies for
the poor, for the minority. Love and true empathy are essential components
of charity.

THE FEEL OF HUNGER

Perhaps the
feel of hunger is why the Lord in his wisdom has linked fasting with giving to
the poor. It took me a number of years to realize why we donate offerings on
the day we fast. So often I have been reminded by others to have a purpose for
the fast. This is excellent advice. It is healthy to think of a personal reason
for fasting or to fast for the spiritual support of another member, but I think
the greatest reason of all is linked to the donation and needs no other motive.
Hunger is the greatest scourge of poverty. When we fast we feel, in the
slightest of ways, what the poor may feel every moment of every day. Knowing
that this is their experience, we are asked to alleviate it by our contribution.
Our fasting is a monthly encouragement to consider the plight of the poor.

Understanding
this becomes an aid for bishops in administering fast offering funds. Knowing
that alleviating hunger is a prime reason for their donation, bishops will use
the funds wisely. While serving as a bishop and needing to make decisions about
using fast offering funds, I was helped by asking myself a question. If I had
to justify what I did with fast offering money to the very people who gave it,
would the Saints in my ward agree with my use of their 
contribution or would they question my wisdom? This thought sometimes held me
back and sometimes made the giving much easier.

In light of
all the preceding considerations, we should understand that the family stands
in the first place of responsibility regarding helping those in financial need.
One’s family customarily knows best the circumstances and how to address them.
They are less likely to give without anticipating a change in fiscal behavior,
if that is necessary. They usually know how much and in what manner money needs
to be dispensed. They also will understand the abilities of the recipient to
give the desired labor. Paul 
instructed Timothy on this critical point when teaching him how to help the
widows under his jurisdiction. “But if any provide not for his own, and
specially for those of his own house [meaning the immediate family], he hath
denied the faith” (1 Timothy 5:8). Charity would demand no less, and who better
to receive the blessings of our empathy than those we have raised or who have
raised us?

THE WISDOM OF GIVING TO GOD

There is
wisdom in giving to the Lord and to his kingdom, because he promises rich
blessings for our generosity. This is not to suggest that we give in order to
receive, but in the Lord’s economy that is the usual result. We give out of
love and unselfishness, which brings its own rewards, but in addition, God is
gracious. We see this principle come into play with fasting, for example.
Isaiah taught the purpose of fasting as we practice it today as well as showing
us how the Lord will respond to our liberality.

“Is it such a
fast that I have chosen? a day for a man to afflict his soul? is it to bow down
his head as a bulrush, and to spread sackcloth and ashes under him? wilt thou call
this a fast, and an acceptable day to the Lord? Is not this the fast that I
have chosen? . . . to undo the heavy burdens, and to let the
oppressed go free, and that ye break every yoke? Is it not to deal thy bread to
the hungry, and that thou bring the poor that are cast out to thy house? when
thou seest the naked, that thou cover him; and that thou hide not thyself from
thine own flesh?” (Isaiah 58:5–7).

If our
sympathies encircle the needs of the poor, they will be balanced by the Lord’s
own benevolence and generosity towards us. “Then shall thy light break forth as
the morning, and thine health shall spring forth speedily: and thy
righteousness shall go before thee; and the glory of the Lord shall be thy
rereward. Then shalt thou call, and the Lord shall answer; thou shalt cry, and
he shall say, Here I am. . . . And if thou draw out thy soul to the
hungry, and satisfy the afflicted soul; then shall thy light rise in obscurity,
and thy darkness be as the noonday: And the Lord shall guide thee continually,
and satisfy thy soul in drought, and make fat thy bones: and thou shalt be like
a watered garden, and like a spring of water, whose waters fail not” (Isaiah
58:8–11).

In typical
Isaiah fashion the entry is poetic. I am particularly impressed and touched by
the phrase “if thou draw out thy soul to the hungry.” What a powerful image of
what is required in giving—the drawn-out soul, not
just a check drawn on a bank account. It is reminiscent of Solomon’s
description of the virtuous woman who “stretcheth out her hand to the poor;
yea, she reacheth forth her hands to the needy” (Proverbs 31:20). There is a
yearning, a longing to help implied in the words, a unity between the giver and
the receiver because both realize they are part of each other and equally
dependent on God. “The rich and poor meet together: the Lord is the maker of
them all” (Proverbs 22:2). Drawing out the soul, however, is recompensed by the
Lord, who will thereafter “satisfy thy soul . . . like a watered
garden” (Isaiah 58:11).

“PROVE ME NOW HEREWITH”

We also give
to the Lord through tithes. Just as in fasting, we show wisdom in obeying this
law because of the aid we receive from the Lord in return. People sometimes
spend great amounts seeking counsel or attending seminars on investments and
how to maximize their capital but fail to take hold of the promised help
offered by the Lord for the faithful payment of tithing. I do not want to
suggest that if we pay tithing the Lord will facilitate our becoming rich, for
that is a temptation few can handle, but I would suggest that through paying
tithing, inspiration will come and he will help us set the proper limits on our
desires and reach contentment. Contentment—not a full balance
sheet—may be what God pours from the windows of heaven. By its
very nature, tithing helps us attain contentment. There are things we cannot do
or have because of what we give. We learn self-discipline; we educate
our desires. We also gain a powerful partner in piloting our financial boat. We
don’t have to row alone. Malachi pledged as much in the most quoted verses on
tithing in the canon: “Prove me now herewith, saith the Lord of hosts, if I
will not open you the windows of heaven, and pour you out a blessing, that
there shall not be room enough to receive it. And I will rebuke the devourer
for your sakes. . . . And all nations shall call you blessed”
(Malachi 3:10–12).

After five
years as a bishop, during which I was able to visit with members every year at
tithing settlement, I discovered that as a general rule those who were faithful
and consistent over the years in payment of a full tithing and free in their
other offerings were better off financially than those who were not. I noticed
that neither the level of income nor the occupations of the members changed
that foundation observation.

Stories of
the Lord’s ability to bless us are myriad. I will share just one as
illustrative of thousands more. The year I had two children in the mission
field at the same time was going to be a 
difficult financial time for our family. That year I published a book on the
temple titled House of Glory. To
my astonishment it won an award which included a $5,000 grant. The amount
necessary to keep a son or daughter in the mission field was $375 per
month—$4,500 per year. We paid our tithing of $500 on the grant, and then
in January paid the mission expenses of one of our children for the entire
year. The heavens had been opened.

“A BAG WITH HOLES”

The book of
Haggai in the Old Testament is entirely devoted to our current topic—the
wisdom in giving to the Lord’s kingdom. In Haggai’s generation the focus was on
temple building. As the children of Israel returned from Babylonian captivity
to rebuild their temple they endured serious hardships. One thing led to
another and progress on the temple slowed. The people began to justify their
delay by saying, “The time is not come, the time that the Lord’s house should
be built” (Haggai 1:2). Another problem was the root of the matter, and Haggai
laid it plainly before the people’s eyes. “Is it time for you, O ye, to dwell
in your cieled houses, and this house lie waste?” (Haggai 1:4). The people
needed their own homes to be sure, but the word cieled, which means “paneled,” shows they
wanted more than an average abode. Elegant homes were often paneled with cedar
wood. Apparently the people were neglecting the temple to build their own
more-than-comfortable homes. Haggai’s question is tinged with
irony. “Consider your ways,” the Lord offered. “Ye have sown much, and bring in
little; ye eat, but ye have not enough; ye drink, but ye are not filled with
drink; ye clothe you, but there is none warm; and he that earneth wages earneth
wages to put it into a bag with holes. . . . Consider your ways”
(Haggai 1:5–7).

Haggai’s
words are delightful, with a touch of humor presented alongside the very
serious subject he addresses. We all know what it is like to put wages into a
bag with holes. There just doesn’t seem to be enough money to cover all our
needs. So we sit down and consider our ways, looking for a way to sew up those
holes or make the dollars stretch. The Lord offers his own needle and thread to
solve the problem of dwindling cash. “Go up to the mountain, and bring wood
[not cedar for their own paneling], and build the house; and I will take
pleasure in it, and I will be glorified, saith the Lord. Ye looked for much,
and, lo, it came to little; and when ye brought it home, I did blow upon it.
Why? . . . Because of mine house that is waste, and ye run every man
unto his own house” (Haggai 1:8–9).

They are to
put the Lord first; then, as Jesus himself promised, “all these things shall be
added unto you” (Matthew 6:33). The people responded, “and they came and did
work in the house of the Lord of hosts, their God” (Haggai 1:14). As a result
of their changed priorities, the Lord told them they would see new results in
their own private affairs. The drain of their wages through the holes in their
purses was about to close. “Consider now from this day and upward,
. . . from the day that the foundation of the Lord’s temple was laid,
consider it. . . . From this day will I bless you” (Haggai
2:18–19).

Wisdom is
certainly required in making financial decisions to best ensure our security in
the present and future. Good, solid, hard thinking is demanded, made in faith,
not greed or fear. In those deliberations must be added the offer of a
wonderful partner, that of the Lord. We all want the best returns on our
investments. It appears that investing in the Lord’s kingdom brings with it
some wonderful dividends. If we are to pass through the needle’s eye our own
eyes must see the needs of the poor. They must see the wisdom of placing the
Lord first—then we can anticipate the blessings promised by
such prophets as Isaiah, Malachi, and Haggai.
