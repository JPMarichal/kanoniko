# “Through the Eye of a Needle”

### “Through the Eye of a Needle” 
S. Michael Wilcox, What Scriptures Teach Us about Prosperity (Salt Lake City 2010)
 
 

“Through the Eye of a Needle” 

Some of the
most interesting discussions I have had in more than thirty-five
years of teaching have come by asking a few very simple questions: “Can you be
rich and righteous? Can you be worthy and wealthy? Is it possible to be
affluent and not be diverted by it?” Now, I understand that wealth, riches, and
affluence are relative terms, so it might be helpful to begin with a
definition. The best I have come across is one offered by President Spencer W.
Kimball, commenting on Matthew 19:24. He wrote, “We may say that he is rich
whose accumulations are sufficiently great to blind him to his spiritual and
moral obligations and to render him slave instead of master.”1 That
is a fine definition because it avoids income levels or accumulations of goods
and focuses instead on moral and spiritual priorities.

When I ask
those questions, invariably I hear positive responses and they come without
hesitation. “Yes,” they answer, “it is possible to be affluent and not diverted
by it.” Almost every person responding knows someone who is accomplishing this
feat, 
or, if they feel they have reached sufficient levels of income to qualify for a
self-analysis, believe they fall into the successful category. There
is a good deal of head-nodding and general agreement. Whatever doubt
there is usually comes from those who do not feel they fit—or
will ever fit—the category. As the conversation continues,
those who doubt are assured by those who do not that it happens frequently and
that they know many people who have affluence without being diverted by it.
This is true of every age level, from high school and seminary students, to
university and institute students, to working professionals, to retired
seniors. I usually remain silent during these exchanges between class
members.

I then ask
how the scriptures would answer those same questions. Here those who earlier
were eager to respond pause in a dilemma of indecision and vacillate before
offering tentative and reluctant reflections. These answers are not so
confident. They are not quite sure what the scriptures teach on this very
relevant concern. The scriptures answer these musings much differently from the
way my students have answered them. There is very little about wealth in the
standard works that is optimistic in nature. I usually ask the class if they
can cite a single verse that speaks positively about money. In all my years of
teaching, no class has produced a single verse. 

There is,
however, a great deal of warning and counsel. The scriptures invariably answer
our questions by affirming how complicated it is. “Not likely,” they teach.
“Extremely difficult; very rarely done; just shy of impossible!” In truth “it
is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle, than for a rich man to
enter into the kingdom of God” (Matthew 19:24). This may have been a proverb of
the time describing something that was completely
impossible—or, with the changing of one letter (kamelos to kamilos) in the Greek, “camel” becomes
“rope,” which may still seem improbable, if not impossible. One can picture the
frustration of trying to thread a rope through the eye of a needle. I actually
prefer this image, but either way, the meaning cannot be mistaken.2

It is easy to
be judgmental and “bash the rich,” as some students affectionately describe it,
but my purpose in generating these conversations is not to be negative. We want
to be realistic but not in a condemnatory or disapproving way. I remember how
devastated I was a number of years ago when reading a book by a prominent
author on this subject. Waves of guilt and uncertainty swept over me, and this
was at a time in my life when I was still trying to finish my education and
raise four little children. Because I was a young teacher, most would not have
classified my wife and me as belonging to those who needed a lot of introspection
on this matter. Yet the impressions that book made haunted me for years.

 “WE ARE TO DO A NEW THING”

So before we
begin our scriptural journey through the intricate maze, ending in
accomplishing what is nearly impossible, let us pause momentarily to contemplate
a most remarkable prophecy given by the Apostle Melvin J. Ballard in general
conference. I think the timing of his statement is wonderfully appropriate. The
date is April 1929, just a few months before the stock market crash that
propelled America into the Great Depression.

Here are his
words: “We are in the age of self-indulgence. It is not peculiar to
this Church; it is in the world. The spirit of it is rampant everywhere. It
beats upon our shores from all points. It enters into the midst of the people.
It is a deadly siege in an attempt to destroy that which persecution, mob
violence, privation and hardship failed to destroy,—the integrity of this
people. I am not a pessimist either. While I recognize the storms that are
raging against our standards, I am as sure as that I live that the promises of
the Lord will be fulfilled, and that this work shall not fail, nor shall it be
given to another people. I recognize however, with my brethren, that the sorest
trials that have ever come to the Church in any age of the world are the trials
of peace and prosperity. But we are to do a new thing, a thing that never
has before been done—We are to take the Church of Christ not
only through the age of persecution and mob violence, but through the age of
peace and prosperity. For we must learn to endure faithfully even in peace and
prosperity.

“I am not
praying for the return of persecution and poverty; I am praying for peace and
prosperity; but above all things for the strength and power to endure this test.
For it was not the design and the intention of the Lord to have this people
always in suffering in bondage and distress. They shall come to peace and
prosperity, but it is the sorest trial that will come to them.”3 

That is such
a positive prophecy. We are going to succeed as a people, if not always as
individuals. We are going to maneuver that tight passage through the needle’s
eye. But the only way we will succeed in doing this “new thing, a thing that
never has before been done” is by being deeply committed to the scriptural path
of mastering materialism and the desire for money. We must learn the Lord’s
road to contentment. We must find security in faith and faithfulness. Let us
not underestimate the challenge. As Elder Ballard testifies, peace and
prosperity “is the sorest trial” that can come to a people or to an individual.
God is not against prosperity. He said himself that he wants us to “have in
abundance” (D&C 49:19). It is what we do with that abundance that is so
extremely essential in his eyes. Because it is such an important part of our
mortal test, and because so much unhappiness and misery is caused by not using
the resources of the earth properly, it is not at all surprising that the Lord
has filled his sacred books with counsel for us all, no matter what income
level we have or hope to achieve. If we can master his lessons we can not only
anticipate greater security and happiness in our financial endeavors, but also
healthier family relationships; for problems dealing with money are the number
one divider of family unity. This is true of marriages, parent-child
relations, and siblings.

 “JESUS . . . LOVED
HIM”

Before we
begin our scriptural search, it might be helpful to reexamine the Savior’s
conversation with the rich young ruler in Mark 10. I think there is something
we miss in this encounter. Key words are important to a story and we must
determine which ones are most critical for understanding the tone of the
verses. The message is often in the tone. The young ruler’s question was
offered in sincerity, “What shall I do that I may inherit eternal life?” (v.
17). We too ask that question. In response to the Savior’s initial reply that
he keep the commandments, the young man assured him, “Master, all these have I
observed from my youth” 
(v. 20). There is no reason to doubt the veracity of his words. This was not
only a rich young ruler but also a righteous one.

Notice the
next lines: “Then Jesus beholding him loved him, and said unto him, One thing
thou lackest: go thy way, sell whatsoever thou hast, and give to the poor, and
thou shalt have 
treasure in heaven: and come, take up the cross, and follow me” (v. 21). Is the
crucial phrase “one thing thou lackest,” which is the usual emphasis? Or is it
“then Jesus beholding him loved him”? Should we focus on the word “lack,” or
“love”? I think it is the second. This conclusion is somewhat validated by the
Savior’s gracious invitation that the rich young ruler “come . . .
and follow me,” which are the identical words he used to call the fishermen
Peter, Andrew, James, and John to be his disciples.

We tend to be
quite critical of the rich young ruler, but the account in Mark expressly tells
us that “Jesus beholding him loved him.” He saw the sincere, deep, inherent,
good-from-birth righteousness in him. He measured his soul and
saw how far he had truly come. He only lacked one thing. As I assess my own
station, I am fairly convinced I am not yet to the point where the Savior would
even make the request. I still “lack” many other things. This is true for most
of us, I suspect. There are numerous other areas of life we need to attend to
in addition to taking on, perhaps, the most difficult and challenging
temptation of all to overcome—the desire for things, and their corollary: the
near-impossibility of relinquishing them once we have them. So amidst
all the criticism of the young ruler, we have to give him a measure of
admiration for reaching a point in life few of us have yet attained.

When the rich
young ruler turned away in sadness, Jesus made his famous comment about the eye
of a needle. His disciples’ conversation among themselves is an indication of
their own astonishment at the prior interchange, “Who then can be saved?” (Mark
10:26). In other words, who is able to attain that level of righteousness? Who
can give up financial security and the good things of life for a “cross”? Who
can curb the desire for more than “our daily bread” and be content therewith?
“With men it is impossible, but not with God: for with God all things are possible,”
Jesus added (Mark 10:27). So it is not impossible—God knows how
to do it! He knows how to teach his children to do it. The lessons are in his
scriptures. They are the most insightful financial planning manual we can read.
If we are striving to keep all the commandments, as this youth was, our Father
in Heaven will make it possible to conquer this last and greatest enticement.
“All things are possible”; even this!

It is
important we recognize another key word in order to make the nearly unattainable
passage through the needle’s eye which, metaphorically speaking, is our
challenge. “Children,” Jesus said, “how hard is it for them that trust in riches to enter into the kingdom
of God!” (Mark 10:24; emphasis added). “Trust” is the primary word. Why is it
so hard? Because to enter therein we must trust in God
instead—in his promises, counsels, warnings, and comforts, in
his ambitions for us—not in our earning capacity or our
401(k)s. We must trust in him! Yet godly promises may not always be viewed as
substantial as money in the bank. After all, we reason, we must be realistic.
Without going into detail, we can reflect on the strong emphasis on money the
adversary presents during the endowment and how much his manipulation of man
hinges on its power. Yet it is also helpful to realize Jesus’ conversation with
the rich young ruler was not a call to poverty, but an invitation to refocus
his life. He calls upon us all to undergo a similar change of perspective with
the assurance that we shall succeed.
