# Dominion, Debt, and Deuteronomy

### Dominion, Debt, and Deuteronomy 
S. Michael Wilcox, What Scriptures Teach Us about Prosperity (Salt Lake City 2010)
 
 

Dominion, Debt, and Deuteronomy 

RIGHTEOUS DOMINION

Let us now
turn to the Old Testament to see what insight it has to offer. It is the first
and oldest book of scripture. Surely if the problem and proper use of wealth
were a critical factor in our mortal testing period, we should find it
addressed in the writings of the ancient prophets, just as we discovered it in
the teachings of Jesus and the Apostles.

One of the
very first commandments given to Adam and Eve in the Garden of Eden was to “be
fruitful, and multiply, and replenish the earth, and subdue it: and have
dominion over the fish of the sea, and over the fowl of the air, and over every
living thing that moveth upon the earth” (Genesis 1:28). Dominion means “to
care for” and it is implied that it is done in a loving, responsible way. Part
of our test, then, is to see how we will interact with the rest of creation.
How far will we push the natural world in our 
desire for both the necessities and comforts of life and for profit? This issue
has always been around, but more recently it has become a world concern. Here,
as in all things, balance may be difficult to maintain. Yet, there are, as we
might anticipate, a few scriptural admonitions we can keep in mind when making
what we now call environmental decisions which may or may not demand monetary
sacrifices.

The command
to multiply the earth was followed immediately by the command to have dominion.
They are inseparable and will demand tough choices. In the Doctrine and
Covenants the Lord speaks of both injunctions in section 49. “It is lawful that
he should have one wife, and they twain shall be one flesh, and all this that
the earth might answer the end of its creation; and that it might be filled
with the measure of man, according to his creation before the world was made”
(D&C 49:16–17). The purpose of this planet is to provide a home for
man. With this in mind the Lord tells us “that which cometh of the earth, is
ordained for the use of man for food and for raiment, and that he might have in
abundance” (D&C 49:19).

In the next
verses the Lord offers two balancing perspectives to the natural, ecological
reserves of the world. 

First, he
does not wish for gross inequalities. “But it is not given that one man should
possess that which is above another, wherefore the world lieth in sin” (D&C
49:20). For a few individuals or nations to enjoy a luxurious expenditure of
the earth’s resources, while others lack the basic necessities, places the
entire planet in an offensive position in the eyes of God—in a
state of sin, as he says. These are strong words. It may be necessary in
maintaining proper dominion for some of us to decrease our 
expenditures of the earth’s wealth, whether that is in food consumption, energy
use, housing, transportation, or other areas.  

Second, the
Lord does not wish us to waste the bounty he has provided. “And wo be unto man
that sheddeth blood or that wasteth flesh and hath no need” (D&C 49:21).
Though the counsel is directed to the taking of animal life, its application to
broader areas is not difficult to defend. The footnote to this verse takes us
to the Joseph Smith Translation, which indicates that the taking of all life
will demand an accounting before the Lord. “The blood of every beast will I
require at your hands” (JST Genesis 9:11). Apparently, if we do not need to
take life and do, we have wasted it, regardless of how we dispose of the
resource. The Lord tells us to use what we need, for he has given resources to
us freely and in abundance. This has been the standard since the beginning.
Even in the Garden of Eden he said, “Of every tree of the garden thou mayest
freely eat” (Genesis 2:16). But take no more than you truly need, especially
not at the expense of others or of the environment. That is waste. This is why
the world lies in condemnation. This is why there is so much sin and
misery.

I recall a
very painful moment I witnessed while directing a tour in China. Each day we
were provided with three meals. Usually the lunch and dinner meal contained
eight or nine different dishes that were placed on a lazy Susan for all to
share. I can’t remember a time the group at the table ate everything. This was
due both to the amount of food and also its newness or taste. One evening we
had left a great deal of food on the table. It was cleared, placed in the
garbage, and thrown away. That night I looked out the window of our hotel. I
could see the hotel dumpster below my room, and there was a family picking
through the discarded remains of our dinner. I am aware of poverty and have
witnessed its dehumanizing effects, but there was something in this scene that
was uniquely disturbing. I had been troubled by the amount of wasted food at
each meal, but this scene put a personal, human dimension on it that left a raw
sore in my heart, and I understood at a different level what the Lord’s
concerns were in Doctrine and Covenants 49. I am not sure we can totally solve
such inequalities, but just the awareness of them is a beginning and an
invitation to exercise better dominion and to avoid waste as best we can.

The Lord
gives further guidance in Doctrine and Covenants 59 regarding dominion
decisions for nations, businesses, and individuals. Notice again how balance is
displayed in the scriptures. “The fulness of the earth is yours, the beasts of
the field and the fowls of the air, and that which climbeth upon the trees and
walketh upon the earth; yea, and the herb, and the good things which come of
the earth, whether for food or for raiment, or for houses, or for barns, or for
orchards, or for gardens, or for vineyards” (D&C 59:16–17). If
forests need to be harvested for houses, we may do so. If animals need be
killed for food or clothing, it is permissible. If habitats need to be plowed
under for orchards or gardens, we may do so.

Having said
this, the Lord then adds several truths to curb an uninhibited grasping for the
bounty the earth offers. We are to realize that not all aspects of creation are
here for man’s physical comforts or economic profit. There are aesthetic
reasons which deal with the soul and heart of man that must take equal
consideration in dominion decisions. “Yea, all things which come of the earth
. . . are made for the benefit and the use of man, both to please the
eye and to gladden the heart; yea, for food and for 
raiment, for taste and for smell, to strengthen the body and to 
enliven the soul” (D&C 59:18–19). Not everything has a practical,
fiscal value. Some things are here to please the eye, gladden the heart, and
enliven the soul. Notice how each of these phrases—“please the eye,”
“gladden the heart,” “strengthen the body,” and “enliven the soul”—moves
the impact of the natural world deeper into the human psyche. Some creations of
the 
earth are here primarily for their beauty and the effect that 
loveliness has on mankind. We simply cannot see all things through the lens of
how much money something can make for us, or how many jobs it can generate, or
how much we need the 
resource. 

That man
should use the earth for both aesthetic and practical purposes is obvious. God
is pleased with our industry as well as with our appreciation of his creative
masterpiece. We read in Doctrine and Covenants 59:20 that “it pleaseth God that
he hath given all these things unto man; for unto this end were they made to be
used, with judgment, not to excess, neither by extortion.” Knowing the capacity
of man to shun moderation in his intemperate excesses, the Lord concludes his
counsel in this passage with a warning. Judgment is required, extortion is to
be avoided. Extortion
comes from a word meaning to squeeze, to wring, or to obtain something under
duress. There is a limit to what man is to wrest from the earth. Combining
these verses in section 59 with those of section 49, we can conclude that the
best way of using judgment and avoiding excess is to realize that one society
should not have more than another. As individuals, we are not to take more than
we need of the earth’s riches to live in luxury while others live in poverty.
Whatever level of life the earth can sustain universally is the desired
standard. Because I think this so critically necessary and obvious, I repeat:
Whatever level of life the earth can sustain universally is the desired
standard. As a race, we will then have fulfilled the Lord’s command to have
dominion over the earth. Thus the decree to have dominion can be a brake on the
creeping average, or on our desire to pull down our barns and build greater as
described in the Savior’s parable, or on our craving more and more
dainties.

PHARAOH’S DREAMS

The earth
does not always give without measure in a never-ending flow of
bounty. There are times of natural shortages as well as abundance. What we see
in the ecological world we see equally in the financial one. The words bull and bear, recession and recovery come to mind. The Lord is aware of
these swings in the fortunes of his children and, therefore, gave guidance for
these contingencies. We come now to the word savings. This foundation rock of financial
security came early in the scriptural record. We see it in the story of Joseph
and his interpretation of Pharaoh’s dreams:

“There came
up out of the river seven kine, fatfleshed and well favoured; and they fed in a
meadow: And, behold, seven other kine came up after them, poor and very ill
favoured and leanfleshed, . . . And the lean and the ill favoured
kine did eat up the first seven fat kine: And when they had eaten them up, it
could not be known that they had eaten them; but they were still ill favoured,
as at the beginning. So I awoke” (Genesis 41:18–21). This dream was followed
by one of a similar vein involving seven good ears of grain and seven stalks of
grain “withered, thin, and blasted with the east wind” (Genesis 41:23).

Joseph knew
the interpretation and gave it to Pharaoh. “All the plenty shall be forgotten
. . . and . . . shall not be known in the land by reason of
that famine following; for it shall be very grievous” (Genesis 41:30–31).
The dream was doubled to impress on Pharaoh’s mind the certainty of its
fulfillment. It is not in Pharaoh’s dreams that we find the importance of this
Old Testament story but in Joseph’s response to it. Mankind has ever known that
plenty is followed by shortage and vice versa. What we do not seem to grasp is
the need to prepare for the lean years by saving during the good ones. Solomon
taught that even the ants are smarter than man in this regard: “The ants are a
people not strong, yet they prepare their meat in the summer” (Proverbs 30:25).
One does not need to be strong, or adept, in making money but wise in its
management. The ants know that that wisdom consists in savings.

Joseph
understood not only Pharaoh’s dreams, but the solution demanded. “Let Pharaoh
look out a man discreet and wise, and set him over the land of Egypt” (Genesis
41:33). The discreet person, the wise one, understands the need to conserve the
abundance of the good years. Conservation, however, is always challenged by the
desire to load up on superfluities during the good years, as if the cycle of
feast and famine never existed. In good years as well, the belief that somehow
we will be the exception to what man has experienced for thousands of years is
often perpetuated. Pharaoh was wise enough to accept Joseph’s advice, saying,
“Can we find such a one as this is, a man in whom the Spirit of God is?
. . . Forasmuch as God hath shewed thee all this, there is none so
discreet and wise as thou art” (Genesis 41:38–39). We see that an ability
to comprehend and act on that understanding—to conserve resources as a
preparation for the future—shows that a man not only is discreet
and wise but also has the Spirit of God.

Joseph stored
one-fifth of the harvest in the good years. Twenty percent is a high
savings rate and would require a tremendous amount of self-control,
especially in today’s world of quick gratification of
desires. I am not suggesting we save 20 percent of our income, but a
judicious decision should be made regarding the amount we do save and then
sustain or increase that level. Ten percent is a good mark to aim for as a
beginning. Saving would also curb our appetites for building greater and
consuming dainties. It is in accordance with the Spirit of the Lord. It shows
wisdom and discretion. What is interesting is that instead of saving in the
good years, people often spend their entire surplus in building greater and
filling that greater with dainties—and then borrow more until
they are entrenched in debt. The logic is not only deeply ironic but impossibly
counterproductive and flies in the face of reason, yet it seems to be as
perennial as the seasons.

During the
famine years, Joseph’s wisdom saved not only the Egyptian nation but his own
family. In succession the Egyptians bought grain from his supplies first with
their money, then with their cattle, then their land, and finally their future
labors. “Thou hast saved our lives,” they said (Genesis 47:25). Joseph then
required they learn from their experience to be always prepared for a similar
crisis in the future. “And Joseph made it a law over the land of Egypt unto
this day, that Pharaoh should have the fifth part” (Genesis 47:26). The
character of Joseph and the context suggests Joseph is not placing the
Egyptians in bondage but directing their efforts in wisdom. The original
savings rate in the seven good years was one-fifth. Here he makes it
a perpetual law.

SHOULD STORMS HOWL ABOUT YOUR HEADS

In the last
decade we have seen a repeat of this story played out in our own economy. In
the priesthood session of general 
conference October 1998, President Gordon B. Hinckley quoted extensively from the
story of Joseph interpreting Pharaoh’s dreams. He then offered the following
counsel and application:

“Now,
brethren, I want to make it very clear that I am not prophesying, that I am not
predicting years of famine in the future. But I am suggesting that the time has
come to get our houses in order.

“So many of
our people are living on the very edge of their incomes. In fact, some are
living on borrowings.

“We have
witnessed in recent weeks wide and fearsome swings in the markets of the world.
The economy is a fragile thing. A stumble in the economy in Jakarta or Moscow
can immediately affect the entire world. It can eventually reach down to each
of us as individuals. There is a portent of stormy weather ahead to which we
had better give heed. . . .

“I hope we
will never again see such a depression [as the Great Depression he saw as a
young man]. But I am troubled by the huge consumer installment debt which hangs
over the people of the nation, including our own people. . . .

“We are
beguiled by seductive advertising. Television carries the enticing invitation
to borrow up to 125 percent of the value of one’s home. But no mention is made
of interest. . . .

“I recognize
that it may be necessary to borrow to get a home, of course, but let us buy a
home that we can afford and thus ease the payments which will constantly hang
over our heads without mercy or respite for as long as 30 years.

“No one knows
when emergencies will strike. . . .”

President
Hinckley then told of President Faust’s experience of paying off a 4 percent
mortgage on his home even though “many people would have told him he was
foolish to pay off that mortgage when it carried so low a rate of interest. But
the first opportunity he had to acquire some means, he and his wife determined
they would pay off their mortgage. He has been free of debt since that
day. . . .

“This is a
part of the temporal gospel in which we believe. . . . If you have
paid your debts, if you have a reserve, even though it be small, then should
storms howl about your head, you will have 
shelter for your wives and children and peace in your hearts. That’s all I have
to say about it, but I wish to say it with all the emphasis of which I am
capable.”1 

I had a
conversation with a very good friend, a fine member of the Church, who has been
successful in his business ventures. He told me that the night he heard
President Hinckley give that address he could have gone home and written out a
check for the balance of his home mortgage. “I thought of doing it,” he said,
“but decided I would invest it instead because the rate of return would be
higher on the investment. I lost it all! And now if I can keep my home I will
be fortunate.” It was about ten years from President Hinckley’s warning to the
day that the present economic crisis became painfully noticeable in our
economy, but we had seen many good years previously. I hope that every
Latter-day Saint listened and heeded the words of President Hinckley,
and though there may be sacrifices to make and challenges to face, the financial
foundation of our families will remain solid.

I am deeply
grateful for a wise mother who instilled in me at a very young age the need to
save. She was a child of the Great Depression also, and its memory stayed with
her. Starting in my first year of school, she would give me my
quarter-a-week allowance. Tithing was separated, and a nickel
was then placed in a small envelope to be taken to the bank for savings. A bank
book was issued for me to track how much I had. I remember vividly placing those
nickels week by week in the envelope, taking it to school where it was
collected, and marking the addition in my bank book. I can still see the
columns of numbers adding up over the years of my education. I remember the
thrill I felt as the total finally reached one hundred dollars and then one
thousand. There were times I wanted to buy something with my coins, but the
satisfaction of watching the growing balance held me back. When I was married
many years later, we had enough saved to pay for the last two years of college
and graduate without debt, buy our wedding rings, purchase furniture, pay for
our first baby, and buy a piano for my wife to play and use to teach lessons.
How thankful I have been over the years for those nickels placed in my hand by a
prudent and judicious mother.

Balance must
be maintained in the area of savings as it is in all areas of scriptural
application. There is a fine line between saving judiciously and hoarding.
Solomon spoke of hoarding wealth in Ecclesiastes: “There is a sore evil which I
have seen under the sun, namely, riches kept for the owners thereof to their
hurt” (Ecclesiastes 5:13). Moderation provides the beam upon which to balance
the opposing weights of stockpiling wealth or saving for future needs. Often we
hoard not out of a love for money or a desire for luxuries and dainties but out
of the sense of security that money instills. The fear-greed cycle
equally applies here. There is nothing quite as effective in compelling one to
accumulate wealth as the fear of not having anything, the fear of insecurity.
The scriptures certainly teach us to save. There is no question about this, but
they also teach us to share, and how we do that wisely will be examined. Hence
we must live with the paradox and 
ambiguity of saving without amassing, of conserving without heaping up. Only
reliance on the Holy Spirit and a sincere desire to use our resources properly
can help us find that delicate balance without tipping the scale too much to
either side. One thing is certain, however—debt is to be
avoided.

It is easy to
see that the flip side of the coin labeled “savings” is one labeled “debt.”
Interest can work for us or against us. It is an easy financial principle to
grasp. The scriptures, therefore, have quite a bit to say about debt. This
takes us to Deuteronomy and the law of Moses.

SEVEN-YEAR RELEASE AND THE JUBILEE

The law of
Moses encompassed many things. It was, certainly, a religious document dealing
with commandments, ordinances, and memorials. It was also a health law, as well
as a legal code. But we shall see that a fourth element in its wisdom dealt
with financial matters and included a welfare system which addressed itself to
the needs of the poor. It had some very interesting provisions to help people
avoid great loads of debt or to stymie the amassing of vast wealth and power in
the hands of a few at the expense of the many.

For an
economy to run, there must be provisions for lending and borrowing. This was
true of the law of Moses, but there were restrictions: “At the end of every
seven years thou shalt make a release. And this is the manner of the release:
Every creditor that lendeth ought unto his neighbour shall release it; he shall
not exact it of his neighbour, or of his brother; because it is called the Lord’s
release” (Deuteronomy 15:1–2). One can readily see how this would place
certain limits on both the borrower and the lender. Modern credit card lenders
invariably want people to pay only the minimum amount due on their cards, thus
extending the loan (in many cases indefinitely) to maximize profits, but under
the Old Testament system this was not possible. Every seven years the debts
were removed.

A level of
honesty and responsibility was assumed, of course, and the Lord added the
command that the needs of the poor were not to be compromised by the year of
release. “Thou shalt not harden thine heart, nor shut thine hand from thy poor
brother: But thou shalt open thine hand wide unto him, and shalt surely lend
him sufficient for his need” (Deuteronomy 15:7–8). Lending and borrowing
was not to be done for superfluous, unessential things. The phrase “sufficient
for his need” would not have included the dainties. If the loan was centered on
the real needs of the borrower, the seventh year of release was not to close
the hand of the lender. It was essentially the poor who were in need of
borrowing, not the rich. “Beware that there be not a thought in thy wicked
heart, saying, The seventh year, the year of release, is at hand; and thine eye
be evil against thy poor brother, and thou givest him nought. . . .
Thou shalt surely give him, and thine heart shall not be grieved.
. . . For the poor shall never cease out of the land: therefore I
command thee, saying, Thou shalt open thine hand wide unto thy brother, to thy
poor, and to thy needy” (Deuteronomy 15:9–11). We will examine what the
scriptures say about wisely helping the poor presently; at this point I would
only stress the safeguarding limits Moses placed on the amount of debt one
could incur and for whom the provision for lending was generally granted.

In fact, it
was the violation of this seven-year law of release that became the
last straw leading to the Babylonian captivity many generations later. The
Israelites were not practicing the law, but as the crisis brought upon them by
the expanding Babylonian empire threatened them, the wealthier people, the
nobility, covenanted they would apply the seven-year release, which
had not been done in some time. Yet their desire for financial advantage and
control was too great a temptation, and they failed in their resolve. “But
afterward they turned, and caused the servants and the handmaids, whom they had
let go free, to return, and brought them into subjection. . . .
Therefore the word of the Lord came to Jeremiah . . . saying,
. . . Behold, I proclaim a liberty for you, saith the Lord,
. . . I will make you to be removed into all the kingdoms of the
earth” (Jeremiah 34:11–12, 17). Since they would not grant the
seven-year liberty, they would not enjoy liberty themselves. It was
as if the Lord was saying, “If you live by the bondage of others you will be
taken into bondage yourselves.” One can’t help but think of the unmerciful
servant who demanded “an hundred pence” from his neighbor in the Savior’s
parable.

Acceptable
loans were not to carry interest. Once again this would have placed a
constraint on the lending practices of our day. Interest could be charged to a
non-Israelite, but not to a brother. “Thou shalt not lend upon usury
to thy brother; usury of money, usury of victuals, usury of any thing that is
lent upon usury: unto a stranger thou mayest lend upon usury; but unto 
thy brother thou shalt not lend upon usury” (Deuteronomy 23:19–20). This
restriction and the seven-year release went hand in hand with the
injunction, “Thou shalt lend unto many nations, but thou shalt not borrow; and
thou shalt reign over many nations, but they shall not reign over thee”
(Deuteronomy 15:6). It is implied that the reigning refers to financial
control. In other words, don’t let other nations have control over you because
you borrow from them. Maintain your independence. To give up 
control over one’s own destiny either as individuals or a nation will surely
bring unwanted consequences in time. To do this for that which is more than
sufficient for our needs showed a major lack of wisdom. Moses would have been
distressed to see a balance carried from month to month on a
credit card.

There were
other checks or inducements placed on the Israelites to encourage a saving
instead of a borrowing society. Every seven years the land was to lie fallow.
This was good agricultural practice, but it also taught the principle of saving
for the future. “Six years thou shalt sow thy field, and six years thou shalt
prune thy vineyard, and gather in the fruit thereof; but in the seventh year
shall be a sabbath of rest unto the land . . . thou shalt neither sow
thy field, nor prune thy vineyard. That which groweth of its own accord of thy
harvest thou shalt not reap, neither gather the grapes of thy vine undressed:
for it is a year of rest unto the land” (Leviticus 25:3–5). This,
naturally, raised a question. “And if ye shall say, What shall we eat the
seventh year?” (Leviticus 25:20). The Lord’s response mirrored the experience of
the Egyptians during Joseph’s sojourn there. “I will command my blessing upon
you in the sixth year, and it shall bring forth fruit for three years”
(Leviticus 25:21). Here, again, the idea of saving surplus for future needs is
instilled in the lives of the people.

Perpetual
transfer of land was forbidden. “The land shall not be sold for ever: for the
land is mine” (Leviticus 25:23). If circumstances necessitated selling land,
everyone knew that it would not be permanent. Every fiftieth year was the
jubilee year. “And ye shall hallow the fiftieth year, and proclaim liberty
throughout all the land unto all the inhabitants thereof. . . . Ye
shall return every man unto his possession. . . . According to the
number of years after the jubile thou shalt buy of thy neighbour, and according
unto the number of years of the fruits he shall sell unto thee” (Leviticus
25:10, 15). This was essentially a lease agreement system. One could lease the
land for a certain number of years, with both parties understanding that when the
jubilee year came, all land returned to its original ownership. This prevented
the unfortunate losses or foolish decisions of one generation from dooming
succeeding generations to perpetual poverty. It also checked the greed of a few
individuals who desired to control property in order to control
prices—a monopoly, in other words. Isaiah condemned his own
generation for this very approach to maximizing profits. “Woe unto them that
join house to house, that lay field to field, till there be no place, that they
may be placed alone in the midst of the earth!” (Isaiah 5:8). The family of one
who had sold land also had a nonnegotiable right to redeem the land whenever
they chose by buying it back or paying off debt. I am not suggesting we
duplicate Moses’ system, as it would not fit into our much more complex world
economy, but the principles are still relevant and offer much guidance
both for individuals and larger entities.

Avoidance of
debt is an assumed virtue in the Doctrine and Covenants and is, therefore, a
foundation principle of money management from the earliest days of the
Restoration. “Pay the debt thou hast contracted with the printer. Release
thyself from bondage,” the Lord told Martin Harris (D&C 19:35). A short
time later, as the grand dream of building Zion dawned on the Church, the Lord
counseled strongly against debt. “Behold, it is said in my laws, or forbidden,
to get in debt to thine enemies” (D&C 64:27). Since it is impossible to
consecrate what we do not own, in his instructions regarding the law of
consecration the Lord returned to the subject of indebtedness. “And again,
verily I say unto you, concerning your debts—behold it is my
will that you shall pay all your debts” (D&C 104:78).

From Old
Testament times to the Restoration, the scriptures have been consistent. The
Lord recommends, encourages, and warns that we save rather than borrow, that we
conserve rather than consume. It makes perfect sense, but far too often people
practice the exact opposite. Solomon wrote, “The rich ruleth over the poor, and
the borrower is servant to the lender” (Proverbs 22:7). The propensity to
increase debt and cease saving, particularly in a quest for more dainties, is
one of the greatest dangers to passage through the needle’s eye. It is
sometimes very difficult to discern between wants and needs. With the exception
of a very few things, such as a modest home, an education, and perhaps a
practical car, the Lord would undoubtedly tell us that if you cannot pay for it
with cash, with the means you have saved, it is a want, not a need!

I believe I
can provide no more fitting conclusion to this part of our scriptural search
than to turn to the words received by Brigham Young in Doctrine and Covenants
136. This counsel was given to the pioneers as they prepared for their westward
migration, but its relevancy is unarguable. Notice that the Lord chose the
specific language of command when directing the early Saints’ use of the
resources he had freely given them: “Thou shalt be diligent in preserving what
thou hast, that thou mayest be a wise steward; for it is the free gift of the
Lord thy God, and thou art his steward” (D&C 136:27).
