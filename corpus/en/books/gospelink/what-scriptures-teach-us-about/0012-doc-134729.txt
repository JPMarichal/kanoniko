# Of Gratitude and Forgetfulness

### Of Gratitude and Forgetfulness 
S. Michael Wilcox, What Scriptures Teach Us about Prosperity (Salt Lake City 2010)
 
 

Of Gratitude and Forgetfulness 

A BOOK OF MORMON THEMATIC CHORD

As we turn
now to center our search in the pages of the Book of Mormon, one thing becomes immediately
apparent: Almost without exception all the major writers and prophets of
the Book of Mormon have something to say about riches. No matter whom we choose
to study—King Benjamin, Alma the Younger, Nephi II, Samuel the
Lamanite, Moroni—they all dealt with the problems caused by
wealth, and they project that counsel forward to our own times. I have pondered
this thematic chord running throughout the Book of Mormon and wondered why
there is such a strong emphasis. Because the chief compilers of the Book of
Mormon saw our day, we must consider that they viewed things that caused them
to put such a heavy stress on riches. While speaking of this particular
concern, Moroni said, “I speak unto you as if ye were present, and yet ye are
not. But behold, Jesus Christ hath shown you unto me, and I know your doing”
(Mormon 8:35). He then announced, in no uncertain terms, the main ailment of
our times: “Ye do love money” (Mormon 8:37). The conclusion must press itself
upon our minds that the frequent cautions against material gain as found in the
Book of Mormon were not placed there primarily for Saints in impoverished
areas. I assume they were for the primary benefit of Saints who would be
blessed with great abundance. This is to be seen not as undesirable but as an
aid for a specific end. The fiscal stability and affluence of “First World”
Church members has built, and continues to build, the kingdom in all the
nations of the world where it is allowed to progress. The challenge, then, as
stated earlier, is to listen to what the ancient American prophets have to say,
allow it to sink deep into our hearts, that we may continue to be afforded the
financial means to bless less-wealthy parts of the kingdom. We must
realize as Church members that that is the main reason we have been so
prospered.

In Moses’
blessings on the tribes of Israel, he promised the tribe of Joseph they would
be granted “precious things . . . precious fruits . . . the
chief things of the ancient mountains . . . precious things of the
earth and fulness thereof.” These gifts would be essential to the main
responsibility of Joseph’s descendants. They were to “push the people together
to the ends of the earth” (Deuteronomy 33:13–17). The image is one of a
bull or wild ox using his horns to keep his herd together. I have seen animals
such as cattle, deer, or elk do this. The precious things were to be granted to
the tribe of Joseph that they might gather Israel and see that they were
unified and fulfilled.

This was not
the only place Moses taught this truth. In his farewell address to his people,
found in Deuteronomy, he warned against a common occurrence when wealth
abounds. “The Lord thy God bringeth thee into a good land, . . . a
land wherein thou shalt eat bread without scarceness, thou shalt not lack any
thing in it” (Deuteronomy 8:7–9). This is a fairly accurate description
of our own promised land. There is a temptation, however, that seems to always
be attached to such bounty—the temptation of forgetfulness.
“When thou hast eaten and art full, then thou shalt bless the Lord thy God for
the good land which he hath given thee. Beware that thou forget not the Lord
thy God, . . . lest when thou hast eaten and art full, and hast built
goodly houses, and dwelt therein; and when thy herds and thy flocks multiply,
and thy silver and thy gold is multiplied, and all that thou hast is
multiplied; then thine heart be lifted up, and thou forget the Lord thy God”
(Deuteronomy 8:10–14). Moses felt such unease and disquiet over this
tendency that he repeated this caution. Earlier he had said that when you live
in “goodly cities . . . and houses full of all good things,
. . . when thou shalt have eaten and be full; then beware lest thou
forget the Lord” (Deuteronomy 6:10–12).

There in a
nutshell is the problem we see again and again in the Book of Mormon. It is the
challenge of forgetting. This is the specific meaning of Nephi’s warning found
early in the ancient record. “Others will he pacify, and lull them away into
carnal security, that they will say: All is well in Zion; yea Zion prospereth,
all is well” (2 Nephi 28:21). “Pacify” and “lull” are well-chosen
words. They suggest ease and a self-congratulatory satisfaction with
one’s efforts. This verse does not mean we all think we are doing okay in following
the commandments. “Carnal security” is financial security, not
self-righteousness. Zion is prospering materially; therefore, all is
well. As Moses indicated, this attitude can breed a too-independent
attitude—why do we need God when market averages are high and
goods are plenteous?

THE GREAT SLAYER OF THE DEMON OF RICHES

Of necessity
there is an alternative, because as we shall see, it is the Lord’s intention to
bless us that we might bless others and accomplish his work. The counter
course, as Moses points out, is to remember in gratitude. The one great slayer
of the demon of riches, for those who will be granted them, is to remain
grateful, to remember they are gifts of God. Yet there is a subtle falsehood
that may creep into the mind of one so endowed—the thought that
one has, in reality, earned everything by oneself. This thought we must resist.
It will block our passage through the needle’s eye. We earn nothing! All is
given!

Moses made
this plain to his own people as they prepared to enter Canaan. “And thou say in
thine heart, My power and the might of mine hand hath gotten me this wealth.
But thou shalt remember the Lord thy God: for it is he that giveth thee power
to get wealth, that he may establish his covenant which he sware unto thy
fathers, as it is this day” (Deuteronomy
8:17–18; emphasis added). There is our assignment and the reason for our
bounty. Nephi spoke of “all the house of Israel, pointing to the covenant which
should be fulfilled in the latter days; which covenant the Lord made to our
father Abraham, saying: In thy seed shall all the kindreds of the earth be
blessed” (1 Nephi 15:18). So we are blessed that we may bless others, but it is
all a gift to be used as the Lord desires, with the sure knowledge that we did
not earn it. Thinking that we earned it all ourselves leads to pride, the great
spiritual disease of the Nephites. Thus the Book of Mormon becomes a valued
guide to fulfilling our destiny of establishing God’s covenant throughout the
world. With this backdrop, let us see what ancient American prophets have to
say to modern-day Saints.

HOLDING TRUE OVER TIME

At the
beginning of our study we looked at a prophecy by Elder Melvin J. Ballard that
indicated the Latter-day Saints would succeed in handling prosperity and
righteousness. We would learn to maneuver the tight passage through the eye of
the needle. This was something, he stated, which had not been done before. Yet
there are two places in the Book of Mormon where it seems the people were both
wealthy and worthy, rich and righteous. In the first chapter of Alma we read:
“They did impart of their substance, every man according to that which he had,
to the poor, and the needy, and the sick, and the afflicted; and they did not
wear costly apparel. . . . And now, because of the steadiness of the
church they began to be exceedingly rich, having abundance of all things
whatsoever they stood in need—an abundance of flocks and herds,
and fatlings of every kind, and also abundance of grain, and of gold, and of
silver, and of precious things, and abundance of silk and fine-twined
linen, and all manner of good homely cloth. And thus, in their prosperous
circumstances, they did not send away any who were naked, or that were hungry,
or that were athirst, or that were sick, or that had not been nourished; and
they did not set their hearts upon riches; therefore they were liberal to all,
both old and young, both bond and free, both male and female, whether out of
the church or in the church, having no respect to persons as to those who stood
in need.” As a result of their unselfish and charitable attitude, Alma
indicates, “And thus they did prosper and become far more wealthy than those
who did not belong to their church” (Alma 1:27–31).

Notice that
this description specifically speaks of the members of the Church. The needle’s
eye has let them through. This positive portrayal is, it seems, what we want
for the Church today. And true to God’s word that he would prosper them in the
land if they kept the commandments, their success exceeded those not living
such an unusually humble and nonmaterialistic life. The windows of
heaven were opened and God’s graciousness poured out. The date of this rare
achievement is “the second year of the reign of the judges” (Alma 1:23). Let us
see the state of the Church in “the eighth year of the reign of the judges”
(Alma 4:6), just six years later.

“The people
of the church began to wax proud, because of their exceeding riches, and their
fine silks, and their fine-twined linen, and because of their many
flocks and herds, and their gold and their silver, and all manner of precious
things, which they had obtained by their industry; and in all these things were they
lifted up in the pride of their eyes, for they began to wear very costly apparel.
. . . The people of the church began to be lifted up in the pride of
their eyes, and to set their hearts upon riches and upon the vain things of the
world” (Alma 4:6–8; emphasis added).

The two
entries are almost identical. I believe the two descriptive accounts of the
Church were deliberately written to match each other, but what a change took
place in just a few years. One very significant addition is made to the second
listing of their possessions, the phrase “which they had obtained by their
industry.” That is a phrase worthy of additional examination. It suggests that
they thought that they had earned, by their own efforts, all they had acquired.
They were forgetting Moses’ caution, both in the failure to realize it was God
who had given them “power to get wealth” (Deuteronomy 8:18) and in the
assignment to use those things to do good in the kingdom rather than build
themselves up.

There is no
question that people can be blessed with great wealth and follow all the
scriptural counsel on how to manage it through the needle’s eye. The question
we all can ask ourselves is: Can we maintain that over the long term? Can we
hold our purpose true over years, over a lifetime? That is the rarest of rare
accomplishments. I think that is why Elder Ballard indicated: “We are to do a
new thing, a thing that never has before been done—We are to
take the Church of Christ not only through the age of persecution and mob
violence, but through the age of peace and prosperity. For we must learn to
endure faithfully even in peace and prosperity.”1 With the people of
Alma’s time in mind, we grasp the essential meaning of Elder Ballard’s
words—learning to endure faithfully in prosperity.

It was this
very situation that caused Alma to give up the judgment seat and devote his
full time to preaching, sensing he had a better chance of changing the dynamics
of his people’s economic situation through the word of the Lord than by
occupying the highest political position in the land. Because the crux of the
problem was spiritual, not fiscal, the solution was spiritual also. There is
food for thought in Alma’s understanding that has deeply interesting
ramifications for modern matters of financial security. Thus, Alma gave the
members in Zarahemla one of his greatest addresses, and one of the most
significant in the pages of the Book of Mormon, that of Alma 5, wherein he
turned them again toward heavenly goals by asking them a long series of
questions. His 
efforts did not go unrewarded, as he saw these once-devoted 
members leave their pursuit of wealth to again focus on higher things.

Lest we fail
to seize on the importance of the difficulty of holding true in the long term,
Mormon presented another example to drive the point home. At the end of the
book of Alma we read once again of the people of the Church achieving the right
balance in regards to riches. This took place just after the great wars of the
last section of Alma. “And they began to grow exceedingly rich. But
notwithstanding their riches, or their strength, or their prosperity, they were
not lifted up in the pride of their eyes; neither were they slow to remember
the Lord their God; but they did humble themselves exceedingly before him. Yea,
they did remember how great things the Lord had done for them. . . .
And they did pray unto the Lord their God continually, insomuch that the Lord
did bless them, according to his word, so that they did wax strong and prosper
in the land” (Alma 62:48–51).

The key, as
Moses had taught, was to continually remember the Lord. This they were doing.
The date of this entry was “the thirty and fifth year of the reign of the
judges” (Alma 62:52). Could this generation learn from the previous one and
hold true? In the “forty and third year . . . there was
. . . a little pride
which was in the church” (Helaman 3:1; emphasis added). “In the forty and ninth
year . . . there was exceedingly great prosperity in the church”
(Helaman 3:23–24). “In the fifty and first year . . . there was
peace also, save it were the pride
which began to enter into the church—not into the church of
God, but into the hearts of the people who professed to belong to the church of
God—and they were lifted up in pride” (Helaman 3:33–34; emphasis
added). “And it came to pass that [in] the fifty and second year
. . . exceedingly great pride . . . had gotten into the hearts of the
people; and it was because of their exceedingly great riches and their
prosperity in the land; and it did grow upon them from day to day” (Helaman
3:36; emphasis added). 

That last
statement is remarkable. We can see in the entries that precede it the growing
sense of pride. Notice the transitions from “little pride,” to “pride,” to
“lifted up in pride,” to “exceedingly great pride.” We feel the growth. This
was the generation that gave birth to the first secret combinations among the
Nephites. The rest of the book of Helaman deals with the problems that wealth
created as the people eventually “set their hearts upon their riches; yea, they
began to seek to get gain that they might be lifted up one above another”
(Helaman 6:17). They “did turn unto their own ways, and did build up unto
themselves idols of their gold and their silver. And it came to pass that all
these iniquities did come unto them in the space of not many years” (Helaman
6:31–32).

Both Nephi II
and Samuel the Lamanite speak to the people, trying to turn them as
did Alma in the previous time of crisis. They both pleaded with the people that
they avail themselves of the two truths that can keep the hounds of wealth at
bay: remembering that the Lord gave them their prosperity, and
feeling gratitude toward him. Nephi said: “O, how could you have forgotten your
God in the very day that he has delivered you? But behold, it is to get gain,
to be praised of men, yea, and that ye might get gold and silver. And ye have
set your hearts upon the riches and the vain things of this world.
. . . Ye have suffered [pride] to enter your hearts, which has lifted
you up beyond that which is good because of your exceedingly great riches!”
(Helaman 7:20–26).

Samuel
focused on gratitude in his approach. We generally think of Samuel’s message as
being concerned mainly with the signs of the Savior’s birth and death, but a
good portion deals with the heart which is set on riches. “Ye have set your
hearts upon them, and have not hearkened unto the words of him who gave them
unto you. [They are gifts, not earned.] Ye do not remember the Lord your God in
the things with which he hath blessed you, but ye do always remember your
riches, not to thank the Lord your God for them; yea, your hearts are not drawn
out unto the Lord” (Helaman 13:21–22).

Remembering
the Lord, never forgetting he is the giver of all we have, and drawing out our
hearts in gratitude—these are the barriers to the Nephite infection whose
contagion so easily spreads and with such spiritually deadly results. “And thou
shalt rejoice in every good thing which the Lord thy God hath given thee,”
Moses taught (Deuteronomy 26:11). The rejoicing in what we have is always
linked with the recognition that it is a gift from God. Notice that in all
this, it is not the possession of riches that is condemned, but the setting of
our hearts upon them and our failure to recognize their source. There was a
time the Nephites had wealth; it was, in fact, given to them by the Lord, but
their possessions and income were not the center of their existence. They were
not driven to acquire more and more, but were content with what they had and
happy to share and bless others. They were not judgmental in their perceptions
nor did they allow what they had obtained to create barriers and gulfs between
them. “Costly apparel” and “vain things” had no appeal. But in time the thought
of how hard they had labored, the thought of all their “industry,” had its way
and all that is negative in the possession of wealth grew upon them “from day
to day.” We ponder again, as we apply these stories to ourselves, can we hold true over the years? Because
many of us are fortunate in what we have, let us not forget the Lord, but allow
soothing gratitude to always hold our minds true to God’s purposes, and the
thought that for all we have we are indebted to his generosity. I repeat: We
have earned nothing! To think so is far too often the source of pride. To
appreciate—or even to concede—our true situation
will always engender gratitude.

GRATITUDE BORN OF INDEBTEDNESS

King Benjamin
used the same principles to teach his own people, even though they were not
entangled in the same predicaments and crises of wealth that Alma and Helaman
were. Better to arm his people beforehand than try to recover them when the
bite of affluence had already sunk deep. King Benjamin was an example of all he
urged, for wealth was not an enticement he heeded. “I . . . have not
sought gold nor silver nor any manner of riches of you. . . . And
even I, myself, have labored with mine own hands that I might serve you, and
that ye should not be laden with taxes” (Mosiah 2:12–14). Benjamin turned
immediately and pointedly to gratitude. “O how you ought to thank your heavenly
King!” In Benjamin’s mind, we could never do that enough. “If you should render
all the thanks and praise which your whole soul has power to possess, to that
God who has created you, . . . yet ye would be unprofitable servants”
(Mosiah 2:19–21).

Three times
Benjamin used the word “indebted” to describe our relationship with Deity. If
we are grateful, if we remember God and follow his guidance, then we will see
his hand in our lives. In his goodness “he doth immediately bless you; and
therefore he hath paid you. And ye are still indebted unto him, and are, and
will be, forever and ever; therefore, of what have ye to boast?” Certainly not
of having made profits, or accumulated money, or garnered fame, or built up a
corporate empire. The power, or opportunity, or genius, to do
so—when done—was all a gift from God. “And now
I ask, can ye say aught of yourselves? I answer you, Nay” (Mosiah
2:24–25). These are pride-killing truths. We are unprofitable!
We will never balance the ledger! We have done nothing! Have earned nothing!
All is a gift from him! This includes ourselves, for we belong to him who
created us (see Mosiah 2:25). We owe gratitude, and in that gratitude humility
is born. Gratitude and humility are a powerful combination when assailed by the
persuasions of material things. A lack of gratitude and of acknowledging God’s
gifts is a fault and demonstrates a want of understanding. Joseph Smith taught:
“And in nothing doth man offend God . . . save those who confess not
his hand in all things” (D&C 59:21). Benjamin therefore concluded what we
inherently know, and tells us, “ye are eternally indebted to your heavenly
Father, to render to him all that you have and are” (Mosiah 2:34). This
acknowledgment is the very cornerstone of the law of consecration, as we will
see in a later chapter.

“NEITHER COLD NOR HOT”

If forgetting
God because we have all we need does not lead us into the traps repeated over
and over in the Book of Mormon, it might, at best, create a lukewarm commitment
to his work. This was the problem among the Laodiceans when John wrote the
epistle we know as The Revelation of St. John the Divine. At the beginning of
Revelation, the Lord speaks directly to each of the seven churches located in
Asia Minor. They all have different strengths and challenges. In Smyrna they
are dealing with poverty, but in Laodicea the difficulty is just the opposite.
“I know thy works,” the Lord begins, “that thou art neither cold nor hot: I
would thou wert cold or hot. So then because thou art lukewarm, and neither cold
nor hot, I will spue thee out of my mouth” (Revelation 3:15–16). There is
nothing as tasteless as tepid cocoa on a snowy day or warm root beer on a
summer day. The Laodiceans are not against God or his work. They have not
entirely forgotten him. They have not strayed as far afield from God as the
Nephites did; rather, they are largely uninterested, unmoved, and unresponsive.
What has caused this sense of apathy? “Because thou sayest, I am rich, and
increased with goods, and have need of nothing” (Revelation 3:17).

To feel we
have enough and need nothing when that “enough” does not include God or his
more necessary gifts is a tragedy. If total forgetfulness and ingratitude is
not the problem, indifference may be. “I counsel thee to buy of me gold tried
in the fire, that thou mayest be rich,” the Lord says; then adds: “As many as I
love, I rebuke and chasten: be zealous therefore, and repent” (Revelation
3:18–19). Remember, Mark told us that Jesus loved the rich young ruler.
He loved the Laodiceans just as much. Shift your zeal from moneymaking to
searching for a finer treasure. The Savior’s words to the Laodiceans are
followed by one of the most beloved invitations offered by the Savior. “I stand
at the door, and knock: if any man hear my voice, and open the door, I will
come in to him, and will sup with him, and he with me” (Revelation 3:20). We
have already discussed in chapter 3 what supping with the Lord entails. The
context and positioning of this invitation suggest that some of the things that
most frequently cause us not to hear the knocking or the call of the Savior are
the overloud voices of a mercantile world “increased with goods.” The needle’s
eye is traversed with the thinning power of gratitude and a finely tuned memory
set on the Lord—not on riches.
