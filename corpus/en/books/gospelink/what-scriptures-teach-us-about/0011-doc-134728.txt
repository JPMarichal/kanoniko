# The Greatest Good and the Greatest Danger

### The Greatest Good and the Greatest Danger
S. Michael Wilcox, What Scriptures Teach Us about Prosperity (Salt Lake City 2010)
 
 

The Greatest Good and the Greatest Danger

“EVERYTHING BEAUTIFUL IN ITS TIME”

For as long
as the world has been spinning men have been asking themselves certain eternal
questions. One vital question is worded in a number of ways: “How is life to be
lived? Wherein can we find the greatest good? What type of existence brings the
greatest happiness?” The writer of Ecclesiastes tackled this age-old
question in that section of the Old Testament we call the “wisdom literature.”
It is attributed to Solomon or to one who spoke from his perspective. Solomon
felt he had been placed in his position so that he could experience life in many
different varieties. He began with his conclusion: “Vanity of vanities; all is
vanity. What profit hath a man of all his labour which he taketh under the
sun?” (Ecclesiastes 1:2–3). He then shared with us what life had taught
him, assuring us “there is no new thing under the sun” (Ecclesiastes 1:9),
meaning, “Don’t expect something new I haven’t experienced to come along which
will answer this question. I have been through it all. Here is what I learned.”

He tried
“mirth,” “laughter,” and “folly.” He “made . . . great works
. . . houses . . . vineyards . . . gardens
. . . pools of water.” He had “servants . . . great
possessions . . . silver and gold, and the peculiar treasure of kings.”
“I gat me men singers and women singers . . . musical instruments,”
he said. Then in a tone of ironic self-mockery, he gave this
assessment: “So I was great,
and increased more than all that were before me. . . . And whatsoever
mine eyes desired I kept not from them, I withheld not my heart from any joy.
. . . Then I looked on all the works that my hands had wrought, and
on the labour that I had laboured to do: and, behold, all was vanity”
(Ecclesiastes 2:1–11; emphasis added). Though this sounds like a
pessimistic assessment of life, Solomon has advice to offer, and he offers it
again and again in Ecclesiastes.

Let us
remember Solomon was given the gift of wisdom from the Lord. Here are his words
to the world: “There is nothing better for a man, than that he should eat and
drink [the basics], and that he should make his soul enjoy good in his labour.
[Take pleasure in the endeavor itself, not necessarily in what the labor can
purchase.] This also I saw, that it was from the hand of God. . . .
For God giveth to a man that is good in his sight wisdom, and knowledge, and joy”
(Ecclesiastes 2:24–26).

Enjoy the
simple life! Be glad in what God has given you! There is too much striving, too
much ambition. As T. S. Eliot wrote in his poem “Ash Wednesday,” “I no longer
strive to strive towards such things.”1 Realize that knowledge and
“wisdom excelleth folly, as far as light excelleth darkness” (Ecclesiastes
2:13). Seek them! “To every thing there is a season”; accept what comes along
knowing God “hath made every thing beautiful in his time” (Ecclesiastes 3:1,
11). “In the day of prosperity be joyful, but in the day of adversity consider:
God also hath set the one over against the other” (Ecclesiastes 7:14). As life
sweeps by with its continual changes, Solomon testifies: “I know that there is
no good in them, but for a man to rejoice, and to do good in his life.
. . . Wherefore I perceive that there is nothing better, than that a
man should rejoice in his own works; for that is his portion: for who shall
bring him to see what shall be after him?” (Ecclesiastes 3:12, 22). Rejoice in
the journey! Do good! The future will bring what it will bring. As Jesus
testified: “Seek ye first the kingdom of God, and his righteousness; and all
these things shall be added unto you. Take therefore no thought for the morrow:
for the morrow shall take thought for the things of itself. Sufficient unto the
day is the evil thereof” (Matthew 6:33–34). Take pleasure in each day
without constant anxiety about tomorrow. The Lord knows your needs and will
make you sufficient for whatever life brings you.

Solomon
perceived that relationships are important, both those of friends and of
family. “For whom do I labour, and bereave my soul of good? . . . Two
are better than one; because they have a good reward for their labour”
(Ecclesiastes 4:8–9). Sharing time and memories with others makes all
labor worthwhile. “Go thy way, eat thy bread with joy, and drink thy wine with
a merry heart; for God now accepteth thy works. Let thy garments be always
white; and let thy head lack no ointment. [Life cannot be all hardship and
struggle.] Live joyfully with the wife whom thou lovest all the days of the
life of thy vanity . . . for that is thy portion in this life, and in
thy labour. . . . Whatsoever thy hand findeth to do, do it with thy
might” (Ecclesiastes 9:7–10). People, not possessions, bring the joyful
life!

Against the
happiness and fulfillment one can experience in family relationships and in
knowing that God is pleased with our endeavors, wealth brings its own burdens.
“He that loveth silver shall not be satisfied with silver; nor he that loveth
abundance with increase. . . . When goods increase, they are
increased that eat them: and what good is there to the owners thereof, saving
the beholding of them with their eyes? The sleep of a labouring man is sweet,
whether he eat little or much: but the abundance of the rich will not suffer
him to sleep. . . . As he came forth of his mother’s womb, naked
shall he return to go as he came, and shall take nothing of his labour, which
he may carry away in his hand” (Ecclesiastes 5:10–15). Wealth promises
much but rarely delivers. In truth, life may be more burdensome with it than
without it.

We think of
spending money and how much a thing costs in dollars or labor. Solomon would
have us think of time and how we spend our days. We have only so many minutes
and hours and weeks and years. What will we do with them? “Remember now thy
Creator in the days of thy youth,” for the time will come when “the silver cord
[of life] be loosed” (Ecclesiastes 12:1, 6). “Truly the light is sweet, and a
pleasant thing it is for the eyes to behold the sun: But if a man live many
years, and rejoice in them all; yet let him remember the days of darkness”
(Ecclesiastes 11:7–8). Rejoice now lest there be regrets later that there
were too many sunsets we did not appreciate and too many days spent without
knowing that happiness is the purpose of life. Passage through the needle’s eye
need not be a joyless thing.

Chuang Tzu, a
Chinese Taoist of the fourth century before Christ, described it well with this
analogy: “The leaders of this generation, that is to say most of them, throw
away their lives in pursuit of material gain. Isn’t it pathetic!
. . . Now this is like a man who takes the pearl of the Marquis of
Sui [an object of immense value] and shoots a bird in the sky with it, high up
in the air. People would obviously laugh at him. Why is this so? Because he has
used something of great value to obtain something of little value. Now surely
life is even more valuable than the pearl of the Marquis of Sui!”2

The message
of Ecclesiastes is that life will only be futile, will only be vain if we seek
that which cannot endure. Life is meaningful in God; in doing good; in living
and sharing with those we love; in increasing in knowledge; in the simple joys
of food and wholesome, engaging work; and peaceful sleep. Wealth cannot
guarantee this level of contentment. Do we really believe that the man sitting
on his yacht is more fortunate than the peasant sitting in his doorway with a
grandchild on his lap? “For he shall not much remember the days of his life;
because God answereth him in the joy of his heart” (Ecclesiastes 5:20).

“THE STUMBLINGBLOCK”

We must never
underestimate the tempting power of wealth. This is a consistent message of
sacred writ. We are much more likely to pass through the needle’s eye if we
constantly call this message to our own attention. Ezekiel said it best when he
described the fall of the children of Israel. “Their silver and their gold” was
“the stumblingblock of their iniquity” (Ezekiel 7:19). Sometimes when our
children wanted to do something their mother and I felt was not wise or to go
into an environment we deemed spiritually unsafe, they would ask, “Don’t you
trust me?” I would always answer, “No!” I would not, however, leave it at that
blunt answer but always add, “I don’t trust myself! Individuals greater than
either of us have fallen.” Their stories are told in scripture. This is true of
the lure of the world of money. The stories of those who fell are warning
tales, as are most of the scriptures that deal with wealth. Warning may carry
an alienating connotation; therefore, let us view the scriptural emphasis as an
invitation to free ourselves from an enticement that will ultimately destroy
our happiness, for that is the purpose of a warning. A few examples should be
sufficient to keep us on our guard.

The first
great crime of mankind was motivated by a desire for gain coupled with anger
over a rejected sacrifice. Cain, forming the first secret combination, an
organization dedicated to the pursuit of “power and gain” (Ether 8:22),
“gloried in that which he had done, saying: I am free; surely the flocks of my
brother falleth into my hands” (Moses 5:33). Those who indulge in uncontrolled
avarice focused solely on gain feel no responsibility to another as long as
they can get away with it. “Am I my brother’s keeper?” Cain arrogantly asked
(Moses 5:34). The bottom line is all-important, regardless of how it
affects others. It’s a dog-eat-dog world! You need to look out
for number one! There’s a sucker born every minute! We see this attitude in
such things as drug abuse, the alcohol and tobacco industries, intensely
competitive business practices, damage to the environment, pornography,
gambling, fraudulent banking, and on and on.

“Well,” we
say, “that was Cain,” or the Gadianton robbers, or other scriptural bad guys,
but the story of Balaam in the book of Numbers shows the seductive power of
wealth and position as it plays upon the soul of a prophet. Balaam is offered
“the rewards of divination” (Numbers 22:7) by Balak, the Moabite king, if he
would curse the children of Israel, whom Balak perceived as a threat. The Lord
told Balaam, “Thou shalt not go with them; thou shalt not curse the people: for
they are blessed” (Numbers 22:12). It was a fairly straightforward answer given
with Balaam’s welfare in mind. “Let nothing, I pray thee, hinder thee from
coming unto me,” Balak countered, “for I will promote thee unto very great
honour” (Numbers 22:16–17). Don’t even let God’s commands keep you away!
Still, Balaam held true to the Lord’s instruction, responding, “If Balak would
give me his house full of silver and gold, I cannot go beyond the word of the
Lord my God, to do less or more” (Numbers 22:18).

But the
gilded lure of wealth and honor had been set, the line needed only to be reeled
in, and though God tried to stop him by the unprecedented medium of speaking
through his donkey, Balaam eventually totally succumbed and in time was
executed by Moses for his unholy counsel to the Moabite king. Both Jude and
Peter used Balaam as an example of one who was corrupted by money. Peter spoke
of those who “have forsaken the right way, and are gone astray, following the
way of Balaam the son of Bosor, who loved the wages of unrighteousness; but was
rebuked for his iniquity: the dumb ass speaking with man’s voice forbad the
madness of the prophet” (2 Peter 2:15–16). Madness is, perhaps, the best
word for what riches can do to some people. In the book of Jude, Cain and
Balaam are linked together as Jude warned of those who “have gone in the way of
Cain, and ran greedily after the error of Balaam for reward” (Jude 1:11).

“IS IT A TIME TO RECEIVE MONEY?”

Even close
association with a prophet of the magnitude of Elisha could not save his
companion Gehazi from the attraction of riches. When Naaman offered Elisha “a
blessing” for having cured him of his leprosy, Elisha answered, “I will receive
none” 
(2 Kings 5:15–16). 

But Gehazi
had to have something. Hoping to deceive Elisha, he crept after the departing
Naaman and hatched a lie to receive from him “a talent of silver, and two
changes of garments.” Naaman, overjoyed to repay Elisha’s kindness, urged
Gehazi, “Be content, take two talents,” which Gehazi did. “Whence comest thou,
Gehazi?” Elisha queried when he returned. “Went not mine heart with thee
. . . ? Is it a time to receive money, and to receive garments, and
oliveyards, and vineyards, and sheep, and oxen, and menservants, and
maidservants?” (2 Kings 5:22–23, 25–26). That is a line of
questions we can ask ourselves when under the spell of silver talents and
resplendent clothing. Even Solomon, the genius behind Ecclesiastes, was not
immune, as he tried to cement the security of his very prosperous kingdom by
entering into marriages with “strange women,” who then “turned away his heart”
(1 Kings 11:1, 3).

The New
Testament offers us the painful story of Judas, whom John indicates “cared
[not] for the poor; but . . . was a thief, and had the bag, and bare
what was put therein” (John 12:6). John observed Judas’s hypocrisy when Mary
anointed Jesus with the spikenard. Momentarily blinded by the offer of thirty
pieces of silver, Judas betrayed Christ, the Master of masters, only to repent
and throw the money at the feet of Jesus’ persecutors, exclaiming in the agony
of his soul, “I have sinned in that I have betrayed the innocent blood”
(Matthew 27:4). He then went and hanged himself. 

Ananias and
his wife, Sapphira, “sold a possession, and kept back part of the price” (Acts
5:1–2). Wanting to appear as full supporters of the Church but unable to
resist the golden glow of shekels, they lied concerning their consecration. In
this case it was not the coveting of their own property that Peter felt was so
grievous but their hypocrisy. “Whiles it remained, was it not thine own? and
after it was sold, was it not in thine own power? why hast thou conceived this
thing in thine heart? thou hast not lied unto men, but unto God” (Acts 5:4).

There are
also those who rejected the ensnaring bait of money. Amulek comes to mind, when
the wealthy lawyer Zeezrom said, “Behold, here are six onties of silver, and
all these will I give thee if thou wilt deny the existence of a Supreme Being”
(Alma 11:22). There is Nephi, who offered Laban “our gold, and our silver, and
all our precious things,” if he would just give them the plates of brass (1
Nephi 3:24). Yet even religion itself has given in to the attractive decoy of
wealth in the creation of “priestcrafts,” when charity was to be the motive for
all religious acts. “The Lord God hath given a commandment that all men should
have charity, which charity is love. . . . Wherefore, if they should
have charity they would not suffer the laborer in Zion to perish. But the
laborer in Zion shall labor for Zion; for if they labor for money they shall
perish” (2 Nephi 26:30–31). And Moroni, speaking of some of our modern
churches and religions, stingingly drove the point home. “Behold, ye do love
money, and your substance, and your fine apparel, and the adorning of your
churches, more than ye love the poor and the needy, the sick and the afflicted.
. . . Why do ye adorn yourselves with that which hath no life, and
yet suffer the hungry, and the needy, and the naked, and the sick and the
afflicted to pass by you, and notice them not?” (Mormon 8:37–39).

I think, too,
of a story told by Benjamin Franklin. In his early life as a printer, when he
was trying to become established, a man came into the office with a piece he
wanted Franklin to publish. He was told he would receive an answer the next
day. When he returned he received Franklin’s decision. “I have perused your
piece, and find it to be scurrilous and defamatory. To determine whether I
should publish it or not, I went home in the evening, purchased a
two-penny loaf at the baker’s, and with water from the pump made my
supper; I then wrapped myself up in my great-coat, and laid down on
the floor and slept till morning, when, on another loaf and a mug of water, I
made my breakfast. From this regimen I feel no inconvenience whatever. Finding
I can live in this manner, I have formed a determination never to prostitute my
press to the purposes of corruption, and abuse of this kind, for the sake of
gaining a more comfortable subsistence.”3 What an example for the
press and businesses of our own day!

GOLDEN PLATES AND THE TURNING OF A MIND

One final
example may be helpful. It is that of Joseph Smith on his first trip to the
Hill Cumorah. Upon arrival he faced the temptation of using the gold plates,
which were destined for the glory of God, for the monetary value they would
have for his impoverished family instead. Which was more valuable, the plates
themselves or what was engraved upon them? We think of all that this wonderful
young man had going for him which would lead us to think he would resist Satan’s
trap. He had seen the Father and the Son. He had had four visits from Moroni
just hours before going to the hill. He was by nature a good boy. Moroni,
during his instruction sessions, had impressed upon Joseph’s mind the grand
vision of the Restoration as seen by past prophets and as projected onto the
world stage in the future. He had the support of his father, with whom he
conversed moments before leaving for Cumorah. He had also been given a specific
“caution . . . that Satan would try to tempt me (in consequence of
the indigent 
circumstances of my father’s family), to get the plates for the purpose of
getting rich. This he [Moroni] forbade me” (Joseph Smith–History 1:46).
Yet in the roughly hour’s time it took to walk from the Smith farm to the Hill
Cumorah, all thoughts of the work of God vanished from his mind in the one
driving desire to possess the golden treasure. Oliver Cowdery described
Joseph’s wrestle in the following words:

“Here was a
struggle indeed; for when he calmly reflected upon his errand, he knew that if
God did not give, he could not obtain; and again, with the thought or hope of
obtaining, his mind would be carried back to its former reflection of poverty,
abuse, wealth, grandeur and ease, until before arriving at the place described,
this wholly occupied his desire; and when he thought upon the fact of what was
previously shown him, it was only with an assurance that he should obtain, and
accomplish his desires in relieving himself and friends from want.”4

Joseph’s
state of mind necessitated three shocks from Moroni and his reappearance before
the calming influence of the Spirit mastered the greed for gold. We do not wish
to be hard on Joseph, as Oliver himself wrote, “In this, which occasioned a
failure to obtain, at that time, the record, do not understand me to attach
blame to our brother; he was young, and his mind easily turned from correct
principles, unless he could be favored with a certain round of experience.”5 What insightful words! We may all be
young and in need of experience when dealing with the considerable power and
enticement of wealth.

Later in his
life when he faced a burden of debt, even though by now “experienced,” Joseph
traveled all the way to Salem, Massachusetts, in search of a reported large sum
of money, a hidden treasure. When he arrived the Lord once again mildly 
corrected Joseph’s folly. “I, the Lord your God, am not displeased with your
coming this journey, notwithstanding your follies. I have much treasure in this
city for you, for the benefit of Zion, and many people in this city, whom I
will gather out in due time for the benefit of Zion, through your
instrumentality. . . . Concern not yourselves about your debts, for I
will give you power to pay them” (D&C 111:1–5).

The
attraction of money must never be underestimated. Most of us will battle with
all that it so readily promises. We will be challenged in discerning between
wants and needs. We cannot ignore our finances, but we must not let them
dominate our lives. We need not feel guilty about the conflict. Some of the
greatest souls sent to earth have faced it. It does not go away with one
enticement, as we will see in the Book of Mormon. We will either master it or
be mastered by it. One thing is certain, though: The Lord wishes us to pass
through the needle’s eye and has provided the scriptures to help us come out
the victor.
