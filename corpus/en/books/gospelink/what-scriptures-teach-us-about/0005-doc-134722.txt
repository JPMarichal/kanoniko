# Talents, Towers, and Far Countries

### Talents, Towers, and Far Countries
S. Michael Wilcox, What Scriptures Teach Us about Prosperity (Salt Lake City 2010)
 
 

Talents, Towers, and Far Countries

“ACCORDING TO HIS SEVERAL ABILITY”

At the
conclusion of his formal ministry at the temple, Jesus retired to the Mount of
Olives, where he taught his disciples of his Second Coming. He indicated
various events and signs that would occur before he returned. His emphasis,
however, was not on those signs, but on being prepared for his appearance. He
did not want his Saints checking events off a list with mounting anticipation;
rather he wanted them doing things and was quite specific about the things he
wanted done. His counsels were not only for the immediate generation that would
witness his arrival, but for all who would live in the turbulent times that
preceded it. Much of that counsel was given in three parables that occupy all
of Matthew 25. Two have relevancy for our topic. They constitute what we can do
to survive our times and prepare for his promised return.

The parable
of the talents has many layers of meaning, as do many of the Savior’s
teachings: “For the kingdom of heaven is as a man travelling into a far
country, who called his own servants, and delivered unto them his goods. And unto one he gave five talents,
to another two, and to another one; to every man according to his several
ability; and straightway took his journey” (Matthew 25:14–15; emphasis
added).

The first
thing we notice is these are the Lord’s servants and his goods. We are not our
own, nor is what we possess our own. All belongs to God. This is the foundation
truth upon which the law of consecration rests, which we will explore in
another chapter. The talents given to each of the Lord’s three servants can
represent a number of things. Some of us have received, through inheritance or
other means, actual wealth. Some have received faculties which enable us to
generate wealth. Some have greater resources of time to bestow upon the Lord’s
kingdom. Some have greater mental capacity, or energy, or genius. For some,
opportunities are created. To some, educational prospects are granted which, in
time, allow them to develop skills that generate material resources. The possibilities
are limitless. What will we do with these godly endowments? The assumption is
made in the parable that the Lord considers our varying abilities when these
gifts and aptitudes are granted, but we must keep foremost in our minds that
they are the Lord’s talents and they are given to increase his kingdom. The
servants did not multiply what they had received to build up their own
financial kingdoms.

“Then he that
had received the five talents went and traded with the same, and made them
other five talents. And likewise he that had received two, he also gained other
two.” In time the Lord returned to see what they had accomplished. “And so he
that had received five talents came and brought other five talents, saying,
Lord, thou deliveredst unto me five talents: behold, I have gained beside them
five talents more” (Matthew 25:16–17, 20). A similar report was given by
the servant who received two.

There is in
the parable no question as to the owner of the original endowment and the
enlargement. The servants increased their allotment for their Lord, not
themselves. However, the reward for their faithfulness indicates a future
dominion, “Well done, thou good and faithful servant: thou hast been faithful
over a few things, I will make thee ruler over many things: enter thou into the
joy of thy lord” (Matthew 25:21). We note that the exact same words of praise
are given to the servant with two talents. Again it is not the total amount of
the increase that is significant but the labor. I think the Lord’s joy is not
in the increase of his goods, but in the faithfulness of his servants. For us,
that would mean we would be able to overcome the natural selfishness, pride, or
false sense of ownership that wealth can frequently create by devoting
ourselves to his cause and recognizing that all things are the Lord’s. We get
our inheritance in eternity, not mortality. On earth we can call nothing our
own. It appears that our eternal dominion depends a great deal on our attitude
toward temporal talents and what we do with them.

This truth is
also taught in a parable studied in an earlier chapter, that of the unjust
steward. The very word “steward” suggests one who uses and works with another’s
goods. “And if ye have not been faithful in that which is another man’s, who shall give you that which is
your own?” (Luke
16:12; emphasis added).

The servant
who had received one talent “digged in the earth, and hid his lord’s money.”
When his turn to account for his labor came, he said: “I was afraid, and went
and hid thy talent in the earth: lo, there thou hast that is thine” (Matthew
25:18, 25). Again we notice the emphasis on the true owner of the talents. The
servant claimed it was fear that motivated his actions, but the Lord reveals
another factor: “Thou . . . slothful servant. . . . Thou oughtest
therefore to have put my money to the exchangers, and then at my coming I
should have received mine own with usury” (Matthew 25:26–27; emphasis
added).

The only
condemnation comes to the servant who does not engage in the enterprise of
building the Lord’s kingdom because of sloth masquerading as fear. Far too
often our criticism focuses on wealth, the apparent result of hard work, but in
this instance those who do nothing, the lazy or indolent, stand condemned. We
may be tested by the Lord with a great stewardship like the man with the five
talents, but we can be equally tested for our failure to increase, according to
our several abilities, what the Lord has placed in our hands. The poor man who
is poor due to his sloth, and therefore gives nothing to the kingdom, may be
equal in censure to the rich man who is flagrant in his waste and thus also
gives nothing to the kingdom.

Increases to
the Lord’s kingdom are not measured in monetary terms. The talents he gives are
not temporal in nature. There are spiritual values to be considered. The Lord
specifically told Joseph Smith that “in temporal labors thou shalt not have
strength, for this is not thy calling. Attend to thy calling” (D&C 24:9).
Joseph was to “devote all thy service in Zion; and in this thou shalt have
strength” (D&C 24:7). A brief examination of Joseph’s temporal affairs will
show the correctness of the Lord’s assessment of his “several ability.” Where
one may not contribute great financial benefits, one may share spiritual gifts.
Other gifts may be equally beneficial to the growth of the Lord’s work on the
earth. I know several families whose material means are very small and whose
talents in church callings or leadership are also somewhat limited but who have
raised large and wonderful families. Perhaps these offspring were the primary
“talents” the Lord gave them. Here too some may receive five, some two, some
one.

“I WAS AN HUNGRED”

As is
apparent from the previous parable, the Lord gives us resources to accomplish
his work on the earth. Part of that work consists of helping others, so
positioning the parable of the sheep and the goats immediately after that of
the parable of the talents certainly seems relevant. One parable tells us to
increase our goods; the other tells us to do good with them. Although we most
often apply this parable to individuals, which is certainly proper, it is
directed to a larger constituency. “When the Son of man shall come in his glory
. . . before him shall be gathered all nations: and he shall separate
them one from another, as a shepherd divideth his sheep from the goats”
(Matthew 25:31–32). The judgment is on a national scale. As Saints, as
Americans, as members of a society blessed with so much, how do we treat what
we sometimes call “the disadvantaged”?

“Then shall
the King say unto them on his right hand, Come, ye blessed of my Father,
inherit the kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world: For I
was an hungred, and ye gave me meat: I was thirsty, and ye gave me drink: I was
a stranger, and ye took me in: Naked, and ye clothed me: I was sick, and ye
visited me: I was in prison, and ye came unto me” (Matthew 25:34–36).

As in the
parable of the talents the implication is that those who can properly handle
the treasures of the earth in selfless acts of goodness will be given an
inheritance hereafter. Blessing the lives of “the least of these my brethren”
(Matthew 25:40) is valued by the Lord as service done to him and is one of the
primary things he wants us to do with that which is his own. King Benjamin
taught the same idea to the Nephites, inviting them to “learn wisdom.” He said,
“When ye are in the service of your fellow beings ye are only in the service of
your God” (Mosiah 2:17). What is interesting about this verse is that it is
given in the context of Benjamin’s emphatic assertion that we are all
“unprofitable servants.” This is true even if we “should serve him with all
your whole souls” (Mosiah 2:21). No matter what we do with our
talents—doubling them as did the servants in the parable or
even increasing them “an hundred fold”—we remain unprofitable. In this
truth we find humility to conquer the pride that might come to any who were
given five talents and saw them increase to ten through their efforts.

“I WILL PAY THEE ALL”

With the idea
of our perpetual indebtedness to the Lord in mind and also his injunction to
alleviate suffering that we may 
be found on his right hand, let us pause to look at another parable of Jesus.
It too speaks in monetary terms. This parable deals with forgiveness, but we
can use its lessons for the subject at hand because it quite graphically
portrays what we too often find in financial dealings. It emphasizes a need for
compassion in pecuniary matters which, if we are not careful, can be seen
strictly through the harsh lens of business bottom lines. As in so many of the
parables we have studied, Jesus begins with “Therefore is the kingdom of heaven
likened unto . . .” (Matthew 18:23), stressing that we are being
shown how the Lord operates. A certain king calls his servant to account and
finds one who owes ten thousand talents, an enormous sum.

“But
forasmuch as he had not to pay, his lord commanded him to be sold, and his
wife, and children, and all that he had, and payment to be made.” How close
this frighteningly calculated, cold solution to the man’s financial dilemma is
to many modern situations is sobering. However, mercy and compassion are called
for and will be forthcoming. “The servant therefore fell down, and worshipped
him, saying, Lord, have patience with me, and I will pay thee all.” Notice that
the servant does not try to avoid payment altogether. But the king knows the
sum is so vast the possibility of repayment is minimal; therefore he extends
mercy. “Then the lord of that servant was moved with compassion, and loosed
him, and forgave him the debt” (Matthew 18:25–27). I am not suggesting
that debts be removed—we will see a very practical solution to
high levels of debt when we turn to the teachings of Moses—what
I am suggesting is that certain matters involving money be dealt with in a
compassionate manner. That is the way of the “kingdom of heaven.”

Unfortunately
the recipient of compassion was not willing to extend it to a fellow debtor.
Having been forgiven his massive obligation, the same servant could not forgive
the debt of “an hundred pence,” a miniscule fraction of what he had owed the
king. “The same servant went out, and found one of his fellowservants
. . . and he laid hands on him, and took him by the throat, saying,
Pay me that thou owest. And his fellowservant fell down at his feet, and
besought him, saying, Have patience with me, and I will pay thee all. And he
would not: but went and cast him into prison, till he should pay the debt”
(Matthew 18:28–30).

Granted, this
is a parable about forgiveness of sins, and yet it still shows a stunningly
credible picture of what can happen if we get too caught up in the pursuit of
wealth. There is something hauntingly realistic in the image of the creditor
grasping the debtor by the throat and demanding full payment. (In modern times,
that would also include a hefty interest rate.) There is an inhumanity about it
that makes a debtor an object, instead of a fellow member of the human race.
This inhumanity can be created by an overly focused pursuit of profit that
dehumanizes both individuals—the creditor and the debtor. We
must remember the key phrase in the parable is “moved with compassion.”

COUNTING THE COST

It might be
helpful to examine another of the Savior’s parables that does not deal directly
with money in its main theme, but teaches a very important financial principle
all the same. “For which of you, intending to build a tower, sitteth not down
first, and counteth the cost, whether he have sufficient to finish it? Lest
haply, after he hath laid the foundation, and is not able to finish it, all
that behold it begin to mock him, saying, This man began to build, and was not
able to finish” (Luke 14:28–30). 

The idea
Jesus taught with this illustration was the necessity of his disciples
committing to follow him to the end. It also teaches our need for advance
planning in the temporal affairs of our lives. Perhaps the most obvious
applications deal with purchasing a home, saving for retirement, planning for
future education needs, and accumulating credit card debt. These are areas
where we need to “count the cost,” to see if we have sufficient to match our
future desires. Mortgage foreclosures, disappointed or delayed retirements,
limited occupational opportunities, and crushing credit card payments are often
the result of not carefully evaluating financial resources or future needs.
Many a partially constructed tower has been lost through lack of a disciplined
view of reality. This does not mean we must give up building the tower
altogether, but we may need a smaller tower, one within the range of pragmatic
possibilities; one also that, as we talked about in a previous chapter, does
not require so much time making bricks. 

“INTO A FAR COUNTRY”

One final
parable of the Savior’s is worthy of attention—that of the
prodigal son. This is a parable about forgiveness and the drawing power of a
father’s love, but it also speaks of what we sometimes call “legacies.” It
teaches the common result of someone winning or inheriting or receiving
considerable sums immediately. How do most human beings react when they
suddenly come into a large amount of money all at once? Sudden wealth does not
seem to produce the element of conservation that funds we work for and
accumulate over time does. Too much at once almost always leads to waste. We
can set that down as a rule of human nature. We see it in business, in
government, even the Church. Money we don’t earn in real terms of labor or
careful investment is easily spent. Solomon with his gift of wisdom taught:
“Wealth gotten by vanity [meaning in this context easily or without personal
effort] shall be diminished: but he that gathereth by labour shall increase”
(Proverbs 13:11). Notice this truth is also suggested in the introductory
verses of the parable of the prodigal son:

“A certain
man had two sons: And the younger of them said to his father, Father, give me
the portion of goods that falleth to me. And he divided unto them his living.
And not many days after the younger son gathered all together, and took his
journey into a far country, and there wasted his substance with riotous living.
And when he had spent all, there arose a mighty famine in that land; and he
began to be in want” (Luke 15:11–14). An excess of feasting almost always
leads to the deficiencies of famine.

One of the
most natural of parental desires lies in a concern for the future of our
children. This has monetary ramifications, as most people want to “leave
something for the kids.” We do this from a desire to bless our children or
grandchildren, but we may, if we are not careful, also cause them harm. I am
not a financial planner, and I will personally have to make these decisions for
my own descendants, but a realization of the tendency of human nature to do,
whether moderately or liberally, what the younger son did must be considered.
In the parable, the elder son handled the division and remained with his
father; the other wasted it. Studies have been conducted which bear out the
principle that children who receive large sums of money often lose it rather
quickly and in a majority of cases it can have negative consequences on the
child.

Solomon
pondered on this very result when writing Ecclesiastes, a book that attempts to
discover the secrets of a wise and a fulfilled and happy life. “Yea, I hated
[meaning, I saw that it was vain] all my labour which I had taken under the
sun: because I should leave it unto the man that shall be after me. And who
knoweth whether he shall be a wise man or a fool? yet shall he have rule over all
my labour wherein I have laboured, and wherein I have shewed myself wise under
the sun. This is also vanity. . . . For there is a man whose labour
is in wisdom, and in knowledge, and in equity; yet to a man that hath not
laboured therein shall he leave it for his portion” (Ecclesiastes
2:18–19, 21). 

In the best
of circumstances, if we ourselves are able to find a passage through the
needle’s eye, we may make it difficult for others to follow by granting
legacies they might not have the wisdom or knowledge to handle.

The
scriptures have a wonderful way of providing balance. We see this in
Ecclesiastes. Solomon also warned against the loss of wealth through avarice or
evil fortune where nothing is left to pass on to the succeeding generation.
“There is a sore evil which I have seen under the sun, namely, riches kept for
the owners thereof to their hurt. But those riches perish by evil travail: and
he begetteth a son, and there is nothing in his hand” (Ecclesiastes
5:13–14). Hence our need of guidance from the Spirit in discovering that
delicate balance between giving blessings to children or handing them a
temptation they cannot master. We would avoid inadvertently inviting them to
travel to a far country, there to spend all and end up in want—just
the opposite of what we intended.
