# Lawsuits and the Lord’s Supper

###  Lawsuits and the Lord’s Supper
S. Michael Wilcox, What Scriptures Teach Us about Prosperity (Salt Lake City 2010)
 
 

 Lawsuits and the Lord’s Supper

“THE SMALLEST MATTERS”

Different
prophets have stressed distinct principles according to their own personalities
and the times in which and people with whom they live. In the epistles and life
of Paul we soon discover that unity was a major theme of his ministry. He tried
to soothe and eliminate all the various divisive elements that might alienate
one member from another—Jew and Gentile, male and female,
learned and unlearned, master and servant, parent and child, rich and poor. As
in almost any age or community, Paul found these problems among his converts in
the early Church and they troubled him. Often the divisions had to do with
material issues which Paul felt was doubly problematic. Concerns of this nature
flourished in Corinth, in particular. It might be helpful to take a look at two
of such problems.

“Dare any of
you, having a matter against another, go to law 
before the unjust, and not before the saints? do ye not know 
that the saints shall judge the world? and if the world shall be judged by you,
are ye unworthy to judge the smallest matters?” 
(1 Corinthians 6:1–2). Legal questions can create some of the most
delicate predicaments. They have a tendency to create festering animosity. As
we saw in the statements and attitude of Jesus, Paul also considered these
types of temporal concerns to be “the smallest matters.” In a somewhat ironic
and mildly sarcastic tone, Paul therefore suggested that “if then ye have
judgments of things pertaining to this life, set them to judge who are least
esteemed in the church” (1 Corinthians 6:4). It is not hard to see the pairing
of the words “smallest matters” with “least esteemed.” When we think in terms
of right and wrong instead of legalities, or charity instead of rights, the solutions
may not be as complex as at first view.

“Is it so,
that there is not a wise man among you? no, not one that shall be able to judge
between his brethren? But brother goeth to law with brother, and that before
the unbelievers. Now therefore there is utterly a fault among you, because ye
go to law one with another” (1 Corinthians 6:5–7). There are undoubtedly
times when financial matters between two Church members or within a family need
to be settled by legal arbitration, but the hard feelings that create these
issues and the uncompromising stands some take can be absolutely destructive to
relationships and the souls of those involved. Property, inheritances,
businesses, wages, wills, and other material matters can become an obsession.
Can we not solve these problems as united members—of either a
ward or a family—who share the same beliefs, without the
complications of lawyers, fees, and courts? Paul believed we could, and he
pleaded in a rather strong tone that we find a way to do so.

We have probably
all seen examples of the damage money matters can do to friends and families.
In my extended family, I have witnessed brothers and sisters threatening
legal action over property. Having feelings of sympathy and love for both
sides, I have found it distressing to watch long-held and valued
relationships decay into bitterness. I know of very fine people who are no
longer active in the Church, nursing sometimes decades-old angry
memories of financial arguments. I am also familiar with people who have let go
of any perceived slight, even to their own fiscal hurt, and moved on with their
lives in forgiveness. They may be sadder and wiser, but they are not resentful
and bitter. Paul suggests that sometimes this is the best solution: “Why do ye
not rather take wrong? why do ye not rather suffer yourselves to be defrauded?
Nay, ye do wrong, and defraud, and that your brethren” (1 Corinthians
6:7–8). If you are entangled in a legal dispute, it is better to be on
the side of the injured, Paul cautions, than on that of the deceiver. This is
in the spirit of the Savior’s own teaching in the Sermon on the Mount: “If any
man will sue thee at the law, and take away thy coat, let him have thy cloke
also” (Matthew 5:40). The spirit of these counsels does not preclude defending
our financial interests, but there comes a point at which both Paul and the
Savior would have us drop the matter, even to our own temporal hurt, rather
than perpetuate bitterness and rancor. Perpetuating bitterness would do more
spiritual harm to us than the monetary matter that is the seed of our disputes.
These situations are particularly painful when family members are the chief
antagonists. It is sad to see years of bonding, sharing, and living together
destroyed by a concern over money. It simply is not worth it!

“THERE BE DIVISIONS AMONG YOU”

A second
point of discord in Corinth was taking place in their religious gatherings.
“When ye come together in the church, I hear that there be divisions among you;
and I partly believe it. . . . When ye come together into one place,
is it not to eat the Lord’s supper? But in eating every one taketh before his
own supper; and one is hungry, and another is drunken. What! have ye not houses
to eat and to drink in? or despise ye the church of God, and shame them that
have not? What shall I say to you?” (JST 1 Corinthians 11:18, 20–22).

In the Church
today we have social gatherings. These are desirable and a natural part of the
gospel. In the early Church some of these took place on the same day as they partook
of the sacrament. Paul was not against such gatherings. They were not to
eclipse the sacrament, but they were nonetheless desirable. Part of the joy of
the Church is in our interactions with each other. When we assemble as members,
however, we must be sensitive to one another. In Corinth, the more
well-to-do members were bringing meals which were beyond that
which the poorer members of the congregation could provide for themselves. This
was causing embarrassment to those who had not the means of feasting at the
same level as their more affluent neighbors.

In the book
of Acts we read of a similar situation, one that necessitated the calling of
seven faithful brothers to see that unity was not damaged by an unequal
distribution of resources. This is the foundation of what has developed into
the seven Presidents of the Seventy in the Church today. “And in those days,
when the number of disciples was multiplied, there arose a murmuring of the
Grecians against the Hebrews, because their widows were neglected in the daily
ministration” (Acts 6:1). This problem was 
exacerbated by the ethnic difference involved. The Apostles felt they could not
“leave the word of God, and serve tables.” Therefore they chose “seven men of
honest report, full of the Holy Ghost and wisdom,” whom the Apostles then
appointed “over this business” (Acts 6:2–3). Stephen, who was later
stoned, was one of the men selected. If these inequalities do not create
outright division, they are sure to induce murmuring, as was the case in
Acts.

We must be
aware of such things also. It was to address this very concern that the
leadership of the Church put all the stakes and wards on an equal budgetary
footing. Now each ward receives funds for ward activities from the headquarters
of the Church, based on sacrament meeting attendance. Many of us remember when
each ward had to raise the necessary budget for building maintenance, youth
programs, ward dinners, and other needs of the ward. During those days some of
the wards whose members generally had higher incomes could provide budget
donations sufficient for extended trips for youth conferences, catered ward
dinners, and other luxuries. At the same time wards whose members could not
contribute at the same level provided youth activities closer to home and
potluck dinners when possible. I once lived near a ward that took all their
youth scuba diving in California one year, while the ward only a few blocks
away could afford only a local youth conference on a very slim budget. They
cleaned a local cemetery, had a dance, and held a progressive dinner throughout
the neighborhood. Equalization in funding missionaries was also instituted at
about the same time to avoid inequities that may trouble the unity of the
Church.

It is not
possible to solve all the problems that fall under 
this area of Paul’s concern, but sensitivity to disparities will be helpful. I
recall a moment that was very embarrassing for my wife and me when we lived in
an apartment in Scottsdale, Arizona. The ward was filled with wonderful people.
I cannot think of a ward I have lived in who had better. The ward had a
barbeque to which members brought their own meat to cook. All the other food
was provided by the ward. We were just out of college with a new baby. Family
finances were very tight. We brought hot dogs. You can imagine how we felt when
the other members of the 
ward brought steak and salmon. They were all gracious to us and some shared
their steak, but it was one of the most distressing social events I have
attended in the Church. We might find ourselves putting pressure on each other
in other areas—wedding receptions, for example. Some can afford a
professionally orchestrated evening while others are stretching to decorate the
local cultural hall. We might keep Paul’s counsel in mind when making decisions
of this nature. Unity is a delicate, fragile thing but one well worth the
sacrifice and perspective it requires to maintain.

“ONE HEART AND ONE MIND”

Harmony
within the brotherhood and sisterhood of the Church is the very quintessence of
Zion.

Probably the
best-known description of Zion is the one found in the book of Moses where the
city of Enoch is described. Here we read: “And the Lord called his people Zion,
because they were of one heart and one mind, and dwelt in righteousness; and there
was no poor among them”
(Moses 7:18; emphasis added). There is no question that the context of the
verse suggests that a large part of their being of one heart and one mind
consisted in financial matters, for the phrase “and there was no poor among
them” is in the same sentence. 

Union between
the haves and the have nots is difficult to achieve. Those who have not may
resent the comforts, the lifestyle, the extravagance, the waste (or what they
perceive to be extravagance or waste) of the more affluent. Those who have may
resent those who have not and their perception that they need to support,
through taxes or other means, those they assess to be lazy, or who should be
taking care of themselves, or should be wiser or more hard-working. These
discordant judgments are far too common. They are not universal, but the
temptation to give in to these thoughts will always be present. The interesting
thing is that depending on where we are—for instance, somewhere
in the middle class—we might experience both positions at the
same time, judging those with higher incomes as well as those with lower
incomes than our own. The irony of finding ourselves in this position of
conflicting viewpoints places us somewhere between the opposing poles of either
the hypocritical or the humorous. Overcoming these tendencies of thought
dovetails perfectly with the other most frequently quoted description of Zion
as being “THE PURE IN HEART” (D&C 97:21). The pure in heart strive to
eliminate these types of judgmental, uncharitable thoughts. They sincerely wish
them not to take root in their minds.

For this
reason, the Lord gave to the Church early in the Restoration a parable to
ponder: “For what man among you having twelve sons, and is no respecter of
them, and they serve him obediently, and he saith unto the one: Be thou clothed
in robes and sit thou here; and to the other: Be thou clothed in rags and sit
thou there—and looketh upon his sons and saith I am just?
Behold, this I have given unto you as a parable, and it is even as I am. I say
unto you, be one; and if ye are not one ye are not mine” (D&C
38:26–27). We hear in this admonition echoes of Christ’s teachings at the
Last Supper to be one. We do not generally interpret Jesus’ teachings on that solemn
occasion as relative to money. However, in this parable his teachings are
applied to fiscal concerns. For passage through the needle’s eye, we must be
constantly aware of our own thoughts and actions regarding unity in matters
that stretch from complicated legal situations to the ward picnic.
