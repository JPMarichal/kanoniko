# The Law of Consecration

### The Law of Consecration
S. Michael Wilcox, What Scriptures Teach Us about Prosperity (Salt Lake City 2010)
 
 

The Law of Consecration

“ALL FLESH IS MINE”

The law of
consecration is in large measure the Lord’s way of tempering our tendencies to
succumb to the seduction of wealth. If we understand its principles, they provide
a path for us to walk so we can prosper without negativity. The Lord declares
it is a privilege to live within the boundaries and be blessed by the
principles of the law of consecration. Since it is a fundamental, indeed
crowning part of the temple covenants we make, it is critical that we
understand its operation. It can ensure our passage through the needle’s eye.
We turn to the Doctrine and Covenants for the most complete description of this
law. The principles necessary for consecration to flourish were introduced
early in the Restoration. In section 38 we find some of those principles. Many
of them are taught in other places in scripture, and most will be recognizable.
“The poor have complained before me, and the rich have I made, and all flesh is
mine, and I am no respecter of persons” (D&C 38:16). We belong to God and
our desire should be to do what he would have us do. Our talents are his,
certainly those we use to acquire worldly gain, as well as our time. The poor
are as important as the rich and the Lord hears their complaints.

As in other
verses previously examined, wealth is not the thing God would have us seek.
There are more important items to attend to. “And if ye seek the riches which
it is the will of the Father to give unto you, ye shall be the richest of all
people, for ye shall have the riches of eternity” (D&C 38:39). While the
riches of the earth are not insignificant, and they are also gifts that God
gives, we must constantly realize they always carry the danger of producing pride.
“It must needs be that the riches of the earth are mine to give; but beware of
pride, lest ye become as the Nephites of old” (D&C 38:39). We may
mistakenly infer from the wording of this verse that though the riches of the
world are God’s, he does give them to us, which implies we now own them. In a
later section the Lord emphasizes the fact that nothing we have is really ours.
The “riches of the earth” are given to us to manage for God, as indicated in
the parable of the unjust steward, and the parable of the talents. “For it is
expedient that I, the Lord, should make every man accountable, as a steward
over earthly blessings, which I have made and prepared for my creatures. I, the
Lord, stretched out the heavens, and built the earth, my very handiwork; and all
things therein are mine.
And it is my purpose to provide for my saints, for all things are mine” (D&C 104:12–14; emphasis
added). We are his, all that we have is his, and we will one day account to him
how we ran his property as his steward. In one sense, we can’t give the Lord
anything. It is all his anyway. Consecration is a recognition of this
established fact. To maintain the idea of personal ownership is to remain blind
to truth. Yet the Lord gives us wide latitude in our discretionary use of his benefits.
We are to keep constantly in mind, however, that “all these properties are
mine, or else your faith is vain. . . . And let not any among you say
that it is his own; for it shall not be called his, nor any part of it”
(D&C 104:55, 70).

“LET EVERY MAN ESTEEM HIS BROTHER AS HIMSELF”

Returning to
section 38, we see that the Lord stresses by repetition another foundation
concept of consecration. “And let every man esteem his brother as himself, and
practise virtue and holiness before me. And again I say unto you, let every man
esteem his brother as himself” (D&C 38:24–25). The needs of my fellow
man are as crucial to me as are my own. This does not demand such a sacrifice
that I am left in want, but one which requires me to be willing to deny myself
luxuries so that others may have necessities. “This is the way that I, the
Lord, have decreed to provide for my saints, that the poor shall be exalted, in
that the rich are made low” (D&C 104:16). Like the ends of a bow which is
pulled taut to shoot an arrow, the wealthy bend down while the poor are lifted.
The archer is the Lord, and he knows how much force to exert lest the bow break
and become useless. The points do not touch, but the distance is greatly
narrowed. Consecration is the bowstring that draws the opposite ends together.
Desire for the well-being of one’s fellowmen is a keen motivator. Each member
is to “improve upon his talent, that every man may gain other talents,” for the
betterment and blessing “of the whole church—every man seeking
the interest of his neighbor, and doing all things with an eye single to the
glory of God” (D&C 82:18–19).

Consecration,
in order to offset a destructive attitude of 
competition, affirms that there is enough to go around, lest we think we have
to get ours before another takes it for his own needs—the
financial and societal equivalent of “survival of the fittest.” “I have made
the earth rich,” he assures us (D&C 38:17). “For the earth is full, and
there is enough and to spare; yea, I prepared all things, and have given unto
the children of men to be agents unto themselves” (D&C 104:17). King David
also realized the fulness of the earth: “O Lord, how manifold are thy works! in
wisdom hast thou made them all: the earth is full of thy riches” (Psalm
104:24).

We all can
have sufficient for our needs, but this will not happen if some take more at
the expense of those who must, therefore, have less. Providing for mankind’s
needs has been prepared for, “but it must needs be done in mine own way”
(D&C 104:16). However, it is not the Lord’s way to force compliance with
his laws. We are agents. Consecration is not the distribution of wealth by an
outside force (in this case, the Church). All must be given freely with no
compulsion attached. No current economic system is consecration.
State-controlled economies deny agency through assuming state ownership of
resources. Free-market economies grant control of property but assume
self-interest as the chief motivator of productivity. 

Notice the
wording describing consecration and stewardship in the following verse: “Every
man shall be made . . . a steward over his own property” (D&C 42:32; emphasis added).
Property does not belong to the state. It does not belong to the Church. It
belongs to the individual, but with the caveat that the individual realizes
that in the final analysis all belongs to God. Each individual family does the
best they can with their talents, property, and resources for the overall good
of the Lord’s kingdom and their fellow men. “Let him obtain whatsoever he can
obtain in righteousness, for the good of the saints” (D&C 57:12). As we
have seen in earlier scriptures, seeking to excel in one’s work or investment
is not for personal gain or aggrandizement but for the good of the Saints.

With the
parable of the rich man and Lazarus in mind, we are reminded that the Lord
exhorts us to freely share because we truly do esteem our brother as ourselves.
“Therefore, if any man shall take of the abundance which I have made, and
impart not his portion, according to the law of my gospel, unto the poor and
the needy, he shall, with the wicked, lift up his eyes in hell, being in
torment” (D&C 104:18). I hesitate to read this verse literally, rather than
metaphorically. The allusion to Lazarus’s wealthy contemporary is clear. God is
not interested in punishment unless it is redemptive, moving the individual to
righteous thoughts and actions. I think it safe to assume that torment is not a
God-imposed punishment, but the realization of failed opportunities.
Charles Dickens described it well when he wrote in A Christmas Carol the words of Marley’s ghost to his
former partner Ebenezer Scrooge:

“It is
required of every man . . . that the spirit within him should walk
abroad among his fellow-men, and travel far and wide; and if that
spirit goes not forth in life, it is condemned to do so after death. It is
doomed to wander through the world—oh, woe is me!—and
witness what it cannot share, but might have shared on earth, and turned to
happiness! . . . Oh! captive, bound, and double-ironed
. . . not to know, that ages of incessant labour by immortal
creatures, for this earth must pass into eternity before the good of which it
is susceptible is all developed. Not to know that any Christian spirit working
kindly in its little sphere, whatever it may be, will find its mortal life too
short for its vast means of usefulness. Not to know that no space of regret can
make amends for one life’s opportunity misused! Yet such was I! Oh! such was I!
. . . Business! . . . Mankind was my business. The common
welfare was my business; charity, mercy, forbearance, and benevolence, were,
all, my business. The dealings of my trade were but a drop of water in the
comprehensive ocean of my business!”1

Scrooge is
then shown many other ghosts like Marley. They are all haunted by the desire to
help in human affairs and no longer are able. “The misery with them all was,
clearly, that they sought to interfere, for good, in human matters, and had
lost the power for ever.”2 Marley’s longing is essentially that of
the rich man when he wanted Lazarus to be sent to his five living brothers and
warn them of their future distress.

FAMILY—CIRCUMSTANCES—WANTS—NEEDS

How do we
determine how much to consecrate? That is a question we will all puzzle over in
no small degree. The Lord offers us some help above and beyond the basic
principles already discussed. Out of all the blessings the Lord has bestowed
upon us, one keeps “as much as is sufficient for himself and family.”
Consecration desires “that every man who has need may be amply supplied
and receive according to his wants” (D&C 42:32–33). “Wants” in this
sense means those particular things necessary to sustain life. These need to be
amply supplied. In another section of the Doctrine and Covenants the Lord lists
four considerations when making decisions about the amount one returns to the
Lord for building his kingdom, doing good, or helping the poor. “Every man
equal,” we read, “according to his family, according to his 
circumstances and
his wants and needs” (D&C 51:3; emphasis added).
Whatever is more than these four factors should be used as the Lord would
desire. “Inasmuch as they receive more than is needful for their necessities
and their wants, it
shall be given into my storehouse” (D&C 70:7; emphasis added). We can also
add the word necessities
to our list of considerations.

The size of
our families is, naturally, a consideration. Larger families of necessity
require more for their maintenance. There may be circumstances such as poor
health, or caring for a parent, which would also determine the amount we
reserve for our use and how much we can consecrate to the Lord’s work. Wants
can be interpreted two ways. One is those things we desire but are not
necessary to sustain life at a simple, rudimentary level—things
we would purchase with what we call discretionary money. The other is a
definition interchangeable with needs. The meaning of needs and necessities is
obvious. As we age and our situations in life change, such as increased or
decreased earnings, family growth or children moving away, declining health, or
retirement, we will need to shift our resources accordingly. Consecration
requires vigilant reassessment.

THE HOUSE WITH TWO DOORS

Here, then,
are the foundation pillars holding up the temple of consecration. We belong to
God. All that we have belongs to God. The Lord does not respect one person
above another and would have us think the same, esteeming everyone as we do
ourselves and concerned for their welfare. The earth has enough for all if some
do not take more for themselves at the expense of others not having enough. We
are all stewards, managing what the Lord has given us, which we freely, without
compulsion, return to him to fulfill his purposes. These consist mainly in
caring for the poor and needy and building up his kingdom. In time we will all
render an account of our stewardship to the rightful owner of all. Family,
circumstances, wants, needs, and necessities are all taken into consideration
when making consecration decisions. There is also the provision that debt be
avoided and paid when contracted, as one cannot consecrate what he or she does
not own. There is a universal application of these principles to us all. “None
are exempt,” the Lord says, “from this law who belong to the church of the
living God” (D&C 70:10). This has a reinforced application to those who
have been to the temple.

There are two
final points the Doctrine and Covenants makes which are essential for our
faith. “If you proceed to do the things which I have laid before you, according
to my commandments, all these things are mine, and ye are my stewards, and the
master will not suffer his house to be broken up. Even so. Amen” (D&C
104:86). The anxiety and worry wealth can bring—as spoken of, for
example, in Ecclesiastes or by Samuel the Lamanite—can
dissolve. These things are God’s, and he will watch over them and help us make
correct decisions concerning their preservation and growth. There is also the
promise that heavenly blessings will follow. We will be rich in knowledge and
revelation. We will be prepared for a celestial inheritance. “That you may be
equal in the bonds of heavenly things, yea, and earthly things also, for the
obtaining of heavenly things. . . . For if you will that I give unto
you a place in the celestial world, you must prepare yourselves by doing the
things which I have commanded you and required of you” (D&C 78:5–7).

Perhaps a
good way to illustrate consecration is through an analogy. Let us compare this
law to a house with two doors, one in the front and one in the back. The Lord
pours in blessings through the front door in the way of talents, resources,
opportunities, education, occupational success, riches, and other gifts. We
assess all he has given us and decide, based on our family, circumstances,
wants, needs, and necessities what we require for the maintenance of the house.
This will include monthly living expenses, tithes and offerings, transportation
and housing expenditures, future retirement plans, health and life insurance, educational
costs—the multiple financial decisions we make from day to day.
We look at what is left, realizing it is the Lord’s, and shovel it out the back
door in doing good for others, helping the poor, and in building his kingdom.
Then we say with a sigh of relief, “I must never shut the back door. I must
keep it wide open.” 

“We have some
unique individuals here,” the Lord observes. “Let’s bless them some more and
see what they do.” He opens the front door and pours in more blessings. We
again assess our family, circumstances, needs, wants, and necessities and once
again open the back door and shovel out the excess in doing good. 

“We have one
in a thousand,” the Lord says, opening the front door again. Lest we be tempted
by all that wealth can do to a soul, we continue to keep the back door open and
keep shoveling. We continue to say, “I must never shut the back door. I must
keep it wide open.” 

As we have
seen, maintaining this attitude over a lifetime is very rare. There is always
the temptation to close the back door or at least to lessen our output
somewhat. If we can keep it open, using with wisdom that which we need, and
then sharing the rest, we will have mastered consecration. It is certainly one
of the highest laws of God’s earthly kingdom but one that shows we are striving
for a celestial kingdom. Consecration is the passageway through the needle’s
eye for the Latter-day Saints.

“CAN I BUY THIS WITH THE LORD’S MONEY?”

Making
financial decisions becomes somewhat easier when we think in terms of consecration.
Take the example of purchasing an automobile. Knowing that all we have belongs
to the Lord, we take him into consideration when making our choice. We ask him,
“Can I buy this expensive red sports car with your money?” Now there may be
circumstances that would warrant an expensive sports car. Perhaps the Lord
would respond positively, but it is more likely he would say, “Why don’t you
buy a good reliable midsize car—throw a sunroof in if you
must—and then with the savings we can educate ten Central
American students through the Perpetual Education Fund.”

A similar
line of thought would be used when buying a home. We consider our family size,
circumstances, wants, needs, and necessities. We take these factors to the Lord
and ask, “Can I buy this
          -thousand-dollar
home with your money? If I buy this home I will pay so many dollars in interest
over the thirty years of the mortgage. It has this many square feet on this
size of lot.” If, in being perfectly honest with ourselves, and truly believing
all belongs to God, we feel the Lord approves of our choice we move ahead. If,
on the other hand, we perceive him saying, “Why don’t you downsize a bit and
use the savings for humanitarian aid or to reduce the number of years it will
take to pay off the mortgage, or lessen the stress on your family budget, or
not require two incomes to support the payments? Could you not be satisfied
with a smaller home which will, in addition, save money on upkeep and
utilities?”

In other
words the normal question of “Can I afford this?” or the thought “I want this”
becomes secondary. The primary question will always be, “Can I do this with
your money, Lord?” We don’t want to push this too far to include every little
purchase we make, but certainly in larger decisions involving significant
amounts or long payment schedules or things that fall into the category of
luxury we would want to use this line of reasoning. This does not mean we still
won’t have a few dainties, but they will be much reduced. We will tend to save
ourselves the necessity of paying interest with the Lord’s money in order to
have an immediate gratification of our material desires. Cash, not installment,
would be the dominant rule in our lives. Most debt, out of consideration for
the Lord and our sense of stewardship over his gifts, would be eliminated.

The reason we
seldom engage in these types of conversations with the Lord rests in the
difficulty of realizing nothing we have is truly our own. We think it is ours
or give a passing nod to the idea it is the Lord’s, but believe that how it is
disposed of should be at our discretion. After all, did we not earn it?
Consecration is one of the most difficult laws to truly practice because it
goes against so much of our societal assumptions and individual thought
processes.

Perhaps
another illustration will be helpful in viewing financial matters through the
lens of consecration and the overall fairness it tries to anticipate. This may
help us understand why the Lord has said he wants us to “have in abundance. But
it is not given that one man should possess that which is above another”
(D&C 49:19–20). Given the varying talents and financial wisdom among
people, this utopian ideal seems nearly impossible. Even if we could reach a
perfectly equal distribution, in a very short time the inequalities would
surface again. Consecration addresses this contradictory dilemma.

More than
thirty years ago, I purchased a house in Colorado. Not long afterward, I heard
on the news the salaries of the top three executives of the bank that held most
of our subdivision’s mortgages. The three salaries totaled more than one
million dollars. They each made about $400,000 per year. (I do not know how
much that would translate to in today’s figures, but certainly a great deal
more.) As coincidence would have it, that very night I was scheduled to teach a
class on the law of consecration. I brought up the salary figures, and the
class took over from there. We had an interesting discussion. The students
decided to find out how many families’ interest payments on their home loans
supported what the students called the “excess reimbursement” of the three
executives. They theorized that the bankers should each earn a yearly salary of
between $80,000 and $100,000. Many of my students stated they did not think
they would ever make that much in a year, but they wanted to overcompensate the
bankers rather than undercompensate them. This left a little under a
million dollars a year in excess payment. Because I knew roughly the amount of interest
each family in my neighborhood paid to finance their mortgages, we divided that
amount into the excess payment figure. The entire interest payments of more
than 130 families were going to support the excess salaries of three
individuals. I think we were all a little shocked by the numbers.

Now, I don’t
think the students were trying to change the economic system of the United
States. What the class discovered in the banking world could be duplicated many
times over in other fields. The issue might turn on what those three bankers
did with the excess. Consecration would anticipate they would do good with the
residue. It would also expect them to take into account how many people’s labor
was supporting their superabundance. This would temper their thoughts and help
them be generous.

Interestingly
enough, about a year later a local company fell on hard times and laid off a
number of its workers. Some were neighbors living in our subdivision. Many lost
their homes to foreclosure. They just could not make those interest payments.
There are economic factors that surely complicate situations such as those we
discussed in class that evening, but in the Lord’s eyes they might not be as
complex as we think. We are asked by the Lord to operate our stewardships as
best we can and then do all the good we can with what remains after our own,
generally basic needs are met. In the meantime, that we may one day pass
through the needle’s eye, our safest route may be to keep saying: “I’m trying
very hard not to be rich! I must keep the back door open!”

“WHAT DESIREST THOU?”

All that we
have said previously must be viewed through the lens of the Lord’s character.
He has tremendous trust in his Saints. Consecration assumes we are agents unto
the Lord. The very word suggests agency, and that entails choices. The Lord is
gracious in allowing us to make them. He also tells us that men are “agents
unto themselves” (D&C 58:28). “You run your stewardships the best way you
can,” he says. “I’ve called you to operate this part of my kingdom for me.” The
word “called” suggests invitation. He doesn’t expect us to ask about everything
but to take the principles he’s given and apply them. This is also implied in
the giving of “keys.” This gives us very wide latitude. He is teaching us responsibility.

Throughout
the scriptures we read of many instances where the Lord asks his people, “What
desirest thou?” (D&C 7:1). I believe this can apply even in fiscal,
temporal matters. When Peter and John were asked this question they gave
different answers, but the Lord said to them both, “Verily I say unto you, ye
shall both have according to your desires, for ye both joy in that which ye
have desired” (D&C 7:8). The bond most often used to describe our
relationship with Christ is that of bride and bridegroom. In the love they feel
for each other, the desire to please is awakened. We say to the Lord, “What
desirest thou of me? What would you have me do with all that you have given me?
I wish to please you.” He answers equally, “What desirest thou? What would make
you happy? I wish to please you.” In the mutual hope to please one another, the
proper decisions are made. This, perhaps above all other things, will see us
safely through the needle’s eye.

Sand Castles

The ultimate
rendering unto Caesar applies to all earthly things. Only the refined spirit or
soul of the individual, shaped and perfected through the choices of life,
returns to God. Perhaps we can conclude with a parable from the literature of
Buddhism. Realizing the truth of what the Buddha taught in this tiny parable
will help us make our passage through the needle’s eye. Buddhism is centrally
concerned with the cessation of improper desires, particularly those centering
on the self. That is what Nirvana consists of—the blowing out
or extinguishing of desire. There are different versions and translations of
the story, but it is essentially as follows:

A number of
children are playing on the shore building sand castles. Each thinks his or
hers is the finest, and they all defend their portion of shoreline, beach sand,
and what they have created out of it with vigor, saying, “This one is mine!”
They keep their castles separate and apart, frequently comparing theirs with
the other children’s handiwork. In their own minds, there is never any question
about which territory or which castle belongs to any individual child. All are
sure of their proprietary ownership. All are building feverishly. Occasionally
there are quarrels when one child kicks over another child’s castle. Then,
enraged, the owner retaliates, sometimes with the support of other children.
And so the day wears on, each child enlarging or improving or changing his
castle according to his whims and wishes. “This is mine! No one else may have
it! Keep away! Mine is better! Don’t touch my castle!” they cry to each other.
In time the day draws to a close, and evening descends upon the playing children.
As it gets dark, the children decide they have played enough and it is time to
go home. No one now cares about their castles. They turn their faces from the
shore and walk away, leaving their creations to the wind and the rising tide.

If Elder
Melvin J. Ballard is right—and I firmly believe he
is—then the turning away as the sun sets on the shore for the
Latter-day Saints will not be difficult. Let the world squabble over
the height or splendor of their castles. We are preparing for mansions on high
built by the Savior himself, which we will inherit because we have passed
through the needle’s eye.
