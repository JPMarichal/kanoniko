# Widow’s Mites and Washed Feet

### Widow’s Mites and Washed Feet
S. Michael Wilcox, What Scriptures Teach Us about Prosperity (Salt Lake City 2010)
 
 

Widow’s Mites and Washed Feet

“RENDER THEREFORE UNTO CAESAR”

Though the
majority of the Savior’s teachings regarding wealth and contentment are
contained in the parables, there are other offerings in the Gospels where we
can perceive the Savior’s attitudes and thoughts. A brief examination of some
of these teachings and stories may prove helpful. One of the Savior’s most
famous and oft-quoted statements centers on the payment of taxes. Yet
there is more in this statement than a charge to be circumspect in our civic
obligations.

A delegation
of the chief priests and scribes attempted to trap Jesus with a question
regarding the very unpopular payment of tribute to Caesar. Was this lawful for
them to do or no? “Shew me a penny,” Jesus responded. Taking the coin in his
hand, he then asked, “Whose image and superscription hath it? They answered and
said, Caesar’s. And he said unto them, Render therefore unto Caesar the things
which be Caesar’s, and unto God the things which be God’s” (Luke 20:24–25).

In typical
fashion he taught more than a surface reading indicates. The phrase used by
Christ has become proverbial for our need to separate things belonging to this
world, and our dues to them, from the things belonging to God, and our duty to
him. It is pragmatic and ambiguous enough to cover a multitude of issues. The
very tone of the Savior’s answer places a huge distance between the two
obligations. There is no doubt in his reply which choice should have the
priority and which weighed very lightly in his mind. This does not mean he felt
we could slight our duties to the state; rather the things the state demands or
expects of us are not of especial value. Give them the money they ask. It is an
insignificant thing. The name of earthly kingdoms and worldly men is engraved
thereon. God wants something infinitely more valuable from you. There is a
nonchalance in his attitude to the coin that we can almost picture while
reading the account.

A similar
attitude is suggested when Peter was challenged by the Savior’s detractors
about his payment of the temple tribute. Though he explained to Peter that as
the Son of God he did not need to present the expected coin, “Notwithstanding,
lest we should offend them, go thou to the sea, and cast an hook, and take up the
fish that first cometh up; and when thou hast opened his mouth, thou shalt find
a piece of money: that take, and give unto them for me and thee” (Matthew
17:27). It’s not worth an argument about rights, so let’s just pay the money
and get on with life.

One final
thought on giving Caesar his expected due. Notice the word the Savior chose to
use was “render.” Render means to give back, to restore, or return. The coin
belonged to an earthly king—give it back to him. Our hearts
belong to a heavenly king—let them not be set on the coin.

“TWO MITES”

In Luke, soon
after the encounter with the chief priests and scribes, we read the story of
the poor widow and her two mites. The positioning of these stories carries
perspective which can’t be ignored. The verse immediately preceding the story
of the two mites contains the last of Jesus’ denunciation of the men who had
just tempted him with the Roman tribute. These were the type of men “which
devour widows’ houses” (Luke 20:47).

There are
times when the coinage of the world is given to God. We learn something about
how he esteems those gifts and the spirit in which they are given from the
Savior’s comments about the widow on this occasion:

“And he
looked up, and saw the rich men casting their gifts into the treasury. And he
saw also a certain poor widow casting in thither two mites. And he said, Of a
truth I say unto you, that this poor widow hath cast in more than they all: For
all these have of their abundance cast in unto the offerings of God: but she of
her penury hath cast in all the living that she had” (Luke 21:1–4). This
is a simple and poignant story, but it tells us that gifts to the Lord may be
measured not by the amount offered but by what is left over after the gift has
been made. She gave two mites because it was not acceptable to give only
one—one mite was considered too tiny an offering for even the
very poorest of Israelites. But the Master noticed, as he does all the gifts
offered to him. Herein we learn that the very soul of giving to God consists in
self-denial. It is important to understand that the Savior did not
belittle or 
disparage the contributions of the rich men who gave of their abundance; these
gifts were acceptable also. He placed the two mites of the widow on a higher
level because of the sacrifice involved in their bestowal. The Lord indicated
as much when he addressed Oliver Granger with the following words: “His
sacrifice shall be more sacred unto me than his increase” (D&C 117:13). The
element of sacrifice adds a dimension to the sanctity of the gift.

Perhaps this
is part of the meaning of giving “with real intent,” as Mormon indicated in his
address to his people. If a man “offereth a gift, . . . except he
shall do it with real intent it profiteth him nothing. . . . If a man
being evil giveth a gift, he doeth it grudgingly; wherefore it is counted unto
him the same as if he had retained the gift” (Moroni 7:6–8). The heart is
all that matters, not the amount. Remember that, as Jesus taught, we worship a
God who notices even an act as simple as giving a cup of cold water to a child
and rewards it (see Matthew 10:42).

What can we
say, then, to those who do have abundance and wish to give as did the poor
widow? There are many worthy souls who are of this mind. I suppose the answer lies
in the self-denial of the widow’s gift. We might ask, “Are there
things I would like to have or do which I forbear, because I wish to cast more
into the treasury? What have I denied myself, which might be in and of itself
good and proper, because of my consecrations? She gave of her ‘living,’ wherein
can I do likewise?”

In Mere
Christianity, C. S.
Lewis addressed this issue with his usual practicality. “I do not believe one
can settle how much we ought to give. I am afraid the only safe rule is to give
more than we can spare. In other words, if our expenditure on comforts,
luxuries, amusements, etc., is up to the standard common among those with the
same income as our own, we are probably giving away too little. If our
charities do not at all pinch or hamper us, I should say they are too small.
There ought to be things we should like to do and cannot do because our
charitable expenditures exclude them.”1 

“A POUND OF OINTMENT OF SPIKENARD, VERY COSTLY”

There is a
certain danger in that last thought. Carried to extremes it creates an
asceticism which is neither necessary nor particularly pleasing to God. Poverty
for the sake of poverty is not a virtue. It may be our lot in life, but I know
of no scripture that presents it as spiritually desirable. Balance is
everything. Jesus once said, “Foxes have holes, and birds of the air have
nests; but the Son of man hath not where to lay his head” (Luke 9:58), but
there is no indication that he was recommending this as a standard for all.
Therefore, it is interesting to study the Savior’s actions and words on a
number of occasions in the Gospels. He was not against happy, celebratory joy.
We see this in his willingness to turn water to wine at the marriage feast of
Cana. One of the criticisms leveled at him by his antagonists was his
too-free acceptance of social gatherings, of feasts and dinners: “The
Son of man is come eating and drinking; and ye say, Behold a gluttonous man,
and a winebibber, a friend of publicans and sinners!” (Luke 7:34).

With this in
mind, we can examine one of the most beautiful moments in the Savior’s life. A
parting gift was bestowed on him by a loving hand, that of Mary, the sister of
Martha. Jesus came to Bethany just before his atoning hours. “There they made
him a supper. . . . Then took Mary a pound of ointment of spikenard, very
costly, and anointed
the feet of Jesus, and wiped his feet with her hair: and the house was filled
with the odour of the ointment. Then saith one of his disciples, Judas Iscariot
. . . Why was not this ointment sold for three hundred pence, and
given to the poor?” (John 12:2–5; emphasis added). Three hundred pence
was a considerable sum. A pence, as we have seen in the parable of the laborers
in the vineyard was equivalent to a twelve-hour-day’s
wages.

In Mark’s
account, Judas was not the only one who was troubled by the huge gift, and the
value of the spikenard 
was higher than three hundred pence. It was also contained in an alabaster box
that Mary broke in order to pour the ointment over both Jesus’ head and feet.
“And there were some that had indignation within themselves, and said, Why was
this waste of the ointment made? For it might have been sold for more than
three hundred pence, and have been given to the poor. And they murmured against
her” (Mark 14:4–5). Jesus was always sensitive to the feelings of others,
particularly women. The accusations of waste leveled at Mary were wounding.
Jesus soothed them by saying, “Let her alone; why trouble ye her? she hath
wrought a good work on me. For ye have the poor with you always, and whensoever
ye will ye may do them good: but me ye have not always” (Mark 14:6–7).

There are
times when a lavish, even extravagant, gift offered in an outpouring of love is
proper and good. This is certainly true of what we give to God in gratitude for
his blessings, but it may be equally true of those we
love—parents for children, a husband for his wife. This is not
always waste, even if it may appear to others to have been money unwisely
spent. The unity this type of gift creates between people may validate the
expense. This is not to say that love and a deep bond between two people can be

purchased; it is the intent that is all-important. Even the poor do
not take precedence in such cases. The poor will always be there for us to
help; a spouse or a child may not. We need not be troubled in conscience. God
may pronounce the work good. Perhaps Mary sensed in a deeper way than the
others present at the supper that her Lord would soon be taken from her and she
needed to express her devotion in a way none could doubt, especially the man
who was the recipient of her largess. It is helpful to remember that those we
love may not always be with us. This realization intensifies love in a unique
manner and moves us to demonstrate that love in ways that cannot be
doubted—as Mary did in Bethany while the odor of the spikenard
filled the room.

Personally,
it took me a while to realize the truthfulness of what Christ was allowing in
his acceptance of Mary’s gift and how it might apply in my own family. It is
sometimes easier to spend our earnings on things rather than moments. I have
noticed this is particularly true in men, but there is something to be said
about moments spent together, about sharing time and place, about allowing the
spikenard to fill the room even though the aroma cannot last. How endearing
that one moment in time must have been to Mary in the years that came and, I
would assume, to the Savior, though he had departed. As I matured I came to
understand the value of memories, of what I sometimes call “smelling the
spikenard.” Things can wear out, fade, be stolen or lost, but memories abide
and, as time passes, grow sweeter. Each year my wife and I travel to Cedar
City, Utah, to enjoy the Shakespeare festival. The days we spend in southern
Utah enjoying live theatre are not inexpensive. In the early years of
attending, I needed to save for months to purchase the tickets, the
bed-and-breakfast room, and other necessities. There were times
I thought I was not spending my money wisely or charitably. “There are people
in the world who are hungry,” I reflected. It seemed such an extravagant four
days. But as I look back with fondness, I can think of no better investment 
I made in my marriage than those times spent in Cedar City.

We have taken
our children to many countries around the world, sharing with them the
cultures, religions, arts, and peoples of the earth. Great expense is involved,
and occasionally I wonder if I should be giving more to the Perpetual Education
or Humanitarian Aid funds of the Church, but the memories are sweet and the
bonds deeper, and I hope the Lord would pronounce his acceptance of these
decisions as he did with Mary. There is value in a memory. There is worth in
drawing people together, occasionally even in “very costly” ways. We cannot do
this all the time lest the spikenard lose its poignancy, and these decisions
need to be weighed with other principles taught in the scriptures, but the
possibility that they are acceptable in the eyes of the Lord is
comforting.

We can see
that there are no hard and fast rules when deciding how to expend the wealth
with which we are blessed. We must apply the scriptures in a manner proper to
the situation and under the guidance of the Holy Spirit. If we study the scriptures
we will find therein all the possibilities presented; then with inspiration we
may choose the proper verse, parable, or story to suit each decision, each
need, without rationalization or apology.

“HALF OF MY GOODS I GIVE TO THE POOR”

Before leaving
the Savior’s teachings on riches as found in the Gospels, let us acquaint
ourselves anew with one rich man who, though despised because of his
occupation, pleased the Lord. His name was Zacchaeus, “chief among the
publicans” of Jericho. He is introduced to us with the curt phrase, “and he was
rich.” Publicans gathered taxes, and because the system was rife with 
corruption, it was generally assumed that any publican who was rich got his
wealth through extortion, chicanery, and cheating. In the case of Zacchaeus,
Jesus found a righteous soul. Because he was “little of stature,” Zacchaeus
could not see above the heads of the crowds that lined the streets. He
therefore climbed into a sycamore tree to watch the Savior pass. Of all the
people who could have been honored by Christ’s presence that day, Zacchaeus was
chosen. Jesus was as sensitive to a wealthy man as he was to a poor widow or to
Mary. He could see good in this publican and was aware of his critics’
censures. But Zacchaeus was not the man his neighbors judged him to be. “And
Zacchaeus stood, and said unto the Lord; Behold, Lord, the half of my goods I
give to the poor; and if I have taken any thing from any man by false
accusation, I restore him fourfold” (Luke 19:8).

There is some
debate on the words of Zacchaeus. Some translations indicate that due to the
Savior’s acceptance of him, he would “here and now” give half of his goods to
the poor, and if he had defrauded anyone in the past, he would pay him back
four times the sum (see NIV, or New English Bible Luke 19:8, for example).
These translations suggest it was the Savior’s recognition and approval of
Zacchaeus that induced the change. Jesus responded to Zacchaeus’s vow with the
words, “This day is salvation come to this house, forsomuch as he also is a son
of Abraham. For the Son of man is come to seek and to save that which was lost”
(Luke 19:9–10). Whether Zacchaeus was always this way or became such as a
result of his day with Christ, we find in him a man that seems to have been
able to pass through the needle’s eye. Herein is there hope for us all.
