# Thorny Ground, Wedding Feasts, and the Eleventh-Hour Laborer

### Thorny Ground, Wedding Feasts, and the Eleventh-Hour Laborer
S. Michael Wilcox, What Scriptures Teach Us about Prosperity (Salt Lake City 2010)
 
 

Thorny Ground, Wedding Feasts, and the Eleventh-Hour Laborer

“SOME FELL AMONG THORNS”

We have
examined the three major parables of Jesus with themes directly tied to
possessions and wealth. It is remarkable, however, how many of the Savior’s
other parables deal with the issue at hand, even if the main objective was not
to teach us how to properly respond to the lure of capital. Indeed, there are
few that can’t be applied in such a manner as to supply wisdom for our passage
through the needle’s eye. The first parable in the Gospels we encounter, that of
the sower, is just such an example.

There are
four types of soil into which the good seed of the word is
sown—by the wayside, onto stony ground, among the thorns, and
into the good soil. Jesus commented on each type. The one we are interested in
is the seed that fell among thorns. “And some fell among thorns; and the thorns
sprung up, and choked them” (Matthew 13:7). According to this sentence, the
good seed and the thorns had an equal chance to grow. The thorns were not
already thriving. They did not have a head start. Jesus specifically said they
had “sprung up.” Luke’s account says the thorns “sprang up with it” (Luke 8:7; emphasis added). I suppose
for most of us the sowing of both the good seed of the Lord’s gospel and the
thorny desires for possessions is accomplished in our childhood. Since little
children generally are not too caught up in the pursuit of things, we might
even say the good seed, if properly placed by a wise parent, has the head
start. However, the thorns are fast-growing, and can quickly catch up
and choke the word.

If we combine
all three of the Gospel accounts, we see that the thorns represent four things.
In Matthew they are “the care of this world, and the deceitfulness of riches”
(Matthew 13:22). Deceitful because they promise what they cannot
deliver—happiness and fulfillment of a lasting nature. I think
it is instructive that the word “care” is associated with riches. Care can be
defined in one sense as “desire,” but care can also mean “worry.” Tremendous effort
is usually expended in the pursuit of riches and much anxiety is devoted to
both their preservation and accumulation.

Mark also
speaks of “the cares of this world, and the deceitfulness of riches.” He then
adds another phrase describing the thorns: “The lusts of other things entering
in” (Mark 4:19). “Entering in” is an interesting phrase to pause at for a
moment. Where, we might ask, are they entering? The heart? The mind? The life?
However we answer that question, it is apparent that they are in an area where
they do not belong. It is an invasion of sorts. There may be a proper place for
considering what “other things” money can buy, but it has no place alongside
“the word of the kingdom” (Matthew 13:19). It does not share the same space. It
may be too vigorous for the good seed. Lust is a strong word and hints that the
“other things” are of the forbidden kind. Their ability to tempt us may be
possible only when we have the means to procure them, hence their attachment to
riches. A lack of funds may often be the best defense against certain types of
lusts. In some ways the wealthy may face steeper temptations than the poor or
at least temptations of a different kind because they can satisfy them.
Ironically, praying for assets may equal praying for greater temptation.

In Luke we
add a word that removes the forbidden connotations inherent in “lust.” He, too,
includes the idea of cares and riches. His addition is “pleasures of this life”
(Luke 8:14). One of the more seductive traits of money is its ability to supply
various kinds of pleasures. We need not think of all of these pleasures as
forbidden or evil. Some may be perfectly acceptable and dangerous only when
they command a higher priority in our lives than the things that should have
the highest priority. Wealth offers us more of these
pleasures—which may become important to us only when they
become possible to have. They are not destructive, just diversionary. We may
ask ourselves when considering legitimate pleasures: “Are they edifying,
constructive, prone to building character?” Wealth can provide opportunity for
greater refinement, in which case the pleasure would not be considered a thorn,
but something “lovely, or of good report or praiseworthy” (Articles of Faith
1:13).

So we face
the prospect of needing to weed out the thorns of cares, riches, lusts of other
things, and pleasures. We must not 
underestimate the risk of the thorns choking out the good seed. In many a soul
they become more powerful than the seed. They grow faster, because it is easier
to find temporary happiness in riches and pleasures than in the
slower-growing, deeper-rooted word of the Lord. In Alma’s
version of this same parable found in Alma 32, he cautioned us about expecting
the precious fruit of faith to grow immediately. We need to be faithful,
diligent, and patient in nourishing the tree. The fruit comes in time. But Alma
assures us it is sweeter, more precious, and more pure than anything else. In
today’s world, with the credit card frenzy of instant gratification, no wonder
the thorns overshadow the slower-growing gospel seed. Let us
remember, however, weeds may grow quickly, but you can’t get much nourishment
from them. Wealth may be a harvest which is all stalk and
leaves—no nutritional value whatsoever.

It is
sometimes helpful when reading the scriptures to visualize what we are reading.
If we imagine the various soils, different pictures come to mind. On the
hard-beaten wayside, we see no plants at all. The birds have eaten
the seed and nothing grows. On the stony ground, the first shoots thrive early,
but in the heat of the sun they die. We see brown leaves and dry stems. But
among the thorns, the plant is still alive, but lost and less than it should be
in the tangle of choking weeds.

Both Matthew
and Mark state that the problem with the plant is in its inability to produce
fruit, not in remaining alive. The thorns “choke the word, and he becometh
unfruitful” (Matthew 13:22; see also Mark 4:19). Luke renders this same passage
a little differently: “And that which fell among thorns are they, which, when
they have heard, go forth,
and are choked with cares and riches and pleasures of this life, and bring
no fruit to perfection”
(Luke 8:14; emphasis added). I am intrigued with the expression “go forth.” It
seems the plant is engaged in the Lord’s service to a degree. The plant we see
in our mind’s eye in Luke’s description is actually putting forth the
beginnings of fruit, 
but there is not enough strength to ripen it. It can’t come to 
“perfection,” an interesting word since that is the word Jesus used with the
rich young ruler. “If thou wilt be perfect, go and sell that thou hast, and
give to the poor” (Matthew 19:21). In Luke’s description, the “good seed,” the
Church member, desires to bless the kingdom, to go forth, and is even striving
to ripen the fruit, but there is not sufficient strength to both deal with
cares, riches, and pleasures and be perfected as the end result of a good seed,
and the fruit remains immature.

The saddest
aspect of a person whose seed falls on thorny ground is clearly seen in Alma’s
use of the seed metaphor. The fruit is for the palate and nourishment of each
individual. They are growing the tree for themselves. If the fruit does not
reach a ripened stage, “this is not because . . . the fruit thereof
would not be desirable; but it is because . . . ye will not nourish the tree,
therefore ye cannot have the fruit thereof.” In time “ye shall feast upon this
fruit even until ye are filled, that ye hunger not. . . . Then, my
brethren, ye shall reap the rewards of your faith, and your diligence, and
patience . . . waiting for the tree to bring forth fruit unto you”
(Alma 32:39, 42–43).

This fruit
satisfies all hungers, at least all real hungers—for part of
the pretense of riches lies in their ability to create hungers that were never
there before and are neither necessary nor natural to a son or daughter of God.
I sometimes wonder what fruits of the gospel yet await me that I have not yet
brought to perfection, either because I have allowed thorns to enter
in or because they take time to season. Yet, by and by I will know their
sweetness. 

COME UNTO THE MARRIAGE

The idea that
the gospel satisfies our hunger is a common theme in the symbolic language of
the scriptures. Feast
is the verb that is often used; for instance, in Nephi’s famous exhortation:
“Feast upon the words of Christ; for behold, the words of Christ will tell you
all things what ye should do” (2 Nephi 32:3). The word, or the teachings of
Christ, or the truth, is generally 
understood to represent the main course at the feast. In the parable of the
marriage feast, Jesus speaks of what keeps people from heeding his gracious
invitation to attend:

“The kingdom
of heaven is like unto a certain king, which made a marriage for his son, and sent
forth his servants to call them that were bidden to the wedding: and they would
not come” (Matthew 22:2–3). It is understood that the guests had
previously been invited, had indicated their willingness to attend, and were
only waiting for the final notification that “all things are ready” (Matthew
22:4). This is consistent with Luke’s account also. “A certain man made a great
supper, and bade many: And sent his servant at supper time to say to them that
were bidden, Come; for all things are now ready” (Luke 14:16–17). In his
willingness to share the sumptuous meal with his now-hesitant guests,
the Lord makes a second request. “Again, he sent forth other servants, saying,
Tell them which are bidden, Behold, I have prepared my dinner: my oxen and my
fatlings are killed, and all things are ready: come unto the marriage” (Matthew
22:4).

The feast is
a supper of revealed truth, doctrines, and wisdom. Yet the originally requested
guests refuse the invitation. Why? “But they made light of it, and went their
ways, one to his farm, another to his merchandise” (Matthew 22:5). Luke gives
us a slight variation on their refusal, supplying more details. “And they all
with one consent began to make excuse. The first said unto him, I have bought a
piece of ground, and I must needs go and see it: I pray thee have me excused.
And another said, I have bought five yoke of oxen, and I go to prove them: I
pray thee have me excused” (Luke 14:18–19).

There are
more important things to attend to. Business, real estate, new equipment take
priority over a celebration. These are not contrary guests. They don’t oppose
the menu. They understand how enjoyable it would be feasting with the
charitable host. They would gladly come if it was a little more convenient, but
the reality of their livelihood makes it impossible. Could they please be
excused? Never mind that it is a king who has asked for their presence at his
feast. This is polite apathy, not a hostile snub. Sadly for these first guests,
they eventually miss the feast completely.

The king,
unwilling for his dinner to go uneaten, sends his servants “into the highways,
and as many as ye shall find, bid to the marriage” (Matthew 22:9). The servants
return with “the poor, and the maimed, and the halt, and the blind” (Luke
14:21), who understand the nature of the feast and willingly respond.

One of the
great dangers of making money is in its ability to distract us from receiving
from the Lord the one thing, above all other things, he wishes to grant us in
mortality—that is truth. On this point he is emphatic. However, there is a certain
intoxication in procuring wealth: a sense that one is doing something
important, is becoming someone important. This satisfaction is in addition to
and outside of the quite obvious pleasures and comforts that wealth can
procure. The search for light and truth, for spiritual wisdom, often can’t
compete with the exhilaration of accumulating profits in the bank or watching a
corporation grow. The Lord is aware of this excitement, so in the early dawn of
the Restoration he issued a countering challenge: “Seek not for riches but for
wisdom, and behold, the mysteries of God shall be 
unfolded unto you, and then shall you be made rich. Behold, he that hath
eternal life is rich” (D&C 6:7; see also 11:7). The ultimate blessing of
seeking knowledge is eternal life. The pursuit of wealth, with all it procures,
ends at death when we leave everything behind. It has no relevancy in the
eternal view. That is why Jesus was so indifferent to it. “Seek to bring forth
and establish the cause of Zion,” the Lord tells us in Doctrine and Covenants
6:6. “Desire” to be the “means of doing much good” (D&C 6:8). Wisdom, the
mysteries of God, the cause of Zion, doing good, these are what the Lord would
have us apply our minds and energies to.

Hamlet said
as much while musing on the purposes of life:

What is a
man,

If his
chief good and market of his time

Be but
to sleep and feed? a beast, no more.

Sure, he
that made us with such large discourse,

Looking
before and after, gave us not

That
capability and godlike reason

To fust in us unus’d.1

“A SUPPER OF THE HOUSE OF THE LORD”

We get an
idea of the latter-day nature of the Savior’s feast in a later
section of the Doctrine and Covenants where the parable of the wedding feast is
applied to the Restoration. The Lord tells the early Saints they are “honored
in laying the foundation [of Zion]. . . . And also that a feast of
fat things might be prepared for the poor; yea, a feast of fat things, of wine
on the lees well refined. . . . Yea, a supper of the house of the
Lord, well prepared, unto which all nations shall be invited. First, the rich
and the learned, the wise and the noble; and after that cometh the day of my
power; then shall the poor, the lame, and the blind, and the deaf, come in unto
the marriage of the Lamb, and partake of the supper of the Lord, prepared for
the great day to come” (D&C 58:7–11).

The feast is
a “supper of the house of the Lord.” It is to the temple that we go to receive
the “well refined” truths, the “well 
prepared” knowledge. Having served as a bishop for five years and then in a
stake presidency for ten, I always felt sad to contemplate the number of people
who had been endowed and sealed in the temple that no longer held temple
recommends. In any given ward the percentage could be as high as 30 to 40
percent. With the exception of a very small number who were working through
moral problems, the vast majority of them were having difficulty paying
tithing. They were good people, they had testimonies, they believed in the
temple and in its ordinances, but overreaching with credit cards or mortgage
debt or car payments had taken its toll.

Nephi’s
brother Jacob, while speaking to his people issued a personal invitation to
them that they come to the feast. He relied on the beautiful poetry of Isaiah
to enhance that invitation. It is evident that both Jacob and Isaiah knew the
greatest distraction to dining with the Lord at his bountiful table:

“Come, my
brethren, every one that thirsteth, come ye to the waters; and he that hath no
money, come buy and eat; yea, come buy wine and milk without money and without
price. Wherefore, do not spend money for that which is of no worth, nor your
labor for that which cannot satisfy. Hearken diligently unto me, and remember
the words which I have spoken; and come unto the Holy One of Israel, and feast
upon that which perisheth not, neither can be corrupted, and let your soul
delight in fatness” (2 Nephi 9:50–51). Beautiful advice for those who
would pass through the needle’s eye.

“HE SHALL GIRD HIMSELF”

If we can
resist the temptation to excuse ourselves from attending the feast of spiritual
wisdom in our hurry to look after our merchandise or prove our oxen or see our
newly acquired property, we will participate in the ultimate feast. The Lord,
in Doctrine and Covenants 101, spoke of that banquet to encourage the early
Saints when they were passing through the trials of Missouri. This scripture’s
promises are extended to us in our own personal challenges. It speaks of those things
that the Father most wishes you and me as his children to receive: “When the
Lord shall come, he shall reveal all things—Things which have
passed, and hidden things which no man knew, things of the earth, by which it
was made, and the purpose and the end thereof—Things most
precious, things that are above, and things that are beneath, things that are
in the earth, and upon the earth, and in heaven. . . . Wherefore,
fear not . . . for in this world your joy is not full, but in me your
joy is full. Therefore, care not for the body, neither the life of the body;
but care for the soul, and for the life of the soul. And seek the face of the
Lord always, that in patience ye may possess your souls, and ye shall have
eternal life” (D&C 101:32–34, 36–38).

I suppose
that all of us have questions we would like to ask the Lord that relate to the
promised knowledge spoken of in these verses. All the answers will be supplied.
Where the dinosaurs fit into the plan for this earth. How there can be no
beginning. What intelligence is. What happened in the first thirty years of the
Savior’s life. And on and on—that will be a feast indeed.

In the
preceding verse, I do not think the Lord means for us to cease to care for our
physical bodies. There is ample proof in the scriptures to the contrary. I do
think he is telling us not to place so much emphasis on the luxuries and
comforts and pleasures that conform to the world’s
thinking—usually to the exclusion of the “life of the soul.”

The first
parable we studied was that of the rich fool found in Luke 12. Immediately
after relating that parable and commenting on it, the Savior directed the
thoughts of his listeners to this definitive, final supper, as if to say,
“Conquer the inconsistencies and lure of wealth, and I will reserve a place for
you at my table.” Pay particular attention to who is sitting at the table and
who is serving the meal:

“For where
your treasure is, there will your heart be also. Let your loins be girded
about, and your lights burning; and ye yourselves like unto men that wait for
their lord, when he will return from the wedding; that when he cometh and
knocketh, they may open unto him immediately [—without making any excuses
because of other commitments]. Blessed are those servants, whom the lord when
he cometh shall find watching: verily I say unto you, that he [Christ] shall
gird himself, and make them [you and me] to sit down to meat, and will come
forth and serve them” (Luke 12:34–37).

Divine
humility reveals itself in this picture. You and I are at the table while the
Savior serves the meal. If we wish to gain even a tiny comprehension of what
that will be like, we need only read of the soul-expanding teachings
of Jesus given to the Apostles at the Last Supper as found in the gospel of
John (see John 14–17). How often have we read of celebrity or political
fund-raising banquets costing thousands of dollars per plate to attend? What
would we give to attend a dinner with Jesus as our host? Yet, as Isaiah
confirms, it is all free. We need only heed the invitation to “come ye, buy,
and eat; yea, come, buy wine and milk without money and without price” (Isaiah
55:1). The banquet is without price not only because it is offered graciously
but because no price, in worldly terms, can be placed upon it that could
compensate for its worth.

ELEVENTH-HOUR LABORERS

The parable
most directly tied to Christ’s comment on how difficult it was for a rich man
to pass through the needle’s eye is the parable of the laborers in the
vineyard. After the rich young ruler “went away sorrowful: for he had great
possessions” (Matthew 19:22), Peter asked Jesus a question: “Behold, we have
forsaken all, and followed thee; what shall we have therefore?” (Matthew
19:27). In other words, “We did what this young man could not do; what will our
reward be?” This was true. One might point out to Peter that, as a fisherman,
his possessions were not as great or as numerous as the rich young ruler’s.
Nevertheless, Peter did
leave when his nets were full, not empty—immediately after the Savior had
miraculously granted his fishermen disciples a bounteous catch.

The Savior
responded to Peter’s question in two ways. First he promised him “everlasting
life” and “an hundredfold” more than had been sacrificed (Matthew 19:29).
Second, he told the following parable. It is a bit of a wake-up call
to those concerned with what they are receiving in relation to how much they
think they are worth, and what they see as the disparity between what 
others are worth and what they are receiving. Though the parable’s main message
deals with other, more eternal themes, it fits nicely into our present inquiry.
It is prefaced with Christ’s statement: “But many that are first shall be last;
and the last shall be first” (Matthew 19:30). This is a tale for those of us
who consider ourselves to be “first.” There is nothing like a high salary or a
lot of money to make one feel they are deserving of that classification. If we
are to pass through the needle’s eye, this tendency must be mastered.

“The kingdom
of heaven is like unto a man that is an householder, which went out early in
the morning [6:00 am] to hire
labourers into his vineyard. And when he had agreed with the labourers for a
penny a day, he sent them into his vineyard” (Matthew 20:1–2). A penny
was a standard wage for twelve hours of labor. But as the day passed, the
householder saw others “standing idle in the marketplace.” He sent them to
labor also, telling them “whatsoever is right I will give you” (Matthew
20:3–4). Thus he sent different groups of men to the vineyard at the
third hour, the sixth hour, the ninth hour, and finally the eleventh hour (9:00
am; 12:00, 3:00, and 5:00 pm, respectively).

They are each
given a penny, a full day’s wage, by the generous householder, no matter when
they went into the vineyard. This, however, created a problem. “But when the
first came, they supposed that they should have received more. . . .
They murmured against the goodman of the house, saying, These last have wrought
but one hour, and thou hast made them equal unto us, which have borne the burden and heat
of the day. But he answered one of them, and said, Friend, I do thee no wrong:
didst not thou agree with me for a penny? Take that thine is, and go thy way: I
will give unto this last, even as unto thee. . . . Is thine eye evil,
because I am good?” (Matthew 20:10–15; emphasis added). That last
question means, “Are you turning my kind mercy into injustice?”

We can make
this a difficult parable if we choose, but it is 
really very simple. Let us recall that the parables are aimed at the
conscience, at helping us become better people, more Christlike, not doctrinal
exposition or logical theory. At heart this story gives us an idea of the level
of mercy we can anticipate from the Lord, one far different than that usually
extended by our fellow men; but it is also a parable about seeking precedence
and overvaluing our worth when comparing ourselves to others. The very nature
of money contributes to comparing ourselves to our neighbors. Jesus insisted we
should not make these kinds of comparisons. They are not part of his
kingdom.

The key
phrase upon which the parable hangs is the accusation, “Thou hast made them
equal unto us” (Matthew 20:12). That seems to rankle in their hearts. Equality
is an extremely 
difficult concept to understand, create, maintain, or even discuss, especially
in matters involving money. We believe in it and yet don’t believe in it.
Perhaps our modern world places too much emphasis on the earning power of
various occupations. We rate jobs and positions—our “net
worth”—on a financial scale. I am not suggesting we change all this, just
our attitudes toward it. A little insight might be gained by questioning if we
are really as important as we think. Are we worth more because we’re paid more
than a coworker or someone in another occupation? Perhaps ranking skews our
estimation of our own value compared to others. Laboring as best one can in
whatever occupation one has seems to be the Lord’s standard. No exterior value
is attached. A quick examination of our society would reveal that some are
vastly overcompensated, sometimes in fields that are not very vital to society,
while others with jobs that are vital to society barely survive. Is the highly
paid athlete really worth more than the elementary schoolteacher who helps a
child learn to read? The very question of value and worth in itself is
something to ponder in light of the parable.

“WHAT IS THAT TO THEE?”

Money can be
a motivator, it is true; we would not argue strongly against that, but it can
stimulate an unhealthy estimation or negation of oneself in comparison to
others, and every field, vocation, or profession faces this dilemma. Can we
resist thinking, “I deserve more than another,” when that other is laboring as
well as we are but perhaps in a different field and with diverse skills? Peter
was apparently too concerned about his recompense. He was making himself
“first.” Jesus wanted him to eliminate his prioritizing. It’s a hard concept to
internalize. Wondering about what the other guy is or is not getting seems
innate. The last lesson Jesus taught Peter as recorded in John 21 shows that
one as teachable as Peter was still undergoing the Savior’s tutorial. On the
shores of the Sea of Galilee after the Resurrection, Christ appeared to some of
his disciples. There he told Peter what awaited him in the future, specifying
his death. John was close at hand and Peter, displaying his human nature,
asked, as I’m sure we would in similar circumstances, “What shall this man do?”
Jesus mildly rebuked him by focusing his view squarely on the gospel by
responding, “What is that to thee? follow thou me” (John 21:21–22).

How would it
be if we could reach the stage when we cease weighing what we are getting
against what others are receiving. We do our work. We’re happy within that
work. We do not place a value on human endeavor based on the money it can
create, judging some first and others last. If we’ve made money our
treasure, in heaven’s view the monetary levels we have attained 
may, at last, be completely reversed. Money must not become the standard by
which we value ourselves or others. There must be neither arrogance nor
humiliation.

Because the
parable commences with the statement “For the kingdom of heaven is like unto
. . .” we can conclude it is telling us something about the nature of
God’s character, what is pleasing to him. In God’s kingdom everyone receives
that which is “right,” or just. That is emphasized twice in the parable (see
Matthew 20:4, 7). There should be no jealous or resentful comparing, no
murmuring, no envying, no assumptions of greater merit or entitlement, no
striving for precedence, no arguments regarding who is of the greatest worth to
the overall enterprise or who got away with the minimum amount of labor. Yet in
our world of monetary recompense, it is truly easier for a camel to slip
through the needle’s eye than to approach life with that degree of
equality.

It sometimes
appears to me that there is no rhyme or reason to our valuation of things. I am
occasionally distressed seeing the discomfiture of a student or adult who shows
some hesitancy to tell what he does for a living or what his parents do because
it measures lower on the societal scale of monetary reward or occupational
prestige. This ought not to be. No matter what standard is used it does not
remain consistent. Mental capacity, manual skill or dexterity, level of
education, usefulness to society all have their exceptions on the higher or
lower scale of value. Jobs that require large amounts of these qualities may be
recompensed at a lower level and those that demand little of the above may 
command fortunes. When my teeth hurt, a dentist is the most important
occupation in the world, but when my pipes leak, there is nothing I appreciate
more than the plumber. When I send my child to school, her teacher’s talents
are at a premium; and when I buy food at the store, those who grew it,
harvested it, inspected it, packaged it, transported it, and sold it are all
necessary. How can we judge with any degree of consistency or justice the value
of another? Then let us not judge. Let us be grateful for our penny, without
expecting five more, and begrudge no other person for receiving his or hers. No
one need be embarrassed. No one need be proud.

“IT SHALL NOT BE SO AMONG YOU”

Positioning
or context is an important tool in studying the scriptures. Where do the
stories or teachings lie in relation to one another? In Matthew’s account, the
very next passage is James’s and John’s request to sit on Jesus’ right and left
hand after he establishes his kingdom. Their mother makes the request (which is
an interesting detail to ponder in itself). When the other ten Apostles heard
of the request, “they were moved with indignation against the two brethren”
(Matthew 20:24).

Jesus then gathered
the disciples together to explain to them that his way of administration was
much different than that of the world, where position and recompense carries
the day. “Ye know that the princes of the Gentiles exercise dominion over them,
and they that are great exercise authority upon them. But it shall 
not be so among you: but whosoever will be great among you, let him be your
minister; and whosoever will be chief among you, 
let him be your servant” (Matthew 20:25–27). Is that not what the washing
of feet signified at the Last Supper? Prominence in a worldly or monetary sense
is usually based on the number of people who serve under you. It is the reverse in the Lord’s
perspective. There the greatest are measured by the number of people they
serve, Jesus being the supreme example, for “the Son of man came not to be
ministered unto, but to minister, and to give his life a ransom for many”
(Matthew 20:28).
