THE ORIGIN AND IMPORTANCE OF THE ARTICLES OF FAITH

EDWARD J. BRANDT

The Articles of Faith of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints were first published in serial form in the 1 March 1842 issue of the Times and Seasons. This was also the same issue in which was begun the first publication of the Book of Abraham. The Articles of Faith were the concluding statements to an article that had been prepared by the Prophet Joseph Smith in response to an inquiry he had received from a Mr. John Wentworth, the editor of the Chicago Democrat, one of the leading newspapers of the day. The Prophet published this article under title, "Church History," and the article contained the following introductory comments:

At the request of Mr. John Wentworth, Editor and Proprietor of the Chicago Democrat, I have written the following sketch of the rise, progress, persecution, and faith of the Latter-day Saints, of which I have the honor, under God, of being the founder. Mr. Wentworth says that he wishes to furnish Mr. Bastow, a friend of his, who is writing the history of New Hampshire, with this document. As Mr. Bastow has taken the proper steps to obtain the correct information all that I ask at his hands is that he publish the account entire, ungarnished, and without misrepresentation. 1

The following questions might be asked: (1) Who was John Wentworth? (2) Was the article ever published? If so, (3) Was it ever published in the Chicago Democrat? (4) Who was this Mr. Bastow? (5) Did he publish the article prepared by the Prophet? Finally, (7) Why were the Articles of Faith written? and, (8) What was their purpose and what is their importance to the Church today?

History of John Wentworth

John Wentworth was born in Sandwich, Carrol County, New Hampshire, on 5 March 1815. He began working for local newspapers at the age of 15 and showed much interest in journalism. He attended Dartmouth College, in Hanover, New Hampshire until 1836, when he graduated. He was an outspoken defender of Andrew Jackson and his expansionist policies. This caused him some difficulty at Dartmouth, because of his blunt and forthright writing while working on the college newspaper. In fact, his graduation from the college was in question for a time, until satisfaction was made concerning some of his statements. 2 Upon graduating from Dartmouth, Wentworth moved westward and settled in Chicago, Illinois, with the intent of studying law. He became associated with a local attorney, Henry Moore, and also with John Calhoon, the publisher of the Chicago Democrat. Shortly after his arrival, however, Calhoon gave up the publication of the newspaper, and Horatio Hill took control and set John Wentworth in charge as editor. In time, Wentworth had others who assisted him and to whom he temporarily turned control of the paper while he returned to the East to complete his study of law. 3 In the spring of 1841, he graduated from Harvard University in law and returned to Chicago to pursue not only the practice of law but also to resume the control of the paper. Whether or not he made the acquaintance of Bastow during his stay in the East while studying law is not known. But it appears that shortly after his return to Chicago his inquiry was directed to the Prophet Joseph Smith.

Wentworth was very prominent in Illinois politics, serving seven terms in the national House of Representatives as a representative for the state of Illinois. 4 He also served as the 21st and 24th mayor of Chicago, and the commissioner of numerous departments through his many years of residency there. A very tall man, about six feet six inches in height, he took an active part in the development of the fire department in Chicago. In fact, the first steam engine fire apparatus in Chicago was named after him; it was called "Long John." 5

The Chicago Democrat first began publication on 26 November 1833 as a weekly. It continued until 1861. However, in February 1840, the Daily Democrat, a daily edition, was also issued by the office. Other newspapers sprang up in the community which were very popular, but the Chicago Democrat remained one of the leading newspapers of the city in that day. The press services of the nineteenth century consisted primarily of borrowing of news from other newspapers. We find, for example, in the Times and Seasons, six citations from the Chicago Democrat between 1841 and 1843. A number of these items demonstrate reasonably accurate and fair reporting by Wentworth and his newspaper concerning affairs relating to the Latter-day Saints.

Most of the extant copies of the Chicago Democrat were destroyed in the 1871 fire, making research into its pages very difficult. For example, for the years 1836-41 there are only ten copies of the paper extant, with no references in those ten issues to the Latter-day Saints. In 1842, fifty-one issues of the Chicago Democrat are known to have been published. Only eleven of these still exist, but from those eleven we find ten references to the Saints. In 1843, again fifty-one issues were printed, of which ten still exist. Of these ten issues, six contain references of the Latter-day Saints. In 1844, fifty-two editions were published, forty-one of which are extant. From these we find ten references to the Church. Finally, for the year 1845, fifty-three issues were published, forty-eight of these are extant, and in these are found fifteen references of the Saints.

None of the extant copies of the Chicago Democrat contain, in part or whole, the Wentworth Letter. That Wentworth had read the letter from the Prophet Joseph seems evident, because a brief article on the Church had appeared in the Chicago Democrat within the month or soon after the letter had been sent. The article was republished in the Times and Seasons as follows:

Gen. Joseph Smith, the President and founder of the sect called 'the Latter Day Saints of the Church of Jesus Christ', was born in Sharon, Windsor Co., Vt. in 1805, 23rd of December. Old Windsor County is now boasting of as many distinguished men in different spheres as any in the union. This poor farmer's son has built up a denomination of nearly 100,000 people in Europe, Asia, Africa, and nearly all the islands of the great oceans. Besides Gen. Smith did not invent his creed himself; but an angel of the Lord delivered it to him on Mount Moriah, N.Y. on the 22nd September, 1827. 6

What is cited in the article certainly reflects some of the information that was found in the Wentworth Letter.

As for the identity of Mr. Bastow, we have been unable to find anything about him. A search of the Dartmouth University records finds no one by this name who might have been a school associate of John Wentworth. A man by the name of George Barstow did publish The History of New Hampshire. The first edition appeared in 1842, and the second edition in 1853. 7 Upon examining copies of both of these editions, which cover the history of New Hampshire through 1819, no reference is made in either to the birth or life of Joseph Smith or anything about the Church.

Publication of the Articles of Faith

The first publication of the Articles of Faith was as a part of this early letter, which gives a brief account of the history of the Church and concludes with these summary statements of the faith. No title is given to them; they merely conclude the letter—basically in the form in which we know them today.

In 1842 the Articles of Faith appeared in an article on the "Mormonites" which was contained in a work by John Hayward, entitled The Book of Religions. 8 The author prefaced the articles with the explanation that he had obtained the information from various publications of the Latter-day Saints. 9 The Articles of Faith were taken from the Times and Seasons printing of the Wentworth Letter.

The Articles of Faith appeared in 1844, as part of a pamphlet published in New York, by John E. Page, and L. R. Foster, entitled, Correspondence Between Joseph Smith, the Prophet, and Col. John Wentworth. This pamphlet was made up of the Wentworth letter from the 1 March 1842 issue of Times and Seasons and other articles from the 1 January 1844 issue. The title "Colonel" for Wentworth goes back to the year 1838 when he served as aid-de-camp for Governor Carlin. This was a title that was only honorary, but it brought much ridicule upon him by his political opponents. 10

Another publication of the Articles of Faith also appeared in 1844 in a work prepared by I. Daniel Rupp, entitled An Original History of the Religious Denominations at Present . . . in the United States. The Prophet Joseph, in his history, noted that on 7 September 1843 he received a request from Rupp for information concerning the history of the Church to be used in his work. 11 The account is essentially the Wentworth letter, with some additional comments added and concluded by the Articles of Faith. The second edition of this work was published in 1854. Rupp quoted a letter from the Prophet which had been written on 5 June 1844 after receipt of a copy of the first edition of the book. He promised to supply further information concerning the Church. The martyrdom of the Prophet ended the correspondence with this publisher.

The next publication of the Articles of Faith appeared in 1849 in a tract by J. H. Flannigan called Mormonism Triumphant, published by the British Mission in Liverpool, England. In this edition, Elder Orson Pratt—the mission president—added some modifications to the Articles of Faith, adding in fact a new article dealing with the resurrection. Seven of the other articles contain words and phrases different from those of the early publications. He also was the first to apply a title by calling them the "Latter Day Saint's Faith." This rendition of the Articles, popularly called by some "The Fourteen Articles of Faith," was republished the next year on 20 February 1850 in The Frontier Guardian, an early Church publication in Kanesville, Iowa, edited by Orson Hyde. The next publication of the Articles appeared in the 1851 edition of the Pearl of Great Price, which had been prepared by Elder Franklin D. Richards in Liverpool. Elder Richards was a member of the Council of the Twelve and president of the British Mission. Elder Richards cited the thirteen articles as they had appeared in the Times and Seasons of 1842. No title was given them.

There appeared about 1852 a broadside published by the Millennial Star office in Liverpool entitled "The Latter-day Saints' Belief," with the text of the fourteen articles as issued by Orson Pratt in 1849. 12 The circular also included the addition of a few scriptural citations and/or references for each of the articles and concluded with a brief admonition to the "reader."

The next publication of the Articles of Faith appeared 5 August 1854 in The Zion's Watchman, a church periodical published in Sidney, Australia, 1853-55. The Articles of Faith were the fourteen articles as published by Orson Pratt, with the further addition of scriptural annotations appended by the editors for the additional study of Church members. The title "Faith and Doctrine of the Latter-day Saints with Scriptural Proofs" was used. The version of the fourteen articles with the "scriptural proofs" appeared in England verbatim later in 1854 as the conclusion to the mission pamphlet, He That Readeth Let Him Understand. 13 The Articles of Faith appeared in the 5 September 1854 issue of the Deseret News Weekly, which was at the time republishing the History of the Church. The Wentworth Letter appeared, along with the thirteen Articles and the introduction "For the faith of the Church. . . ." The history was prepared for republication by Elder George A. Smith, who was also Church Historian. After the signification of the Prophet Joseph Smith as the author, Elder Smith then appended a fourteenth item pertaining to patriarchal or plural marriage.

The Millennial Star later published the Articles on Faith (on 21 February 1857) in the reprint of the Deseret News series.

The Articles appeared in 1878, in the revised edition of the Pearl of Great Price which was prepared by Elder Orson Pratt in Salt Lake City. He used the 1851 edition as his source. In spite of the fact that Elder Pratt had published his expanded version of the Articles in 1849, for the Pearl of Great Price he utilized the same thirteen that Elder Franklin D. Richards had taken from the Times and Seasons. The title given them was "Articles of Our Faith." This 1878 edition was presented in the October Conference, 1880, for the acceptance of the membership of the Church as a standard work. In 1888 a reprint of the 1878 Pearl of Great Price contained the title "Articles of Faith." Later there seems to have been some question as to whether the Articles of Faith were really to be binding upon the Saints. So on 6 October 1890, in the General Conference, Elder Franklin D. Richards presented the Articles of Faith to the membership of the Church "as the rule of faith and conduct for the Latter-day Saints," and they were accepted and sustained by the membership.

Another edition of the Pearl of Great Price appeared in 1891, and finally, in 1902, a new revised edition appeared which had been prepared by Elder James E. Talmage under the direction of the First Presidency. here the final minor alterations in the Articles of Faith appeared, and the title "The Articles of Faith of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints" was given to them. This revised edition was presented to the Saints in the October 1902 General Conference by President Joseph F. Smith, and the Articles were again accepted as a standard work. 14

While others in our Church history have presented similar series of articles telling something of our belief or a restatement or a reconfirmation of our belief, none of these has ever come to take the place of these original statements for which the Prophet Joseph was responsible. It is interesting to note, for example, that on 26 March 1907, "An Address of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints to the World" was issued by the First Presidency at the General Conference and then published for the membership and the world. In this document, President Joseph F. Smith "solemnly declared to be true" thirteen items as they relate to the faith of the Latter-day Saints. It was not new doctrine but a reconfirmation or an official restatement of doctrines peculiar and important to the Church from its founding. In the address he also cited the 12th Article of Faith in its entirety, showing his recognition of the importance of these statements.

The most recent change in the Articles of Faith was made with the publication of the 1981 edition of the Pearl of Great Price. A comma was deleted in the fifth article, while the abbreviations "viz." and "etc." were spelled out in articles 6 and 7, respectively, as "namely" and "and so forth." In the fifth article a comma was placed after the word "authority," to clarify that those called to preach and administer the ordinances were to do so only after they had received an appointment for such by those designated or commissioned of the Lord to direct and govern in such matters. 15 The tenth article was changed to clarify some of the terminology. In the two versions below, the differences in the tenth article are italicized.

1921 Edition

10 We believe in the literal gathering of Israel and in the restoration of the Ten Tribes; that Zion will be built upon this [the American] continent; that Christ will reign personally upon the earth; and, that the earth will be renewed and receive its paradisiacal glory.

1981 Edition

10 We believe in the literal gathering of Israel and in the restoration of the Ten Tribes; that Zion (the New Jerusalem) will be built upon the American continent; that Christ will reign personally upon the earth; and, that the earth will be renewed and receive its paradisiacal glory.

Nature of the Articles of Faith

Why were the Articles of Faith written? They appear to have been prepared for a specialized audience, as was the Wentworth Letter itself—particularly for non-Latter-day Saints. We should remember that the Prophet Joseph had initiated earlier attempts to record his own history as early as 1831-32. Also, in 1835 and again in 1838-39 accounts of the coming forth of the Church were prepared under his direction. This latter account has been published as his history and, incidentally, first began to appear in print in a serialized form one issue after the publication of the Wentworth Letter containing the Articles of Faith. The Prophet introduced the series with this note: "The last number I gave a brief history of the rise and progress of the Church. I now enter more particularly into that history and extract from my Journal." 16 For the membership of the Church, then, the Prophet Joseph presented a more detailed history rather than the brief overview which had been presented in the Wentworth Letter.

The Articles of Faith seem not to have been intended to be all-inclusive, but rather a distinctive statement in the context of the times and the religious parties of that day. They presented items that would be easily recognizable to the people and yet declared something of the distinctiveness of the beliefs of the Latter-day Saints.

Other accounts of the coming forth of the Church had been prepared and published earlier, also containing brief statements of our beliefs. In the October 1834 Latter Day Saints' Messenger and Advocate, Oliver Cowdery penned a brief letter wherein he stated eight items of our belief, centered around the restored doctrines of the Prophet Joseph Smith. In 1842, in England, Orson Pratt published a tract entitled An Interesting Account of Several Remarkable Visions and of the Late Discovery of Ancient American Records. He wrote a brief history of the coming forth of the Church, and particularly the Book of Mormon, and (at the conclusion of the pamphlet) he also presented a number of articles or statements of belief attempting to demonstrate the distinctness of our faith. Both of these accounts declaring some of the beliefs of the Latter-day Saints do have some parallels with the Articles of Faith which appeared in the Wentworth Letter. It is possible that the Prophet Joseph Smith may have referred to these sources to assist him in preparing the Wentworth Letter.

The Prophet Joseph Smith, however, clearly stands as being fully responsible for our Articles of Faith. Certainly they provide for us a clear, concise, while not all-inclusive (yet very distinctive) statement concerning some of the major items of our belief. President Spencer W. Kimball, in the October 1975 General Conference of the Church, reiterated for the membership of the Church the importance and continued relevance of the Articles of Faith and challenged every priesthood bearer to memorize them—"word perfect"—that they might help them in the performance of their priesthood responsibilities.
