THE ISRAELITE CONQUEST OF CANAAN (Joshua)

JOHN M. LUNDQUIST

Introduction

The book of Joshua is named after the major character in the book, who served as Moses' trusted assistant during the wilderness period and now served as his successor. Num. 26 gives the account of the calling and setting apart of Joshua under Moses' hands. According to that narrative, Joshua was a man "in whom is the spirit" (v. 18); Moses "laid his hands upon him, and gave him a charge" (v. 23). The book of Joshua relates the account of the Conquest of Canaan under Joshua's direction, covering the period between the translation of Moses and the death of Joshua. The book can be divided into two sections:

I. The conquest of Canaan (chapters 1-12)

II. The settlement of the tribes in Canaan (chapters 13-24)

In this article I will attempt to establish a historical and archaeological background for the Israelite Conquest of Canaan, as this event is described in the books of Joshua and Judges. This event in ancient Israel's history has for long engaged the attention of archaeologists and historians, since by its very nature it is an event, or perhaps better a series of events, which lend themselves to demonstration or to possible validation through physical evidence of the type familiar to archaeologists. The accounts in the book of Joshua speak of military engagements against fortified cities, or defeats and destructions, of resettlement or reoccupation of destroyed cities by Israelites, and so forth. Such events should be reflected in the archaeological record in the form of ashy destruction layers and the intrusion of different, new objects of daily life and shifts in technological arrangements. And in fact these kinds of evidence have been forthcoming from archaeological work in Israel and Jordan. Although this evidence is not universally accepted by scholars, and although there are widely divergent theories available to explain the archaeological and historical background of the Conquest, it is possible to present a point of view that agrees substantially with the biblical account. While I will refer in passing to divergent theories, in this article I will lay out the evidence that can justifiably be seen as supporting the Joshua and Judges accounts.

Before we begin to consider archaeological evidence, it is important that we understand what the Bible itself says on the subject of the Conquest. As was the case with the Exodus, as I tried to point out in my article on that subject, 1 it is important to know what the Bible does say, and what it does not say. Quite frequently, scholars set up straw figures, presumably based on the biblical text, and then, after they have demolished those figures, go on to substitute other theories for what happened, some of which are as absurd as the straw figures which they supposedly derived from the Bible. Perhaps the major straw figure of the Conquest is that of a horde of land-hungry nomads swooping in out of the desert to attack rich, highly fortified but vulnerable Canaanite cities, which the nomads attacked in a kind of Blitzkrieg with several direct frontal assaults, after which land was divided up under the leadership of Joshua, etc., etc. 2

If we dissect the "straw" theory point by point, we find in the first place that there were no "hordes" of Israelites. I have referred above, in my article on "The Exodus," to the problem of inflated numbers in the King James Version of the Bible. The same caution would apply here. In a classic study of some years ago, George E. Mendenhall, taking the Hebrew word 'eleph to mean a subsection of a tribe, then, by extension, a military unit, rather than the numerical unit "one thousand," arrived at a total of 5,550 men levied from the tribes for military duty in Num. 1, and at 5,750 men levied for military duty from the tribes in the census list of Num. 26. These figures contrast with 603,550 men for Num. 1, and 601,730 fighting men for Num. 26, if one uses Hebrew 'eleph in the meaning "one thousand." The resulting numbers, several million Israelites at this period when one adds in the women and the children, are impossibly high. A fighting army of several thousand men at the time of the crossing of the Jordan River under Joshua would probably be closer to the truth. 3

The second element in the straw figure presented above was "nomads," as in "hordes of land-hungry nomads." In spite of the fact that one continues to find the Israelites of this period referred to as nomads or as bedouin in popular presentations, it is a general scholarly consensus that the Israelites in this period, and during the preceding period of wilderness wanderings, were not nomads. According to George E. Mendenhall, biblical studies has continued to be haunted by what he calls the "Bedouin mirage," according to which ancient Israel in the period under discussion was in a nomadic state, about to become sedentarized after taking possession of the land of Canaan. 4 According to such views, modern Arab bedouin would provide the proper study for a life-style comparable to that of Israel in the period of the Wanderings and the Conquest. This is simply no longer accepted by scholars working at the forefront of research in the field, nor, most importantly, is this what the Bible itself says. The Old Testament narratives make it clear that the Israelites were fully settled, in an Egyptian setting, during the four hundred or so years of the sojourn in Egypt. While in Egypt, they would have been settled, practicing farming and sheep herding in villages in the eastern Delta region, or would have actually lived in one of the cities they were forced to build for the king. The Wanderings narratives in Numbers make it clear that, far from being nomads or bedouin, they were almost totally maladapted to such a life-style. They constantly referred back longingly to the settled way of life they had left behind in Egypt and in numerous ways showed that they were not accustomed to life in the Sinai wastes (see the numerous references in the book of Numbers). The main content of the "murmurings" was that of dissatisfaction with and inability to adapt to their life in the wilderness. 5

The supposed Blitzkrieg nature of the Israelite conquest is now generally rejected and will be discussed at length below. As for the nature of the Canaanite cities themselves, it is now generally thought that they were in a state of considerable decline and decrepitude. Going back to the fourteenth century B.C., the "Amarna Age," during which time numerous petty Canaanite city states vied for the attention of the Egyptian Pharaohs Amenophis III and IV (Akhenaten), Canaan had been torn by internecine feuding and the marauding activities of lawless brigands known in the ancient texts as 'apiru. Clay tablets written in the cuneiform script, utilizing the international diplomatic language of Akkadian and found in the ruins of the archive of Akhenaten's capital city (modern Amarna), indicate that the number of military troops required for the defense of a typical Canaanite city of the time was few indeed. Requests for military assistance in these tablets sometimes mentioned only from ten to fifty men, or perhaps fifty chariots at the most. 6 As the Late Bronze Age ended, around 1200 B.C., the general picture that we get of Canaanite cities is one of impoverishment and general weakness. It was indeed customary during the Late Bronze Age in Canaan for cities not to rebuild decaying fortification systems, and in fact, to simply reuse the massive city walls that still stood from the Early and Middle Bronze ages that had preceded. This latter fact is indeed at the heart of the problem over the non-existence of Late Bronze Age city walls at Jericho, as documented by the excavators there. Those who accept the biblical account of the destruction of Jericho, but who must take into account the non-existence of Late Bronze Age city walls there, point to the practice that I have just mentioned, of reusing of Early and Middle Bronze Age city walls, which walls would have been the ones destroyed by the Israelites. 7

Biblical Evidence

Let us leave straw figure reconstructions of how Israel conquered the land of Canaan and look at what the Bible itself says on this subject. In order to do this, we must examine the books of Joshua and Judges; to leave out of consideration the statements in the early chapters of Judges that relates to the Conquest would seriously skew our picture. The book of Joshua tells us that the Conquest began in the Spring of the year (1:11; 2:6; 3:15; 4:19), at which time the crossing of the Jordan River took place. From that point the strategy of attack was first to seize the cities near the point of crossing, Jericho, Ai, and Gibeon (Josh. 6-9). From there, the Israelite troops went up against a coalition of southern kings, representing Jerusalem, Hebron, Lachish, Eglon, and Yarmuth. With the defeat of this coalition, and with the defeat of some of their cities, particularly Lachish, the Israelites took control of what we know geographically as the hill country of Judah, the Jordan Valley, the hills west and south of Jerusalem, and the Negev (see Map #4 in the LDS publication of the Bible).

At that point Joshua and the Israelites turned their attention to the northern part of Canaan, to battle a coalition of Canaanite kings headed by Jabin, King of Hazor. Hazor, identified as having been at one time "the head of all those kingdoms" (Josh. 11:10), was a major city located in the Upper Galilee, north of the Sea of Galilee. With the defeat of this coalition, and significantly, with the burning of Hazor (Josh. 11:13), the Israelites were in effective control of the northern part of the country. A list is then given of thirty-one kings that Israel had defeated on the west side of the Jordan, in addition to the two that they had already defeated earlier on the east side of the Jordan, before the crossing (Josh. 12; Num. 21). With this, the land was divided up among the tribes, and the Conquest could be said to have been successfully completed. But unfortunately, such was not the case. Already in the book of Joshua itself hints of nagging problems were heard. We read that the Ephraimites did not drive the Canaanites out of Gezer (Josh. 16:10) and hear of their complaints that Canaanites, possessing chariots of iron, controlled the important Jezreel valley, with cities such as Beth-Shean. Thus Israelite division of former Canaanite land existed in many cases not as a reality, but as a potential, to be realized by the allotted tribes as soon as they were militarily strong enough to take the land given them by force (Josh. 17:14-18).

The reality of these "post-Conquest" problems becomes even more stark in the book of Judges, where we are served a veritable litany of land areas presumably already conquered by Israel, and certainly awarded to various tribes, but which were still in the hands of their enemies. Thus Judah continued to extend its conquest of southern areas (Judg. 1:9-21) but could not drive the Canaanites out of the valley (Judg. 1:19), and, indeed, could not conquer Jerusalem (Judg. 1:21). Likewise Manasseh could not end the Canaanite domination of the entire Jezreel Valley, and its important cities of Beth-Shean, Taanach, and Megiddo (Judg. 1:27). In addition to other areas of the country described as remaining under Canaanite domination, of great importance is the mention of the coastline, kept from the control of Asher (Judg. 1:31-32). If we thus attempt to see the complete picture of the Conquest as it is depicted in the Bible, we would have to say that Israel experienced great and probably complete initial success over virtually the entire land of Canaan. Following the initial success, however, they were thrown back in numerous instances by the superior weaponry of their enemies, especially in the main valleys, Jordan and Jezreel, along the coastline, and at several of the previously important Late Bronze cities, such as Jerusalem and Gezer. The Israel that we see in the beginning of the book of Judges, and indeed during the entire period of the Judges until the rise of David, is an Israel living in villages along the spiny hill country of Judea and Samaria (to use the later and classical geographic terminology). The main thoroughfares, the trade routes, the coastline and many strategic cities were controlled by non-Israelite inhabitants of Canaan. And it is this picture which finds remarkable support from archaeological investigations in Israel during the past several decades.

Archaeological Evidence

Archaeologically, the Conquest would have begun in the late thirteenth century B.C. or toward the very end of the Late Bronze Age (which ends in 1200 B.C.). As we saw in the article on "The Exodus," the Merneptah Stele places "Israel" in Canaan approximately 1220 B.C. One of the most crucial sites in Israel for relating the Conquest to both the biblical account and to archaeology is Hazor. According to its excavator, Yigael Yadin, 8 the Late Bronze Age Canaanite city of Hazor (Level XIII) was destroyed and burned late in the thirteenth century B.C., between 1230 and 1200 B.C., and replaced, on the upper part of the mound, by a poor, "semi-nomadic" settlement (Level XII), similar to the village settlements found in the upper Galilee that are dated to the twelfth century B.C., and which are attributed by Yadin to the Israelites. This provides perhaps the clearest case of the correlation between historical (biblical—Josh. 11:10-13—the destruction and burning of Hazor) and archaeological evidence. It is taken by Yadin as clear and indisputable evidence for the position of William F. Albright, who, as a result of his life-long work as a brilliant Bible scholar and archaeologist, found fundamental agreement between the Bible and archaeology, particularly with regard to the Conquest. 9

Beginning around 1200 B.C. iron technology began to be introduced in the Near East, partially as a result of the interruption of trade in tin, the main element in the transformation of copper into bronze. One of the leading authorities in the field of Palestinian archaeology, James A. Sauer, divides the early Iron Age into three main phases, Iron Age IA, IB, and IC. Iron Age phases IA and IB run contemporaneously, both being dated from about 1200 to 1000 B.C., roughly the period of the Judges. Iron Age IC represents the period of David, particularly his defeat of the Philistines and his consolidation of an Israelite empire, and dates from 1000 to 918 B.C. According to Sauer, the Iron IA occupation is represented archaeologically by a low level of technological expertise, poorly built houses within small village settings, poorly made pottery, and no fortifications. This culture has been identified very widely on both sides of the Jordan River, succeeding Late Bronze Canaanite occupations at cities that were in many cases destroyed. The evidence indicates that few Late Bronze sites in Canaan survived destruction, and many of them show this poor, village-like Iron Age IA culture rising from the ruins. This is the Israelite culture, although it is now known that essentially the same technological attributes were shared by the Ammonite, Moabite, and Edomite cultures on the east side of the Jordan River. The low level of technology evinced in the early Israelite culture is not to be taken as evidence of nomadic or semi-nomadic origins, but simply as the natural result of arriving new in an area rich in advanced (but now decrepit) technological traditions, from a forty-year period of peasant-like existence, and needing time to develop native skills into higher technological levels. In other words, Israel would have begun its existence in the promised land at a subsistence level of technology, based on farming and grazing, rather than on international trade, commerce, and other economic attributes of the Canaanite cities, and also on the basis of social egalitarianism—which would require different architectural requirements—away from palatial structures, for example. 10 One of the most remarkable traits of the Israelite technology is that its typical pottery types were a kind of debased, less expertly produced Late Bronze Age pottery. Israelite pottery is in some ways "virtually identical" to Late Bronze Canaanite types, but made with less finely levigated clay and not as expertly fired. This was a transitional period for Israel, one in which much of the conquered culture was taken over, but at a lower level of expertise. 11

The other major subdivision of the early Iron Age, according to Sauer, the Iron Age IB, "consists of larger sites with a more sophisticated physical culture, including public structures, smelting installations, and pottery that is often painted in multiple parallel lines, nets, semicircles and birds." 12 This is the Philistine culture, representing the presence in Canaan of "Sea Peoples" who had been settled in Canaan (they, of course, gave this area its other well-known name, Palestine), particularly on the coast, by Pharaoh Rameses III of the 20th Dynasty, following his defeat of various groups of Sea Peoples in his fifth and eighth years (beginning about 1800 B.C.). It can be shown that in a number of cases, particularly along the coast, it was this Philistine culture that destroyed the Canaanite city and built its own city in the place of the Canaanite. In many other cases, however, particularly those where the Canaanite city was destroyed or simply replaced by the Israelite culture (Iron Age IA), the Iron IA culture was short lived and transitional, lasting perhaps twenty-five years or so, and was then pushed out and replaced by the Iron Age IB culture—the Philistine, which lasted until it was removed by Iron Age IC—the conquest of Philistia by David. This, of course, beautifully parallels the biblical account as it is contained in Joshua and Judges: universal initial success by the Israelites, who were then pushed back by "Canaanites," by which we should understand Philistines. The Old Testament uses the broad term "Canaanite" for the inhabitants of Canaan, although archaeological evidence, coupled with the evidence of David's conquests, along with evidence from Joshua itself (13:2-3—the occupation of the coastline by Philistines) tells us that it was in fact Philistines who were Israel's major "thorns" (Judg. 2:3) during this period. 13

As the dust settles at the opening of the book of Judges, the Israelites had been pushed back into the spiny hill country of Judea and Samaria, while their enemies—primarily Philistines—occupied the coast, the valleys, both Jezreel and Jordan, and certain crucial cities. Thus Israel, during the period of the Judges, existed as a peasant farming culture in small villages (some of them fortified), while its Philistine adversaries controlled more prosperous cities, trade routes, and possessed technological advantages such as superior metal technology (1 Sam. 13:19-20). 14

It is true, of course, that there are exceptions, some of them very important ones, to the pattern sketched out above. It should not be thought that such a neat state of affairs can be completely worked out with materials as difficult and open to such diverse interpretations as archaeology can be. There are indications, for example, of peaceful transitions from Canaanite to Israelite occupation, such as at Khirbet Rabud (possibly biblical Debir—see Josh. 10:38). 15 Furthermore, it is well known that in two of the most prominent cases, the biblical accounts of the destructions of Jericho and Ai (Josh. 6-8), the archaeological evidence does not agree with the biblical account. Although Ai presents more formidable problems in trying to harmonize the archaeological results with the accounts in Joshua, 16 the case of Jericho is different. Numerous prominent scholars, among them William F. Albright and the English scholar Kenneth Kitchen, have presented reasonable solutions to the fact that Kathleen Kenyon's excavations found no Late Bronze city walls (thus no walls that could have been destroyed as described in Josh. 6). As I have already alluded to above, there are reasonable grounds for thinking that the lack of Late Bronze city walls at Jericho results from a combination of Late Bronze reuse of Early and Middle Bronze walls, a well-known phenomenon in Late Bronze Age Canaan, along with massive erosion of the Late Bronze remains of the city, this latter also being a well-attested phenomenon in archaeology. There is archaeological evidence, though scanty, for a town at Jericho in the time of Joshua, the reason for the scantiness being the heavy erosion that washed away much of the post-Middle Bronze remains. 17

Israelite Military Strategies

One final section, of great interest and importance for our understanding of the Conquest, remains to be presented: the nature of the military activities during the Conquest. We are dependent for our understanding of the military side of the Conquest on the important researches of Abraham Malamat. Malamat has isolated numerous military strategies and techniques, which, far from indicating a massive frontal assault by tens of thousands of Israelite troops against wellfortified Canaanite cities, presents rather the picture of relatively small numbers of highly mobile, expertly led and fast-moving troops operating in what we now know as classic methods of warfare pitting superior numbers of foes operating from fortified or partially fortified cities against relatively poorly trained attackers in a hilly region. Malamat has isolated the following "major fundamentals of early Israelite warfare:" (1) intelligence and espionage, with the classic instance being that of the information provided about Jericho by the harlot Rahab (Josh. 2); (2) logistics, including the choice of the proper time of year for attack (Spring—Josh. 3), when for example Israelite troops could "eat of the fruit of the land of Canaan" (Josh. 5:12); the gathering in of several days of provisions before the attacks began (Josh. 1:10-11); and the use of Gilgal as a logistic and operational base, to which the Israelites would return after each campaign in the south (Josh. 9:6; 10:6-9, 15, 43); (3) "the indirect military approach," according to which the Israelites sought to avoid direct frontal assaults and encounters with the enemy in the open, where the Canaanite chariot would have been a potent weapon (as indeed it was eventually, resulting as it did in the denial of much of Canaan to the Israelites until the time of David—see references above); (4) "neutralization of city defenses," the primary example being Jericho, where the strategy of marching several times around the walls is noted elsewhere in military history, and would have well-documented negative psychological effects on the defenders. The conquest of Bethel, described in Judg. 1:22-26, was accomplished by means of secret entry through the postern (secret—rear) gateway to the city; (5) "enticement of city defenders," of which the best example is that of Ai, in which the defenders were drawn out of the city by a ruse, then ambushed, after which the city was taken (Josh. 8). Malamat notes that in the case of Ai the strategem was employed only after a direct frontal assault failed miserably (Josh. 7); (6) "surprise attacks," which resulted in victory over Gibeon (Josh. 10:9-10), and at the waters of Merom (Josh. 11:7). This tactic requires secrecy and speed in order to succeed, and both were clearly evident in these cases; (7) "night operations," which were employed in the battles of Ai (Josh. 8:3) and Gibeon (Josh. 10:9). In the case of Gibeon, the Israelite force would have made a forced night march, covering the territory between Gilgal, their camp, and Gibeon, a distance of 25-30 kilometers with a rise in elevation of about one thousand meters. 18

We can thus see the extraordinary richness and diversity of the evidence that can be used to unite the discoveries of archaeology with the witness of the Bible. In the case of the Israelite Conquest of Canaan, such evidence allows us to fill out our understanding of the biblical record and to add many levels of understanding and of appreciation to the accounts given in the books of Joshua and Judges of this series of events. It should be obvious that we do not seek to depend on archaeology, in the vulgar sense, to firmly establish our faith in the Bible, or to establish the believability of the scriptural record. The scriptures stand on their own and are not in need of outside testimony, be it historical or archaeological, to establish their validity. Such outside evidence does indeed exist, however, and our appreciation of the biblical accounts can only be strengthened and deepened by reference to this evidence. In the case of the Conquest, we can close with the statement of Yadin that "the 'silence' of the archaeological evidence is ...resounding ..." in support of the biblical accounts of these events. 19
