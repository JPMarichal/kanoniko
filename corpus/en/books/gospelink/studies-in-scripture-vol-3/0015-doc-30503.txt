# The Good News of Moses (Deuteronomy)

### The Good News of Moses (Deuteronomy)
Robert L. Millet, Kent P. Jackson, Studies in Scripture, Vol. 3: Genesis to 2 Samuel ( 1985)

THE GOOD NEWS OF MOSES (Deuteronomy)

KEITH H. MESERVY

Israel's story under Moses' leadership is a pioneer story of two dimensions and two phases: Dimension one, first phase began when the enslaved Israelites forsook the secure, familiar, albeit brutal and smothering, environment in Egypt for freedom in the vast spaces of Sinai's inhospitable wilderness. That was a pioneer generation.

The second dimension overlapped the first, when those pioneers forsook the pagan world in Egypt to enter the divine frontier at Sinai. How eagerly and boldly they seemed to venture into a new, spiritual realm that seemed to them, as God's elect, so full of promise of instant fulfillment. They accepted God's challenge and readily promised to give him their total allegiance, little realizing what unforseen obstacles this commitment entailed: obstacles that required a certain amount of soul heroism to surmount; obstacles that tried their commitment to pioneering and to God more than they might have allowed. With Moses' encouragement, however, they gradually changed the kind and quality of their lives in hopes that one day they might enter with Moses as holy people into their spiritual land—the rest of the Lord—as Moses had promised.

Their children, on the other hand, grew up knowing a Bedouin-like life as the norm, without ever experiencing the trauma of change known by their parents. They, in their turn, inaugurated the second phase of the first dimension by leaving their familiar but unproductive home in the wilderness to keep the milk and honey flowing in the new land. Each phase and both dimensions demanded much adaptability of rugged pioneers.

Deuteronomy

Deuteronomy is the Greek name of the fifth book of the Bible. It means "Second Law," or "A Copy of the Law." The Hebrew name of the Book, elleh haddebarim ("These Are the Words"), is taken from the first words of Deuteronomy: "These are the words which Moses spake unto all Israel on this side Jordan in the wilderness, in the plain. . . . according unto all that the Lord had given him in commandment unto them" (1:1, 3, 5; unless otherwise specified, all references are to the book of Deuteronomy). It is also familiarly known to the Jews as mishneh hattora, "copy of the law" (17:18).

Deuteronomy consists of a series of three sermons which Moses delivered to the Israelites in Moab near the end of his life:

First Speech: Chapters 1-4. This speech is a recitation of the events that took place between the departure from Sinai and the arrival east of the Jordan River.

Second Speech: Chapters 5-26. This discourse contains Moses' account of the events that took place at Sinai, and the instructions that Israel received there, often called the Deuteronomic Law (chapters 12-26).

Third Speech: Chapters 27-30. These chapters contain Moses' final instructions to his people, including blessings and curses that he promised to them, based on their behavior.

Chapters 31-34 relate the last acts of Moses. In this section we read that Moses died and was buried in Moab (34:5-6). Modern revelation, however, confirms that he was translated, in order to fulfill a later mission prior to his resurrection (see Alma 45:19). That mission was his appearance and restoration of keys with Jesus and Elijah on the Mount of Transfiguration. 1

Moses' speeches were his final efforts to bring perspective to the experiences that he had shared with Israel. In them he explained the meaning of their forty-year joint venture in faith.

The old generation had spent forty years sloughing off the flesh pots of Egypt. Those were empathy-filled years for Moses, who, as Israel's prophet, identified himself with the needs of the people and often pled with God in their behalf for time enough to bring them back to him. But, as God's prophet, he also empathized with the divine agenda and was often distressed by Israel's failures and frequent recalcitrance. Forty intimate years of experiences with Israel's spiritual ups and downs had linked Moses and Israel indissolubly. Consequently, the thought of leaving them to go to his well-deserved rest while they went to their long-promised land must have been extremely painful to him. The book of Deuteronomy, however, shows us that more painful to Moses than leaving them was any thought that through some negligence or thoughtlessness they might leave God and lose all the gains made in the wilderness. He was concerned that their old yearnings might prevail over their new commitments. Therefore, the question that underlies much of what is said in Deuteronomy is: Would the values recently acquired at Sinai and in the wilderness take root so the coming generations would continually feast on the manna of God's abundant life? Moses knew this could happen only if God remained central to Israel's life. The book of Deuteronomy records Moses' determined efforts to insure that this happened.

"Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thine heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy might" (6:5). If Israel did this, said Moses, then "these words, which I command thee this day, shall be in thine heart," and, therefore, special things would happen:

"Thou shalt [note how he addressed each individual Israelite by using this specific, second person singular pronoun]:

1. "Teach them diligently unto thy children, . . ."

2. "Talk of them when thou sittest in thine house, and when thou walkest by the way, and when thou liest down, and when thou risest up. . . ."

3. "Bind them for a sign upon thine hand, and they shall be as frontlets between thine eyes. And thou shalt write them upon the posts of thy house, and on thy gates" (6:6-9). This is the basis for the Jewish use of phylacteries and mezuzoth—spiritual reminders to keep them in remembrance of God. God knows how quickly his children tend to forget him and how slow they are to remember him. Of necessity, he uses various means—including sacraments and Sabbath days—to help them keep him in remembrance. Moses knew that like attracts like and that those who yearn for God eventually find him. "For intelligence cleaveth unto intelligence; wisdom receiveth wisdom; truth embraceth truth; virtue loveth virtue; light cleaveth unto light; mercy hath compassion on mercy" (D&C 88:40). Those who love God inevitably embrace him. Thus, said he: "It will be our righteousness, if we observe to do all these commandments before the Lord our God, as he hath commanded us" (6:25).

The book of Deuteronomy is not the story of Israel's unique experiences in the wilderness but Moses' interpretation of the meaning of all those happenings. This book explains how Israel, out of its fundamental experience with God, developed its "national testimony" about him and his relationship with them. Here their unique call and relationship with God is emphasized, as well as the price they had had to pay to qualify to enter the promised land. Here we learn how God had helped and would continue to help them succeed, the circumstances under which they would qualify for his conditional blessings, and how they might hope to establish a just and holy nation. This kind of discussion made this book into an important guide book for individual Israelites as well as their king. Consequently, Moses required: (1) that future kings would make a personal copy of this book to guide them in leading the nation (17:18-20); (2) that the Israelites would assemble themselves every seven years at the Feast of the Tabernacles to read this book (31:10-11); and (3) that the Israelites would make a copy on two plastered stone pillars at Mt. Ebal, where, available for public scrutiny, it could be a factor in shaping the quality of their national life (27:2-4, 8).

These specific future needs tell us something of Moses' perceived audiences as he prepared this book. Consequently, what is distilled in Deuteronomy is not the Levitical practices of the Mosaic law—those were kept in another reference manual known as Leviticus—but some of the most important provisions governing right relationships with God and each other, the essence of Mosaic religion. One might think of Deuteronomy, therefore, as the Gospel of Moses, i.e., the good news or story of God's need for and care for Israel and through them his care for the world.

Israel, the Chosen of God

As Moses addressed his people, he was addressing every generation who seeks to know God. Deuteronomy is an account of the work of God in another time and place; but because it is a story of God, who does not change, and of human nature or human need, which does not change, it is a story for all generations. Thus, while addressing himself to his contemporaries, Moses was also addressing anticipated future needs as well.

It is obvious that part of Moses' live audience included some members of Moses' generation who had been with him from the beginning, those who remembered all-too-well from their childhood what life in Egypt was like, as well as the events leading up to their dramatic departure. With him, they had experienced the crossing of the Red Sea. They had survived hunger and thirst in the wilderness, heard the voice of God while observing the fiery mountain at Sinai, heard the spies report at the end of their forty-day reconnaissance of the land, heard rebellious plans made to replace Moses and return to Egypt, saw the results of the abortive effort to invade Canaan without God's help and contrary to his counsel, seen what happened to Korah, Dathan, Abiram, and their 250 fellow-rebels, stayed alive day in and day out for forty years in the wilderness, survived the disaster at Baal Peor, seen God at work against Og and Sihon, and had come to reverence Moses as the venerable old prophet who had served both God and them very well and was now about to leave their midst (1-4). Moses drew upon their cumulative experience to discuss what they had learned in the wilderness (see chapters 5-11 especially). Moses' recital was not merely a nostalgic voyage through the past for a 120-year-old patriarch. It was rather a need to show the rising generation how God had been involved in their lives, how Israel had needed that awful wilderness to learn how faith and obedience secure life, how they had been enabled to do whatever they were called to do when they worked with God, and how no other people had received such intimate divine direction as Israel had received.

By showing the relationship between their desires and actions and the desire and actions of the Lord, Moses provided valuable insight for us. While we know that God is active in this world, it is not always easy to recognize his hand—especially in the midst of adversity—and recognize that he is present. But Moses provided this viewpoint. In doing it, he became a true and faithful historian who perceived truth—the way things really are, were, and will be (D&C 93:24). Who, therefore, could honestly tell the story the way all the participants—including God—perceived it? It took a prophet to discern what had happened, what was happening, or what would happen. Deuteronomy is especially valuable because we have the prophetic insight of Moses to illuminate God's role in Israel's history.

Time and again Moses emphasized the uniqueness of Israel's experience with God and, therefore, how important it was that their story be remembered:

Did ever people hear the voice of God speaking out of the midst of the fire, as thou hast heard, and live?

Or hath God assayed to go and take him a nation from the midst of another nation . . . by signs, and by wonders, and by war, and by a mighty hand . . . according to all that the Lord your God did for you in Egypt before your eyes?

Unto thee it was shewed, that thou mightest know that the Lord he is God; there is none else beside him.

Out of heaven he made thee to hear his voice, that he might instruct thee (4:33-36).

Moses affirmed Israel's special relationship with the Lord:

A. They were God's special possession: "The Lord hath taken you, and brought you forth out of the iron furnace, even out of Egypt, to be unto him a people of inheritance, as ye are this day" (4:20), "a special people unto himself, above all people that are upon the face of the earth" (7:6).

B. Their special calling was to be "an holy people unto the Lord thy God" (7:6).

C. Their relationship with God was a covenant relationship: "The Lord our God made a covenant with us in Horeb [Sinai]" (5:2).

D. They were to be faithful to their covenant to serve him: "Take heed unto yourselves, lest ye forget the covenant of the Lord your God, which he made with you, and make you a graven image" (4:23).

E. God had recently renewed this covenant in Moab. "The Lord made not this covenant with our fathers, but with us, even us, who are all of us here alive this day" (5:2-3). All Israel covenanted with the Lord in order that he might "establish thee to day for a people unto himself, and that he may be unto thee a God, as he hath said unto thee, and as he hath sworn unto thy fathers, to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob" (29:13).

F. The covenant relationship was based both on love and promises made: "Because the Lord loved you, and because he would keep the oath which he had sworn unto your fathers, hath the Lord brought you out with a mighty hand, and redeemed you out of the house of bondmen" (7:8).

G. God is faithful to his promises: "Know therefore that the Lord thy God, he is God, the faithful God, which keepeth covenant and mercy with them that love him and keep his commandments to a thousand generations" (7:9).

H. Israel was dispossessing the Canaanites of the good land because of the wickedness of the Canaanites: "Speak not thou in thine heart, after that the Lord thy God hath cast them out from before thee, saying, For my righteousness the Lord hath brought me in to possess this land: but for the wickedness of these nations the Lord doth drive them out from before thee . . . and that he may perform the word which the Lord sware unto thy fathers, Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob" (9:4-5; cf. Gen. 15:16; Lev. 18).

I. Stiff-necked Israelites were not entitled to be self-righteous: "Understand therefore, that the Lord thy God giveth thee not this good land to possess it for thy righteousness; for thou art a stiffnecked people. . . From the day that thou didst depart out of the land of Egypt, until ye came unto this place, ye have been rebellious against the Lord" (9:6-7). He recited a long list of examples to remind them: "Ye have been rebellious against the Lord from the day that I knew you" (9:8-24).

Moses' castigation of Israel for disobedience was proportionate to the great amount of light they had received. If no law had been given, then no disobedience would have been possible. But, as modern Israel learned: "Unto whom much is given much is required; and he who sins against the greater light shall receive the greater condemnation. Ye call upon my name for revelations, and I give them unto you; and inasmuch as ye keep not my sayings, which I give unto you, ye become transgressors; and justice and judgment are the penalty which is affixed unto my law" (D&C 82:3-4). One must read Moses' comments regarding Israel's persistent disobedience in the light of their high calling and their great revelations. Similarly, the Lord in the Doctrine and Covenants often scolded his modern Saints.

A Society of the Pure in Heart

Calling Israel to be holy was God's invitation to them to be like him. "Ye shall be holy: for I the Lord your God am holy" (Lev. 19:2). Their fathers—Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob—had all been sanctified (made holy) and entered into God's presence. When Moses "sought diligently to sanctify [the Israelites] that they might behold the face of God" (D&C 84:23), they learned that it was within their capacity to do the same.

Since God is holy and his children are not, his work with them is preparatory in nature, to help them become holy. Whenever he succeeds in doing this, a society of the pure in heart is produced. While God's challenge to his people is not always expressed in these words, this is his oft-expressed goal—to build up a holy people—God's kind of people. Such a community of people is called Zion. To help meet this end, God's people often are commanded to build temporary sanctuaries (tabernacles and temples set apart as holy places), so that he, the Holy God, might reside in their midst. This is to be the first step in establishing Zion.

Based on the external ritual of the Mosaic law, the Israelites knew that no unclean thing could enter into God's presence. The ritual also taught them that whoever was unclean, might, through repentance and ritual, overcome uncleanness and come to God in his tabernacle. This fundamental teaching provided hope for any who were willing to turn to God, that they might again come back into his presence. Thus, every practicing Israelite knew that he could become holy and thereby qualify to come to the Lord of holiness in his tabernacle or temple. 2

To Moses the Lord said: "And the Lord hath avouched thee this day to be his peculiar people, as he hath promised thee, and that thou shouldest keep all his commandments; and to make thee high above all nations which he hath made, in praise, and in name, and in honour; and that thou mayest be an holy people unto the Lord thy God, as he hath spoken" (26:18; cf. Ex. 19:5-6). As he said elsewhere: "Ye shall be holy: for I the Lord your God am holy" (Lev. 19:2). Similarly the Lord told Joseph Smith: "Sanctify yourselves that your minds become single to God, and the days will come that you shall see him; for he will unveil his face unto you" (D&C 88:68; cf. 93:1). He proclaimed also:

And inasmuch as my people build a house unto me in the name of the Lord, and do not suffer any unclean thing to come into it, that it be not defiled, my glory shall rest upon it;

Yea, and my presence shall be there, for I will come into it, and all the pure in heart that shall come into it shall see God. . . .

And the nations of the earth shall honor her, and shall say: Surely Zion is the city of our God, and surely Zion cannot fall, neither be moved out of her place, for God is there. . . .

Let Zion rejoice for this is Zion—THE PURE IN HEART (D&C 97:15-16, 19, 21).

Within this same prophetic environment Moses came into the midst of Israel and challenged them in God's behalf: "Ye shall be holy: for I the Lord your God am holy" (Lev. 19:2). The Lord then charged them to build a sanctuary in their midst where, said he, "I will meet you, to speak there unto thee. And there I will meet with the children of Israel, and the tabernacle shall be sanctified by my glory. And I will sanctify the tabernacle of the congregation, and the altar: I will sanctify also both Aaron and his sons, to minister to me in the priest's office. And I will dwell among the children of Israel, and will be their God. And they shall know that I am the Lord their God, that brought them forth out of the land of Egypt, that I may dwell among them: I am the Lord their God" (Ex. 29:42-46; cf. 25:8). Israel was on the road to perfection, and so the Lord commanded: "Thou shalt be perfect with the Lord thy God" (18:13); "The Lord shall establish thee an holy people unto himself, as he hath sworn unto thee, if thou shalt keep the commandments of the Lord thy God, and walk in his ways" (28:9).

The Attributes of Covenant People

God's willingness to elevate his children by sharing what he knows is so characteristic of him; he is a revelator and teacher. Typically his Saints are "crowned with blessings from above, yea, and with commandments not a few, and with revelations in their time" (D&C 59:4). Moses said: "Hearken, O Israel, unto the statutes and unto the judgments, which I teach you, for to do them" (4:1).

Behold, I have taught you statutes and judgments, even as the Lord my God commanded me. . . . Keep therefore and do them for this is your wisdom and your understanding in the sight of the nations, which shall hear all these statutes, and say, Surely this great nation is a wise and understanding people. For what nation is there so great, who hath God so nigh unto them, as the Lord our God is in all things that we call upon him for? . . . [and] that hath statutes and judgments so righteous as all this law, which I set before you this day? (4:5-8).

How like a prophet, how like God, to teach his children correct principles so they may live abundantly. How much love, confidence, and encouragement is reflected in God's willingness to teach his children! Who can emphasize his role as a teacher and loving parent enough? What a classic expression we find in Deuteronomy of this willingness to teach, and what a profound effect such teaching had—not only on the lives of those who were listening, but also upon fortunate neighbors who could recognize by such laws not only how beneficent God is but also how intelligent his directions for his people are.

God's goal is to make people holy like he is, he can teach them how to do it, and they are capable of receiving instructions. The Lord called Enoch's people "ZION because they were of one heart and one mind" (Moses 7:18). Likewise, Moses' people were called to find unity with God by loving and following his direction. Sanctification—being made holy—requires submissiveness—a quality of the spirit involving unity with God. Sons and daughters who are malleable to their Father will grow up to be like their Father (Heb. 12:5-9). At Sinai, Israel was filled with desire and willingly promised full-hearted submission. To Moses they said: "Speak thou unto us all that the Lord our God shall speak unto thee; and we will hear it, and do it" (5:27). The people of Moses only partially fulfilled this promise, despite the fact that "Moses . . . sought diligently to sanctify his people that they might behold the face of God; . . . [but they] could not endure his presence" (D&C 84:23-24). Consequently, God resided in their midst, but behind the veil of the tabernacle rather than openly as he did with Enoch. Nevertheless, he was there.

The Israelites set aside a special area in the camp where God was in residence. They set up his tabernacle there. They sanctified it, its vessels, altar, priesthood, themselves, and their firstborn sons and livestock. Then, as long as they did not pollute the building by uncleanness, it remained holy and God was in their midst, teaching by his presence what their ultimate option was. The camp, as the environment where God was in residence, was to be kept clean because of the presence of the Lord within it: "For the Lord thy God walketh in the midst of thy camp, . . . therefore shall thy camp be holy: that he see no unclean thing in thee, and turn away from thee" (23:14). Zion was coming, but some time in the distant future. In the meantime God was sanctifying his people. So the challenge remained to be "an holy people unto the Lord thy God" (7:6; 14:2).

In Enoch's Zion the people were "of one heart and one mind, and dwelt in righteousness; and there was no poor among them" (Moses 7:18). Compassion for those in need was also characteristic of Mosaic society: "If there be among you a poor man of one of thy brethren within any of thy gates . . . thou shalt not harden thine heart, nor shut thine hand from thy poor brother: But thou shalt open thine hand wide unto him, and shalt surely lend him sufficient for his need, in that which he wanteth" (15:7-8). Moreover, "the poor shall never cease out of the land: therefore I command thee, saying, Thou shalt open thine hand wide unto thy brother, to thy poor, and to thy needy, in thy land" (cf. 15:11; 22:1; 24:14).

Israel was to care for the stranger and, as always, God was their model: "[God] loveth the stranger, in giving him food and raiment. Love ye therefore the stranger; for ye were strangers in the land of Egypt" (10:18-19).

Since God is just, his people must also be just. The test for determining whether courts were administering justice was to see whether those with the least power in the community—the widows and orphans—received justice when their interests conflicted with those who had much power. God cannot be bribed, neither should those who judge in his behalf accept bribes: "For the Lord your God is God of gods . . . which regardeth not persons, nor taketh reward [Heb.: shobad, gift or bribe]: He doth execute the judgment of the fatherless and the widow" (10:17-18). Again, God was the model for his children who could become divine by following a divine example. The Lord said: "Judges and officers . . . shall judge the people with just judgment. Thou shalt not wrest judgment; thou shalt not respect persons, neither take a gift; for a gift doth blind the eyes of the wise, and pervert the words of the righteous. That which is altogether just shall thou follow" (16:18-20). Under such a mandate, King Jehoshaphat many years later sent a solid signal to his judges: "Ye judge not for man, but for the Lord, who is with you in the judgment. Wherefore now let the fear of the Lord be upon you; . . . for there is no iniquity with the Lord our God, nor respect of persons, nor taking of gifts [shobad]" (2 Chron. 19:6-7).

Conflict of interest could shatter a society of one heart and one mind. Therefore, in business dealings, where personal interest tends to shatter one's commitment to fairness and honesty, Israel was to avoid the temptation to seek dishonest or unjust gain by not planning how to do it. She was never to use two standards—one honest and one dishonest: "Thou shalt not have in thy bag divers weights, a great and a small. Thou shalt not have in thine house divers measures, a great and a small. But thou shalt have a perfect and just weight, a perfect and a just measure shalt thou have: that thy days may be lengthened in the land which the Lord thy God giveth thee" (25:13-15). Later, when covetousness set in, Amos, Micah, and Jeremiah lamented the loss of integrity in business and spoke about the impending falls of their respective nations as a consequence of their failure to bridle their covetousness.

These laws suggest the value that God placed on having a holy, honest, just and merciful society. Whoever loves these characteristics and attributes may know that he really loves God. Conversely, whoever has no desire to be holy, truthful, merciful, or just will know, by this fact, that he does not really love or want God. How willing one is to comply with God's laws and live by his standards becomes a test of how much one actually wants God.

Administrators in Zion are humble servants of God and the people. They do not elevate themselves above their subjects. Every king was to read from his personal copy of "this law," "that his heart be not lifted up above his brethren, and that he turn not aside from the commandment, to the right hand, or to the left" (17:18-20). Humility of leaders before their people was another divine (Christ-like) virtue to be sought by those who were appointed to lead.

Sacred Lessons From the Wilderness Experience

One of the most fascinating stories in the scriptures is the story of how God, who saves his children from all their enemies, including death, deliberately led Israel into the wilderness of Sinai. In that hostile environment he showed, as he could under no other conditions, that in their extremity he is strong in behalf of all who put their trust in him. Israel also learned that survival did not depend merely on having full supplies of bread. God did have a plan, and he saved them from every enemy they encountered.

When the wilderness experience was over, Moses stood in their presence and testified: "The Lord thy God hath blessed thee in all the works of thy hand: he knoweth thy walking through this great wilderness: these forty years the Lord thy God hath been with thee, thou hast lacked nothing" (2:7). What a testimonial for him to express in front of that generation who had been there—who, looking back, knew they agreed with him.

The Bible shows us how little knowledge Israel had about God when they left Egypt and, therefore, how little trust they had in him. In fact, time and again the people made it clear that they did not trust him at all. They did not know him and his marvelous resources, and consequently, they did not see how they could survive in that forbidding wilderness. Wherever they looked they saw disaster—death either by starvation or thirst, or, surviving, death finally at the hands of Canaanites. They saw no salvation from any of their enemies; all they could see was death. Little did they know that God had already planned this trip into the wilderness, that he had sufficient bread in heaven to feed them, that he could make water come out of unexpected places, that he could make walls tumble, and that five of them could put a hundred of the enemies to flight. So they murmured, and complained all along the way, because they did not know that God was trustworthy. If they had known God as Moses did, they would have trusted in his judgment, his preparation, his power, and his knowledge, even as Moses did. Moses, excepting one complaint—that the load seemed too heavy for him to bear (Num. 11:14)—never murmured at God's demands.

The rising generation grew up knowing God as their great provider and savior. So Moses challenged them to be sure that subsequent generations knew the things they had learned from experience about God that provided the basis of their testimonies. It was this knowledge about God, gained in a wide variety of experiences, that would help the new generation learn what others knew about him. It would provide the basis upon which they might make their own discoveries about him and build their own testimonies. The value of teaching and learning from the lessons of the past has caused the prophets today to challenge us to prepare and hand down journals and records of how the Lord has worked in our own lives. Doing this seems to be Moses' major objective in writing Deuteronomy, and it is for us a major reason for studying it. The Israelites learned many things from their experience in the wilderness:

A. God talks to man and man survives: "Behold, the Lord our God hath shewed us his glory and his greatness, and we have heard his voice out of the midst of the fire: we have seen this day that God doth talk with man, and he liveth" (5:24).

B. They had not only heard God's voice, as a people, but had also seen how he had taken them to freedom, which is a divine value. Had God ever taken a nation "from the midst of another nation, by temptations, by signs, and by wonders, and by war, and by a mighty hand, and by a stretched out arm, and by great terrors, according to all that the Lord your God did for you in Egypt before your eyes?" (4:33-34). "Beware lest thou forget the Lord, which brought thee forth out of the land of Egypt, from the house of bondage" (6:12).

We who have seen or heard of the agony of freedom fighters around the world know how impossible it is for people to become free when the will of the state is so opposed. And yet God freed Israel from Egypt by his own devices. How important it is to future generations to know their God as a liberator, who ultimately liberates from all enemies, including the entangling power of sin.

C. God is unique: "Unto thee it was shewed, that thou mightest know that the Lord he is God; there is none else beside him" (4:35). Because this real God brought them out of Egypt, they could be assured that he would drive out nations greater and mightier than they (4:36-38).

D. Because "the Lord he is God in heaven above, and upon the earth beneath: there is none else. Thou shalt keep therefore his statutes, and his commandments, which I command thee this day, that it may go well with thee, and with thy children after thee, and that thou mayest prolong thy days upon the earth, which the Lord thy God giveth thee, forever" (4:39-40).

E. Man does not live by bread alone. Perhaps the most dramatic evidence of the Lord's closeness to Israel came on a daily basis, when he sustained their lives by the gift of manna in their hour of daily need. Their survival in the wilderness was a major testimony to his care and power.

At one point Moses pointed out the interrelationship between human need, divine power, and the nature of this physical world when he explained the meaning of Israel's forty-year stay in such an environment: "And thou shalt remember all the way [who could forget that!] which the Lord thy God led thee these forty years in the wilderness, To humble thee, and to prove thee, to know what was in thine heart, whether thou wouldest keep his commandments, or no" (8:2).

That God tests his children is a familiar idea, especially since the overall purpose of the Lord in placing his children on this earth is to "prove them herewith, to see if they will do all things whatsoever the Lord their God shall command them" (Abr. 3:25). To modern Israel he has said: "I have decreed in my heart . . . that I will prove you in all things, whether you will abide in my covenant, even unto death, that you may be found worthy" (D&C 98:14). In that wilderness, death was a reality, survival was at stake, life and death were issues; the unknown was ever present, and faith in God—his purpose in bringing them there, his ability to see them through, his love for them—were all subject to question. God was not only testing his children, but his children were also testing God, and under such dramatic conditions God was shown vividly not to be wanting. The irony is that when God tests his children, his children test him. When and where God proves man worthy, man proves God worthy of trust. Obviously, the wilderness—a capsulized earth, containing as it did the conditions any laboratory test might need for such purposes—served God's purpose well. When blessings come after faith, the condition that engendered the faith becomes the real blessing. If God had wanted merely to get Israel from Egypt to Canaan, he would have chosen a more direct route. But the Lord needed more to get Egypt out of Israel. This was something that took much longer.

Moses explained what the Lord was doing in the wilderness in these words: "[God] humbled thee, and suffered [allowed] thee to hunger, and fed thee with manna, which thou knewest not, . . . that he might make thee know that man doth not live by bread only, but by every word that proceedeth out of the mouth of the Lord doth man live" (8:3). In the wilderness Israel had survived with God when they followed his directions—something all who survive come to know. Life is more than a body sustained by bread; it is also an essence of the soul that is sustained by faith. Israel gradually learned to trust God with their physical lives. But more importantly they learned that by trusting him they lived more fully than any bread could have provided: "For the Lord thy God hath blessed thee in all the works of thy hand." That magnificent testimony came out of very real wilderness experience! "He knoweth thy walking through this great wilderness: these forty years the Lord thy God hath been with thee; thou hast lacked nothing"—that is, nothing essential (2:7). As examples, Moses cited: "Thy raiment waxed not old upon thee, neither did thy foot swell, these forty years" (8:4). God loved them enough to discipline them. "Consider in thine heart," he said, "that, as a man chasteneth his son, so the Lord thy God chasteneth thee" (8:5).

How like life are wildernesses. A veil separates us from God—our support. Life and death are the issues, without the knowledge that God knows our particular wilderness and has chartered our way. Under such circumstances we venture forth. Surrounded by opposition, we discover in the process that he has chartered the way, the dangers are known, and to the extent we trust him he will guide and direct us to the water and bread of life, help us overcome the poisonous serpents that bite, and help us triumph over every hostile force. Gradually we learn that after our faith has been tried, the blessing comes—the discovery that God was there. If there had been no trial, there would have been no blessing.

Teaching the Lessons of Life

Growth comes only from experience. It may be simulated and it may be shared, but, without experience, there is no growth. At the same time, if there is no memory of experience, i.e., no recall of what happened, then the experience may be useless. While experience is essential to growth, memory is the tool of its use. Whoever has experience but does not remember it is doomed to repeat it until he does. The benefits of experience, however, last as long as the memory does. Old age, with an accumulation of much experience, should produce wisdom. Who else can advise travelers on how to travel the course better than those who have so recently been over it? The elder generation may be no more intelligent than the younger, but the inexperienced, who hope to avoid life's needless pains and better know its infinite joys, are wise to listen when experience talks.

Israel was challenged to remember: "Keep thy soul diligently, lest thou forget the things which thine eyes have seen, and lest they depart from thy heart all the days of thy life; but teach them thy sons, and thy sons' sons" (4:9). Because the experiences of that older generation were unique, they would not be duplicated. And since only memory could preserve them, how tragic it would be to forget them.

But what should they remember?

God had spoken directly to them as a people. Some of them knew personally the sound of his voice. Each of them knew what he had said to them. The whole experience had been filled with awe and wonder: "Ye came near and stood under the mountain; and the mountain burned with fire. . . . And the Lord spake unto you out of the midst of the fire: ye heard the voice of the words, but saw no similitude; only ye heard a voice. And he declared unto you his covenant, which he commanded you to perform, even ten commandments; and he wrote them upon two tables of stone" (4:11-13). They could personally testify: "The Lord our God hath shewed us his glory and his greatness, and we have heard his voice out of the midst of the fire: we have seen this day that God doth talk with man, and he liveth" (5:24). Although Moses referred to this as though this were a "face to face" encounter between Israel and God, he had served as their mediator (5:4-5).

There was no question about the origin of the law on the stone tablets, about the sound of the voice, about what the voice was calling them to do, about whether Moses got the message right, about Moses' divine role as God's prophet. That generation had witnessed the primary events relative to the origin of their faith. Thus they could witness to the next generation what they had experienced. What generation ever had any greater witness that God was working with their leader? Their record becomes a primary document for us in seeing how God instills faith in his children. By reading it, we also may have a vicarious experience through it with the Lord, if we read by his Spirit.

Since much of Deuteronomy relates to a bygone day and a fulfilled law, one may wonder what relevance it may have for us who live in the light of the gospel fulness? This, of course, is the larger question that any student of the Old Testament must ask himself in order to justify the expenditure of time essential to study this large variegated book.

As indicated above, God can only be trusted when he is known to be trustworthy. Studying the records of God's words and acts helps us to know him better, because he is today the same God with which Israel dealt. The Bible is one of our oldest source-books dealing with God's words and his acts. And the book of Deuteronomy provides us with prophetic commentary on a very important creative period of Israelite history, a period which helps us interpret many of the events recorded in the preceding records. Such records of God's dealings with his children help us better to understand him and, consequently, we know better how to work with him for the betterment of our souls.

We all tend to pity ourselves when difficulties arise or opposition or persecution occur; we may wonder how a loving and divine Parent could allow or cause to happen what has occurred. Perhaps we suspect that he does not love us or he would not let such seemingly bad things befall us; one tends to think that whenever the Lord loves someone, he cushions him against life's hardships and protects him from life's blows. But the stories of Joseph, or Jeremiah, or Nephi, or Jesus, or Paul, or Joseph Smith, or Spencer W. Kimball, and especially Moses and the children of Israel in the wilderness, show us this is not so. When we see what has happened to these and other noble servants in other ages, we tend to whimper more silently.

All of us have a deep spiritual need for orientation—for being pointed in the right direction. The Old Testament, by showing us a long sweeping view of God at work with very special people, helps us see the big picture of our own lives inside the larger framework.

The book of Deuteronomy is a jewel among the jewels in God's hand. How fortunate we are to be able to learn vicariously that "man doth not live by bread alone." How much is it worth to learn such a lesson? To what would one be willing to submit in order to learn it? While Israel may not have deliberately chosen to learn the lessons, in retrospect they could see through the eyes of a seer what a blessing God had been in their lives. We who read the story understand and are fascinated to observe God's method as he works to perfect his children.

Without the divine perspective given in Deuteronomy we might have wondered at the wisdom of God in taking Israel into that wilderness. Moses let us see, however, what really was happening out there in that terrible wilderness of Sinai. Any true history can only be written when the divine perspective is known.

And Moses made an end of speaking all these words to all Israel:

And he said unto them, Set your hearts unto all the words which I testify among you this day, which ye shall command your children to observe to do, all the words of this law.

For it is not a vain thing for you; because it is your life: and through this thing ye shall prolong your days in the land, whither ye go over Jordan to possess it (32:45-47).

Moses knew that God could be relied on to meet all of the future needs of Israel, as he had met their need in the past. Deuteronomy described a trustworthy God.
