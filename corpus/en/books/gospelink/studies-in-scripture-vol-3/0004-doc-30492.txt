APPROACHES TO THE PENTATEUCH

S. KENT BROWN

The text of the Bible preserves the richest continuing legacy of sacred literature in the world. And Latter-day Saints have been beneficiaries of this abundant heritage, seeing connections to it even throughout our modern scriptures. For instance, the Book of Mormon traces the history of three groups of people whose roots lay in the ancient Near East and the world of the Bible: the Jaredites, Lehi's family, and the Mulekites. Moreover the Doctrine and Covenants, in specific passages, both adds to and explains not only problematic but also deficient passages in the Bible. 1 In this essay I wish to note two important items. The first has to do with the distinctively biblical view of what was important in Israel's origins. The second has to do with contemporary views of the composition of the Pentateuch.

Israel's Beginnings

When one examines the point of view embedded in the Pentateuch in general and in the book of Genesis in particular, one senses a movement from the large to the small. Genesis opens with the grandeur of the creation of the heavens and the earth, narrated from the point of view of one standing upon the earth and its watery waste during the process of its organization. The reader's attention is next directed toward our primordial parents, Adam and Eve, and the start of their family life. Consistent with the theme of beginnings, we are then introduced to troubles which would come to characterize human life on the earth, i.e., the struggle between good and evil typified in the conflict of Cain against his brother Abel.

The Bible next traces the growing human family, while focusing more particularly on those persons who bore the covenant which was originally vouchsafed to Adam and Eve. Tracing this line of righteous persons brings us to Noah, who, along with his family and because of the flood which destroyed all life, became our second primal father, another Adam as it were. In this regard Noah stands at a beginning, a constantly recurring motif in Genesis. After listing the seventy descendants of Noah in Gen. 10 (in the Hebrew text 2), the Bible then narrows its focus to a clan descended from Shem, one of Noah's sons. Within this clan stood a man named Terah, whose righteous son Abraham was chosen by the Lord to bear the divine covenant to the inhabitants of the earth. With this selection, the narrative has now come to focus on Abraham and Sarah, the individuals whose descendants—whether physically or spiritually—would carry the covenant of God's blessings to the inhabitants of the earth.

The Bible has thus brought us from the sweeping, grand vista of the creation—by gradually narrowing our view and directing our focus—to the humble family of Abraham and Sarah. With this the narrative begins to trace the story not only of this small family but also of the enormous impact which it was to have on others down the corridors of time. As a bearer of the covenant, Abraham thus represents another beginning, restoring what had been possessed in other ages and in other places. 3

As we read the pages of the early books of the Bible, we find ourselves following this special family, through trials and triumphs, from one generation to another, a story told at times in rather detailed sequence and at other times only sketchily. Jewish tradition says that it was the Prophet Moses who was responsible for that story and its compilation. 4 Interestingly that view has been challenged so seriously that today very few biblical scholars believe that Moses was responsible for its composition, opting instead to accept the notion that the materials in the Pentateuch were gathered and compiled in a much later age. Let us turn to review the major hypotheses concerning how the early books of the Bible came to be.

Theories About the Composition of the Pentateuch

The Documentary Hypothesis

This theory affirms that there was a series of documents drawn up in antiquity which, through an editorial process, finally became the first five books of the Old Testament, the Pentateuch. This view—articulated chiefly during the last century—is associated with the names of two German scholars, Karl Heinrich Graf and Julius Wellhausen. 5 While those who subscribe to this hypothesis do not agree in details, they do accept the following general outline. In the first place, they propose, there were no written histories per se of the ancient Israelites until the period of the kings. It was then that a person living in the south during the united monarchy—possibly the reign of Solomon (ca. 950 B.C.)—is believed to have drawn up an account of the history and traditions handed down among his people. This account, called J because of its consistent use of the name Jehovah, consisted generally of stories now in Genesis and the narrative portions of Exodus and Numbers, although it did not include everything which we now have in those books.

The second "document" is called E, which stands for the idea that a second author, some one hundred years later than J (ca. 850 B.C.), wrote a similar narrative of the events of Israel's past, using throughout the name of Elohim for the name of God. This person is thought to have lived in the kingdom of Israel, the northern half of the territory originally united under the rules of Saul, David, and Solomon. As with the first author, the second is said to have written of events which eventually found their way into Genesis, Exodus, and Numbers. Afterwards, according to the theory, the E document was joined to the J narrative by an editor during the eighth century, perhaps shortly before the fall of the northern kingdom to the Assyrians in 721 B.C. This combined narrative then formed a sort of national history.

The third document is labeled by the letter D, which stands for Deuteronomy. We note that in 621 B.C., during a major renovation of the temple, a book of law was found during the reign of King Josiah of Judah (2 Kgs. 22:3-20). From the description of this work it has been concluded by most scholars, including those who follow the documentary hypothesis, that the law book found in the temple was Deuteronomy, or at least an early edition of it. In 2 Kgs. 23:3, the book of the law is said to have contained God's "commandments and his testimonies and his statutes" as well as "the words of this covenant." Because these terms are especially at home in the book of Deuteronomy as opposed to other parts of the Pentateuch, it has been assumed widely that the law book found in the temple was at the very least a version of Deuteronomy, perhaps shorter in length than the one which we possess today. 6 In my view, this notion is a rather plausible deduction. The question remains, however, whether this law book was composed during Josiah's reign (640-609 B.C.) or during an earlier era which would be more consistent with the idea of Mosaic authorship.

The last of the four documents hypothesized is called P, which stands for a priestly text. It was after returning from exile in Babylonia that the former inhabitants of Judah turned to the temple with a fervor which allowed the priests to become the most prominent authorities in ancient Jewish society. In an effort to solidify the temple as the center of the worship, according to the theory, the priests drew together the laws which governed worship and its attendant features of life. When assembled, these laws constituted the book of Leviticus and the legal portions in the late chapters of Exodus. The priests were also thought to be responsible for editing the whole Pentateuch into more or less its current shape. Some have suggested that Ezra had a good deal to do with this literary activity after his arrival in Jerusalem from Babylon. However, other recent studies propose earlier dates for the writing of the P source, placing it before the Exile. For instance, Menahem Haran dates it to the reform of Hazekiah (late eighth century), while D. N. Freedman dates it to the last half of the seventh century. 7

While objections to the philosophical underpinnings of the Documentary Hypothesis have been dismissed by its proponents, 8 serious criticism of this view has persisted. This is because it was erected on the Hegelian principles of cultural and historical evolution, that is, that things progress from the simple to the complex. 9 The Graf-Wellhausen view holds that the most complex stage of ancient Israelite society was represented by the post-exilic community living in and near Jerusalem. Since the most complex parts of the Pentateuch included those which have to do with temple worship and priesthood matters, it was consequently concluded both that these parts were composed last and that the simpler, unadorned narratives were composed earliest. It is especially in light of recent archaeological discovery concerning the ancient Near East that the most serious challenges are being mounted against this particular view of the development of ancient Israelite literature. 10 For it is being shown now that civilizations were often at some sort of zenith when initially founded. 11

Ceremonial Life and the Hexateuch

Gerhard von Rad advanced a hypothesis which has not received wide support but has some interesting dimensions. Among others, Professor von Rad noted that there were extremely early references to ceremonial formulae recited among ancient Israelites which rehearsed the acts of God performed on behalf of his people Israel. 12 Importantly, von Rad also noted that these passages sketched in general outline the history of the people of Israel from the patriarchs down through the conquest under Joshua. Because these ceremonial recitations featured the conquest, it was natural to think that, under the influence of these recitations, the attempt to write the history of Israel included the book of Joshua with the first five books of the Old Testament, thus creating a Hexateuch (six-fold book) rather than a Pentateuch. The interesting element in this theory is the notion that the influence of ceremony was so strong that it affected the way in which Israel viewed its own past. In this view, temple life stood at the center of all things and virtually dictated how ancient Israelites viewed their place in the cosmic scheme.

Oral Tradition

Another influential "school" has been the Scandinavian group whose intellectual center has been at Uppsala University in Sweden. Representatives of this point of view have held that the materials preserved in the first five books of the Old Testament were memorized and thus kept by oral tradition for generations before being put into writing. 13 Their key model for demonstrating that someone could keep such traditions in his or her head consists of the modern group of "law reciters" of Iceland, a professional guild of people who both memorize the entire corpus of Icelandic law and are employed in the courts of that country. On this view, ancient Israelites are believed to have memorized the traditions, laws, and accounts of their forebears, something which was handed on by memory. Such persons, having learned these materials by rote, could then pass them to the next generation. And it was only when there was a national crisis, such as the threat of the destruction of the northern tribes by the Assyrians and the imminence of the destruction of the Kingdom of Judah by Babylonia, that the traditions known to those who had memorized them were finally reduced to writing because of the fear of losing life and thus memory.

The basic contention of this view is that memory is more accurate than manuscript. For in copying and recopying manuscripts, errors can be introduced, whether through additions or omissions. On the other hand, people who memorize such things remember them more accurately than they can be recopied onto the written page. Naturally, whether or not this view holds true remains to be demonstrated, since it is clear that the activities of the law reciters of Iceland finally cannot be employed as a precise measurement for what happened in ancient Israel. There simply is not enough known to be able to affirm whether or not any persons actually functioned in this manner either among the priests or within the royal courts.

Family Histories

One theory which has caught my eye is that proposed by R. K. Harrison. A number of years ago he suggested that the phrase "these are the generations of . . ." constitutes the closing line of a family history. 14 In fact, he suggested that this oft-recurring phrase in the book of Genesis may deliniate the end of the tablets upon which these family histories were written. 15 It was then left to a later compiler (Moses, in Harrison's view) to gather these tablets together into a single narrative which sketched a history from the creation down to the descendants of Jacob. While this view is not without its problems, 16 the theory has its attractions. Doubtless there were records upon which Moses or those associated with him drew for the materials of the Pentateuch. Such is especially evident in the Book of Moses in the Pearl of Great Price, which quotes from a record of Enoch, 17 which in turn quotes from a record of Adam. 18 That there were written records which preceded the composition of the Pentateuch is without doubt true. One immediately thinks of Gen. 14, which derives from a source independent of its current surroundings. One also recalls the so-called Covenant Code (Ex. 20:22-23:33) and the Holiness Code (Lev. 17-26). Abraham mentioned "the records [which] have come into my hands" (Abr. 1:28). Further, one must include old liturgical formulae, such as those noted in note 12, along with extremely early poetic pieces (e.g., the song of Deborah in Judg. 5 and the songs of Moses and Miriam in Ex. 15) and ancient genealogical lists (e.g., Gen. 46:8-27 and Ex. 1:1-5, both of which passages I have dealt with in my essay cited in note 2 above). Some of those earlier written sources are even cited by name in the biblical text, e.g., the Book of the Covenant (Ex. 24:7), the Book of the Wars of the Lord (Num. 21:14), the Book of the Upright (or, Jasher: Josh. 10:13 and 2 Sam. 1:18), and the Book of Samuel the Seer (1 Chron. 29:29). 19 Harrison's view takes account of this idea and suggests that Moses really had something to do with the composition of the first five books of the Bible, which included compiling earlier records as the basis of parts of that work.

The Impact of Archaeology

No other recent development has had more influence on how the Bible is currently read by scholars than the emergence of the science of archaeology, especially as it affects the world of the Bible. 20 Since the days when Flinders Petrie first went to Palestine to excavate the site of Eglon in 1890, biblical studies have not been quite the same. 21 No longer can one skeptically dismiss this or that incident in the biblical text as being unhistorical or unintelligble, 22 for discoveries at a number of key sites have underscored the essential reliability of much of the biblical narrative. 23 One thinks for example of the excavations of Professor Yigael Yadin at the ancient site of Hazor in the Galilee. This site has yielded clear evidence of a major destruction at the end of the Late Bronze Age which coincides with the invasion of the Israelites into Galilee as chronicled in Josh. 11. 24

While sites such as Jericho 25 and Ai 26 still present problems, one cannot discount that the archaeologist's spade has unearthed material remains and texts which have illuminated the world not only of the patriarchs 27 but also of those who came conquering with Joshua, 28 and much more. Because of its impact, the science of archaeology has spawned a generation of scholars who do not take for granted the results of the previous generations of Old Testament investigators and instead seek a synthesis between what is known from the biblical text and what can be learned from the archaeologist's work, leading to a much more positive view of the trustworthiness of the biblical materials themselves. 29 Such will continue, for it has now become an important ingredient of study to investigate thoroughly what the earth can tell us about the past. As the past comes more and more into focus in this way, we shall have opportunity to view more clearly and with greater understanding the narratives of Israel's beginnings.
