RUTH

STEPHEN D. RICKS

Introduction

The book of Ruth, which Goethe described as "the loveliest complete work on a small scale," appears in the "Writings," the third division of the Hebrew Bible (which is divided into sections designated as "Law," "Prophets," and "Writings"). It is one of the five Megillot ("scrolls"), each of which is read during a Jewish feast or fast. Ruth is recited during Shavuot, or the Feast of Weeks. 1 In the King James Version (as also in the Septuagint and Vulgate versions) the book appears immediately following the book of Judges, doubtless because the events which are outlined in the book occurred during the reign of the Judges.

No indication of authorship is given in the book of Ruth itself. According to the Talmud (Baba Bathra 14b), the prophet Samuel wrote the book of Ruth (as well as the book of Judges and 1 Samuel). However, the genealogy at 4:18-22 is probably a later addition and postdates Samuel, whose life must have been roughly contemporary with the events described in Ruth; in addition, the description of the removal of the sandal in 4:7 as a custom from the remote past suggests a date substantially after the occurrences outlined in the book. The period of the early monarchy appears to be a likely one for the composition of Ruth. 2

Outline of the Book of Ruth

The content of the book of Ruth may be outlined in the following manner:

I. Sojourn in Moab (1:1-5)

A. Because of famine, Elimelech of Bethlehem-Judah 3 departs with his wife, Naomi, and two sons, Mahlon and Chilion, for Moab

B. Elimelech dies (1:3)

C. Mahlon and Chilion marry Moabite women, Ruth and Orpah (1:4)

D. Mahlon and Chilion both die without children (1:5)

II. Return to Bethlehem-Judah (1:6-22)

A. Hearing that the famine has abated, Naomi, now a motherless widow, decides to return to Bethlehem (1:6-7a)

B. Orpah and Ruth both express the desire to go to Bethlehem (1:7b)

C. Naomi attempts to dissuade her daughters-in-law and finally succeeds with Orpah (1:8-14a)

D. Ruth remains firm in her desire to return with Naomi (1:14b-18)

E. Naomi and Ruth return to Bethlehem, where they are met by the women of the town (1:19-22)

III. Ruth Encounters Boaz (2:1-19)

A. Ruth gleans in a field belonging to Boaz, a kinsman of Elimelech (2:1-3)

B. Boaz, coming from Bethlehem to inspect his fields, asks his overseer about the identity of Ruth (2:3-7)

C. Boaz addresses Ruth, inviting her to remain in his fields gleaning and eating with his workers (2:8-14)

D. Boaz tells his workers to allow Ruth to glean among the sheaves if she wishes to (2:15-16)

E. After the day's work, Ruth returns to Naomi with an ephah of barley (2:17-18) 4

F. When Ruth tells Naomi where she had been gleaning, Naomi informs her that Boaz was a kinsman (2:19-20)

G. In accordance with Boaz' invitation, Ruth continues to glean in his field until the end of the barley and wheat harvest (2:21-23)

IV. Naomi's Plan (3:1-3; 18)

A. Naomi tells Ruth to find Boaz at the threshing floor in the evening and to lie down by him and "uncover his feet" (3:1-5) 5

B. Ruth acts according to Naomi's plan (3:6-7)

C. Boaz is startled at first (3:8)

D. Ruth asks that Boaz act the part of a kinsman (i.e., marry her) (3:9)

E. Boaz agrees to do so in the event that the nearer kinsman does not wish to (3:10-13)

F. In the morning, Ruth returns to Naomi with six measures of barley (a gift from Boaz) and a report of the night's events (3:14-18)

V. Events at the Gate (4:1-12)

A. Boaz finds the nearer kinsman at the city gate and asks him to redeem Naomi's property and marry Ruth (4:1-5)

B. When the nearer kinsman refuses to redeem the property and to marry Ruth, Boaz expresses a willingness to do so (4:6-12)

VI. Marriage of Ruth and Boaz (4:13-18)

A. Boaz and Ruth wed (4:13a)

B. A son is born to Ruth and Boaz, whom Naomi's neighbors name Obed (4:13b-17)

VII. Genealogy cited from Pharez (the son of Judah and an ancestor of Boaz) through Obed to King David

Social Customs in the Book of Ruth

The book of Ruth is rife with allusions to social customs and legal procedures observed at the time. An understanding of these customs will enhance an appreciation of the book. Among the most important of these are the references to gleaning in the fields (2:2-17), the redemption of property (4:3-7), and the remarriage of a childless widow.

Gleaning. According to what must have been an ancient custom, codified in Leviticus and Deuteronomy, landowners were expected to leave the "corners" of the fields for the less fortunate members of the community to glean from. Thus in Lev. 23:22 we read: "And when ye reap the harvest of your land, thou shalt not make clean riddance of the corners of thy field when thou reapest, neither shalt thou gather any gleaning of thy harvest: thou shalt leave them unto the poor, and to the stranger" (cf. Lev. 19:9-10). Similarly, in Deut. 24:19 the Israelites were commanded: "When thou cuttest down thine harvest in thy field, and hast forgot a sheaf in the field, thou shalt not go again to fetch it: it shall be for the stranger, for the fatherless, and for the widow." Ruth, as a widow and a resident alien (Hebrew ger, rendered "stranger" in the KJV of Lev. 23:22 and Deut. 24:19), had a right to glean in the fields. It was a sign of Boaz' generosity (as well, perhaps, as a response to Ruth's request in 2:7: "I pray you, let me glean and gather after the reapers among the sheaves") that he told his workers to allow Ruth to glean throughout the field. That Ruth gathered an ephah (i.e., 29-50 pounds) of barley in one day attests to her success in gleaning.

Redemption of Property and the Remarriage of a Childless Widow. At a pivotal point in Ruth, Boaz hailed someone called "Such a One" (Hebrew almoni peloni, something like "Mr. So and So") at the city gate and brought him before the elders of the city, who happened to be sitting there, and advised him of his opportunity to redeem the property of Naomi. At first "Such a One" seemed interested and expressed a willingness to purchase the property. At this juncture, however, Boaz informed him, "On the day that you purchase the field from Naomi, I will be acquiring Ruth the Moabitess, the wife of the deceased, in order to raise up a name for the deceased upon his inheritance" (4:5—my translation). 6 This vital passage can best be understood in the light of the Israelite custom of redemption of property and the remarriage of a childless widow, practices which were designed to preserve property within the family and to perpetuate the memory of the deceased.

The kinsman's right of redemption of property is outlined in codified form in Lev. 25:25-34. According to the custom in Israel, a kinsman had the right to redeem property that had been sold on account of poverty. The order of those having the right to redeem property may have been the same as the order for inheritance: brother, paternal uncle, and the "kinsman that is next to him of his family" (Num. 27:11). However, the situation in Ruth is somewhat different from the Leviticus legislation. In Lev. 25:25, because the property had already been sold, the stress was on the right of the kinsman to redeem it. In Ruth, the property of Naomi had not yet been sold, but the relative was given the right of first refusal. 7 No obligation to do so is stated in Leviticus, nor is it implied in Ruth. Still, we must assume that this was the standard practice during the time the events recounted in Ruth took place, since, as D. R. G. Beattie pointed out, "a story-teller . . . will not create a legal situation which his audience will know to be impossible." 8

The remarriage of a childless widow 9 (codified in one particular form in the Pentateuch in legislation referred to as the "levirate law") is a practice mentioned in the twenty-fifth chapter of Deuteronomy:

If brethren dwell together, and one of them die, and have no child, the wife of the dead shall not marry without unto a stranger; her husband's brother shall go in unto her, and take her to him to wife, and perform the duty of an husband's brother unto her.

And it shall be, that the firstborn which she beareth shall succeed in the name of his brother which is dead, that his name shall not be put out of Israel (Deut. 25:5-6). 10

The situation in Ruth differs from the "levirate law" codified in Deuteronomy in two important respects: (1) Boaz was clearly not a brother-in-law (Hebrew yabam, Latin levir), and (2) there seems to have been no obligation for either Boaz or "Such a One" to marry Ruth, or it would certainly have come to their attention soon after the arrival of Naomi and Ruth from Moab. However, in one way—which is important for our appreciation of "Such a One's" reaction to Boaz' declaration of his intention to marry Ruth—the remarriage of a childless widow had the same effect as the "levirate": any offspring born to that union would have the right to inherit the property redeemed from Naomi. "Such a One's" initial willingness to redeem the land probably stemmed from his expectation that it would eventually pay for itself. However, the prospect of creating an heir to that property through Ruth, which heir might also be eligible to inherit the property that "Such a One" already owned and would otherwise have fallen to the sons he already had, made the purchase far less appealing. As a consequence, he encouraged Boaz to "redeem thou my right to thyself; for I cannot redeem it" (4:6b). Part of the storyteller's art lies in the suspense in which he kept his listeners for "Such a One's" reaction to Boaz' expression of intention to marry Ruth after "Such a One" had already said that he would redeem the property.

Theology and Literary Characteristics

The dramatis personae of the book of Ruth are unusual, if not unique, among the narrative writings of the Old Testament: in the entire story there is no king, no royalty, no priests, Levites, Judges, or prophets. All the characters in the story were ordinary people who lived in a town far removed from the centers of power, intrigue, and violence. A similarly striking feature of Ruth is its absence of traditional manifestations of Israelite religion: there is no evidence of the priesthood, nor is there a trace of oracles, visions, dreams, or any of the other usual means of determining God's will. Still, God's "all-causality" was felt continually as he moved in the background of events in the lives of everyday people who possessed the virtues of patience, loyalty, generosity, and trust. It brings the story to its successful and happy resolution. 11

Related to the theme of God's "all-causality" is God's love, which extends to the lives and homes of the simplest of his people, as well as across national borders to traditional enemies of Israel. Ruth is repeatedly referred to as "the Moabitess" in the course of the story (1:22; 2:2, 6, 21; 4:5, 10). Yet, despite the fact that she was a foreigner, and despite the traditional antagonism between Israelites and Moabites (cf. Deut. 23:3-6), through God's love she was permitted to become a mother in Israel and to play an even larger role in God's design as the ancestress of David (4:22) and his greater Son Jesus (Matt. 1:1). 12

Naomi left Bethlehem-Judah with a husband and two sons. When she returned to her home, she was widowed, bereft of both her sons; she brought only a Moabite daughter-in-law with her. Naomi lamented upon her return, "I went out full, and the Lord hath brought me home again empty" (Ruth 1:21). It seemed to Naomi that "the Almighty [had] dealt very bitterly" with her. Nevertheless God, in his never-flagging love, directed the subsequent events which led to the marriage of Ruth and Boaz and the birth of Obed, whom Naomi lovingly held in her bosom and for whom she acted as a nurse at the end of the story.

Ruth is a masterpiece of the storytellers' art. With an absolute economy of words, without the use of a "villain" or any real antagonist, without the entrance of kings or priests onto the stage of the story, without the searing emotions or wrenching conflicts of which many stories are made, the teller of Ruth maintains the attention and interest of the listener from beginning to happy conclusion. As long as ordinary people who demonstrate the simple virtues of loyalty, love, and generosity are valued, the book of Ruth will remain a cherished part of our biblical heritage.
