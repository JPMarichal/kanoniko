THE JUDGES OF ISRAEL

DONALD W. PARRY AND STEPHEN D. RICKS

The Judges in Israel

The book of Judges is named after the book's thirteen protagonists, who led Israel between Joshua and Saul. 1 Its author is unknown, but it appears that it was written during the monarchy (after ca. 1000 B.C.). The period of the Judges began with the death of Joshua (Judg. 1:1) and concluded with the anointing and accession of Saul as king (1 Sam. 10:1). Othniel, the younger brother of Caleb, served as Israel's first Judge (3:9), and the sons of Samuel the prophet were the final Judges before Saul became the first king of Israel. The narratives of the judges Eli, Samuel, and Samuel's sons are included in 1 Sam. 1-10, and the book of Ruth (1:1) also notes that it is to be included within the historical period of the Judges.

The Judges were not simple definers of the law or judicial leaders. The Hebrew term shophet, translated "judge" in the sense of a "lawgiver . . . or governor," 2 is derived from the Hebrew verb shaphat, meaning "to act as ruler." 3 According to C. K. Kraft, the judges of Israel "were not so much legal consultants as champions or heroes vindicating Israel in war." 4 However, at least one of the rulers of Israel sat in judgment and functioned as an adjudicator, in addition to being a war hero (4:5). Others believe that judging was "acting so as to uphold the covenant" made at Sinai. 5 The Lord told Nathan that he had commanded the "judges of Israel" to feed his people (1 Chron. 17:6). This would make them shepherds of the flock of Israel and wardens of the Sinaitic covenant.

The book of Judges was written with a clear didactic and moral intent, that is, to show, through the flow of historical events, that obedience to Jehovah brings life and peace, while disobedience results in hardship, oppression, and death (see, for example, Deut. 27-29). The writer of Judges took great pains to show that the invariable result of apostasy from the worship of Jehovah was affliction and foreign domination. Israel had been forewarned by Jehovah:

And this people will rise up, and go a whoring after the gods of the strangers of the land, whither they go to be among them, and will forsake me, and break my covenant which I have made with them....

For when I shall have brought them into the land which I sware unto their fathers, that floweth with milk and honey; and they shall have eaten and filled themselves and waxen fat; then will they turn unto other gods, and serve them, and provoke me, and break my covenant (Deut. 31:16, 20).

Moses also warned the Israelites that if they were disobedient, they would be "smitten before their enemies," would "be oppressed and spoiled evermore," would "be oppressed and crushed always," and would "serve other gods" of "wood and stone" (Deut. 28:25-37). The book of Judges chronicles the fulfillment of these stern promises. When the covenant people forgot Jehovah and served idols of wood and stone, the resultant curses occurred: the Israelites were "oppressed," "spoiled," and "crushed."

The book of Judges describes a recurring cycle of apostasy, oppression, repentance, and deliverance, with Jehovah playing a pivotal role in that cycle. When the Israelites abandoned the worship of Jehovah for the rites of the Canaanite deities, Jehovah allowed the neighboring nations to afflict them. Following a period of oppression, the Israelites became humble and prayerful and begged the Lord for deliverance from their enemies. In response to their cries, Jehovah sent a war hero ("judge") to deliver the people.

This cycle of apostasy, oppression, repentance, and deliverance occurs several times in Judges. Each time, the narrator of the book uses the same or similar language to describe the phases in the cycle: (1) apostasy: "And the children of Israel did evil in the sight of the Lord"; (2) oppression: "And he sold them into the hands of their enemies"; (3) repentance: "And the children of Israel cried unto the Lord"; (4) deliverance: "And the Lord raised up a deliverer." A chart showing each of these elements in the cycle as they occur in the book of Judges is given on the following page. Each instance of apostasy and oppression is a confirmation of the prophetic promises of Moses and serves as a warning to future generations.

Apostasy: Part One of the Cycle

The narrator of Judges begins each cycle of apostasy with the same words, which he repeats seven times in thirteen chapters: "And the children of Israel did evil in the sight of the Lord" (see chart). The prophets had repeatedly warned Israel not to worship other deities (Ex. 20:5; 22:20; 34:14). In the Decalogue, God forcefully commanded: "Thou shalt have no other gods before me. Thou shalt not make unto thee any graven image" (Ex. 20:3-4). However, Israel was attracted to the myriad deities of the surrounding nations and "went a whoring after" them, "bowed themselves" before their fashioned stones and chiseled logs (Ex. 2:17), served these deities (Ex. 2:19), and even sacrificed their own offspring to them (Ps. 106:34-46).

The two deities which posed the greatest challenge to the integrity of Israelite worship were Baal and Ashtoreth. Baal, characterized as the supreme storm-god, controlled the winds, clouds, rain, lightning, thunderings, and all the elements of the atmosphere. He was often depicted as grasping a thunderbolt with his left hand and an uplifted mace in his right hand. A helmet is shown on his head, adorned with the horns of a bull, symbol of fertility. 6

Ashtoreth, the consort of Baal, was an astral goddess of fertility who caused or increased the fruitfulness of plants, animals, and man. Ashtoreth was generally depicted naked, her head adorned with horns, and her bosom and pudenda emphasized. She was associated with sensuous love. 7 On many occasions, both in the book of Judges and elsewhere in the Bible, the Israelites abandoned the exclusive worship of Jehovah for Baal and Ashtoreth: "And the children of Israel did evil in the sight of the Lord, and served Baalim. . . . And they forsook the Lord, and served Baal and Ashtaroth" (Judg. 2:11, 13).

On the surface, there were numerous similarities between aspects of the religions of Canaan and Israel. Like Jehovah, Baal had his prophets (1 Kgs. 18:19-25; 2 Kgs. 10:19), 8 his temple (2 Kgs. 11:18; 10:21-27, 2 Chron. 23:17), his altar (6:25-30; 2 Kgs. 11:18; Jer. 11:13), his high places (Jer. 19:5; 32:35), and his priests (2 Kgs. 10:19). Furthermore, sacrifices and burnt offerings were offered to him (2 Kgs. 10:19, 24; Jer. 19:5), worshippers wore special vestments while in his temple (2 Kgs. 10:22-23), he was entreated with prayer (1 Kgs. 18:24, 28), and incense was burned on his behalf (Jer. 7:9; 11:13). As O. R. Gurney has suggested, "the ritual observances at a Hebrew and at a Canaanite sanctuary were so similar that to the mass of the people Jehovah worship and Baal worship were not separated by any well-marked line." 9 However, the fundamental difference between the two systems lay, from the point of view of the Israelite historians, in the nature of the two deities: while Baal and the other deities of the Canaanite pantheon were no more than "molten images" (2 Chron. 28:2), Jehovah was "the true and living God" (Alma 7:6). Hence the writer of Judges expressed strong condemnation of the Israelites when they "did evil in the sight of the Lord, and served Baalim" (Judg. 2:11).

Oppression: Part Two of the Cycle

According to the cyclical pattern in the book of Judges, oppression followed the apostasy of Israel. Again, the narrator uses similar language when introducing the "oppression" section of the apostasy cycle. From the passages listed in the chart, it is evident that the Lord allowed the neighboring nations to attack and oppress Israel. According to the writer of Judges, God either delivered Israel "into the hands of their foes" (2:14; 6:1; 13:1) or he "sold them into the hands" of their enemies (2:14; 3:8; 4:2; 10:7). Repentance: Part Three of the Cycle

The invariable Israelite reaction to foreign domination was repentance. The phrase constantly repeated in the repentance phase of the cycle is "and the children of Israel cried unto the Lord" (3:9, 15; 4:3; 6:7; 10:10). A particularly striking example of this phase of the cycle is found in Judg. 10:6-16, which describes how Jehovah sold the Israelites "into the hands of the Philistines, and into the hands of Ammon" (10:7). These nations "vexed and oppressed" (10:8) the Israelites to the extent that Israel was "sore distressed":

And the children of Israel cried unto the Lord, saying, We have sinned against thee, both because we have forsaken our God, and also served Baalim....

We have sinned: do thou unto us whatsoever seemeth good unto thee; deliver us only, we pray thee, this day. And they put away the strange gods from among them, and served the Lord (10:10, 15-16).

God's response to the contrition of the Israelites was compassion: God's "soul was grieved for the misery of Israel" (10:16), and he raised up another Judge to deliver the Israelites from their foreign oppressors.

Deliverance by the Judges: Part Four of the Cycle

It is evident that the Judges were not merely individuals who became leaders through their charismatic qualities. Many scriptures demonstrate that the Judges were appointed and chosen by Jehovah to deliver Israel from its frequent bondage. The New Testament says that the Lord "gave unto them judges" (Acts 13:20); similar language in Judges says that "the Lord raised up judges" (2:16) and "the Lord raised up a deliverer" (3:9, 15). Elsewhere, Jehovah "commanded judges to be over my people Israel" (1 Chron. 17:10) and told them to "feed my people" (1 Chron. 17:6). The epistle to the Hebrews states that many of the Judges "subdued kingdoms" through their faith (Heb. 11:32).

Many Judges, such as Othniel (3:10), Gideon (6:34), and Jephthah (11:29), were blessed with the "Spirit of the Lord." Of Samson it was said that "the Spirit of the Lord came mightily upon him" (14:6). Deborah was a prophetess (4:14), Jephthah's leadership began at a solemn ceremony (11:10-11), and an angel appeared to Gideon during his call to the judgeship (6:11-12). An angel also appeared to the mother of Samson announcing to her that Samson would "begin to deliver Israel out of the hand of the Philistines" (13:5).

Many of the Judges were called moshi'a (the Hebrew word meaning "deliverer" or "savior"). As such they acted as temporal saviors for Israel. Jehovah is the ultimate moshi'a (Isa. 43:3; 45:15; Ps. 40:17; 144:2) in both a spiritual and a temporal sense. He is the power that made the Judges successful in bringing deliverance to Israel. As a "man of war" (Ex. 15:3), the Lord went out before the Israelite army (4:14). Israel's battles were "the Lord's battles" (1 Sam. 18:17; 25:28), and the enemies of Israel were the foes of Jehovah (5:31). In addition, Jehovah was consulted before a holy war was initiated (20:23, 28), sacrifice was offered before a battle (1 Sam. 7:9-10), and battle participants had to be ritually clean (Deut. 23:9-10). Further, Israelite soldiers had to be willing to do battle (5:2, 9), and those lacking courage were sent home (7:3; Deut. 20:8). Finally, the Spirit of the Lord moved upon many of the judges directly, both before and during their confrontation with the enemy (3:10; 6:34; 11:29-30; 14:19).

Often in the period of the Judges, the Israelites obtained freedom from bondage through Jehovah's miraculous aid and intervention. Sometimes the Israelites won the battles against their enemies in the most unlikely manner. From twenty thousand volunteers, only three hundred men were chosen for Gideon's army against a much more numerous enemy, and even these three hundred men never entered the field of battle (7:2-21). Similarly, Samson killed a thousand men with the jawbone of an ass (15:15) and later died along with more than three thousand Philistine nobles and lords after causing the roof of Dagon's temple to collapse upon them (16:23-31). Gideon, the war hero against the Midianites, was the least in a poor family of Manasseh (6:15), while Jephthah, the champion against the Midianites, was the son of a harlot (11:1). Similarly, Sisera, the captain of the Canaanite army, was slain by a woman called Jael (4:21), and Abimelech, an adversary within the ranks of Israel, died after he was struck in the head by a stone that was pushed from a city wall by an unknown Israelite woman (9:53).

Conclusion

Probably no historical book of the Old Testament has so transparent a "philosophy of history" as does the book of Judges. Through its use of constantly repeated phrases for each of the phases in the cycle, the writer of Judges has driven home the inescapable lesson of the period of the Judges: apostasy and disobedience will inevitably result in sorrow and affliction. Similar patterns and lessons of history also form a constant refrain in the Book of Mormon: prosperity often results in pride, pride in iniquity, iniquity in oppression (often at the hands of the Lamanites), and oppression brings the people to humility and repentance—which ends in the Lord's intervention and deliverance. This pattern is clearly laid out in Helaman 12:

At the very time when he doth prosper his people,...then is the time that they do harden their hearts, and do forget the Lord their God, and do trample under their feet the Holy One—yea, and this because of their ease, and exceedingly great prosperity.

And thus we see that except the Lord doth chasten his people with many afflictions, yea, except he doth visit them with death and with terror, and with famine and with all manner of pestilence, they will not remember him (Hel. 12:2-3).

If the scenes in which modern history plays itself out have changed slightly, the theme and the lessons of Judges (and the Book of Mormon as well) retain a telling relevance for our time.
