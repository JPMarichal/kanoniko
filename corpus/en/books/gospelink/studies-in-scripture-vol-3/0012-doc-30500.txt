THE LAWS AND THE SANCTUARY (Exodus 19-40)

DAVID P. WRIGHT

This discussion of the laws which appear in the last half of Exodus can only be a summary. 1 In this article we will examine the laws as they pertain to (a) behavior in worshipping God and (b) behavior affecting one's fellow.

Laws Pertaining to Religious Worship

Perhaps the most important religious obligation put upon Israel was to worship Yhwh, 2 and him alone (Ex. 20:2-5 [— Deut. 5:7-10]; Ex. 23:24; 34:12-17). Israel was forbidden to make images of him or of any other god (20:4-5, 22; see Lev. 19:4; 26:1; Deut. 12:1-4; 16:21-22; 27:15). One who sacrificed to gods other than Yhwh was to be proscribed (i.e., put to death; Ex. 22:20; cf. Deut. 13 and 17:2-7, where apostates were to be put to death; see also Ex. 23:32-33; Lev. 20:1-5). This penalty, as we have seen, was operative in the aftermath of the golden calf episode (Ex. 32:27-28, 35).

Besides avoiding idolatry, one was to otherwise show respect for deity. For example, one was not to use God's name improperly and unpurposefully (Ex. 20:7 — Deut. 5:11). This would mean that one was not to use his name in an oath in an act of perjury or swear an oath without keeping the obligations one thereby put upon him- or herself (see Lev. 19:12). This would also mean one was not to blaspheme God's name (Lev. 24:10-16; note here the death penalty). In connection with this, the Covenant Code warned that one was not to treat God contemptuously (Ex. 22:28). Also related to the command of properly using God's name was the command not to mention the names of other gods (23:13). This implied that one was not to take oaths in the names of other deities.

Another of Israel's obligations toward God was that of observing the Sabbath (Ex. 20:8 [— Deut. 5:12-15]; Ex. 23:12; 34:21). All work, by master and slave, was to be avoided. Work thus prohibited would include even minor things like kindling fire or gathering wood for a fire. The punishment for such was death (31:15; 35:2-3; Num. 15:32-36; see also Jer. 17:19-27; Neh. 13:15-22).

Another act offensive toward God was sorcery (Ex. 22:18). Those practicing this were not to be allowed to live. It appears that this law had foreign peoples mainly in mind, but it would have applied also to Israelites who practiced such. Other similar practices, such as divination and conjuring ghosts—also practices of many of the other peoples in the ancient Near East—were likewise prohibited (see Lev. 19:26, 31; 20:6, 27; Deut. 18:9-14).

Certain regulations in the laws of Exodus 19-40 had a ritual orientation. The focus of ritual legislation in the Covenant Code and the "Ritual Decalogue" (34:5-26) was the celebration of the three annual pilgrimage festivals: the Passover and Feast of Unleavened Bread (Ex. 23:15; 34:18, 25; Ex. 12; Lev. 23:5-8; Num. 9:1-14; 28:16-25; Deut. 16:1-8; Ezek. 45:21-24), the Feast of Weeks (— Pentecost, Shevuot; Ex. 23:16a; 34:22a; cf. Lev. 23:15-21; Num. 28:26-31; Deut. 16:9-10), and the Feast of Booths (— Tabernacles, Ingathering, Sukkot; Ex. 23:16b; 34:22b; cf. Lev. 23:33-36; Num. 28:12-39; Deut. 16:13-18; Ezek. 45:25).

Other ritual prescriptions in the Covenant Code and "Ritual Decalogue" included the following: Israel was to bring the firstfruits of their produce to God (Ex. 22:29; 23:19; 34:26; Num. 18:13; Deut. 26:1-11; Ezek. 44:30); they were to bring their firstborn animals (Ex. 22:29-30; 34:19-20; cf. 13:12-13; Lev. 27:26-27; Num. 18:15-18; Deut. 12:6; 14:23; 15:19-23); they were not to cook the meat from a goat kid in the milk of its mother (Ex. 23:19; 34:26; cf. Deut. 14:21; this law probably had a humanitarian basis; cf. Lev. 22:27-28; Deut. 22:6-7; 25:4); they were to build altars only of earth or unhewn stone and not build steps going up to them lest the priests' nakedness be exposed (Ex. 20:24-26; cf. 28:42-43; Deut. 27:5-6); they were not to eat meat from an animal that had suffered death by another animal (Ex. 22:31; on food prohibitions, see Gen. 9:4; Lev. 3:17; 7:23-27; 11; 17:10-16; Deut. 14:3-21; 15:23); sacrifices were not to be offered with leavened bread (Ex. 23:18; 34:25; cf. Lev. 2:11).

Other ritual laws are scattered throughout the sanctuary prescriptions. They deal with: the offering of the regular burnt offering (Ex. 29:38-42; cf. Num. 28:3-8; 2 Kgs. 16:15; Ezek. 46:13-15; Dan. 8:11-13; 11:31; 12:11); the setting up of the bread of presence (Ex. 25:30; cf. Lev. 24:5-9; 1 Sam. 21:1-7; 1 Kgs. 7:48); lighting the lights of the lampstand (Ex. 27:20-21; cf. Lev. 24:2-4; Num. 8:1-4); the preparation of incense and burning of it on the incense altar (Ex. 30:7-10 [v. 10 here refers to the annual application of blood to the incense altar on the Day of Atonement; see Lev. 16:16]; Ex. 30:34-37); the rite for the consecration of the sanctuary and priests with rules for the concomitant sacrifices (Ex. 29; 40; cf. Lev. 8-9); the recipe for the anointing oil used in the consecration rites (Ex. 30:22-33); the washing of the priests before ritual duties (30:17-21).

Laws Pertaining to Interaction with Others

The crime affecting one's fellow being the most is murder. The Decalogue states the prohibition against murder in an abbreviated manner: "Do not kill" (Ex. 20:12 — Deut. 5:17). This tersely worded prohibition is to be interpreted by the fuller discussion of murder in the Covenant Code and the rest of the Pentateuch. Murder which was premeditated was punishable; killing in self-defense and accidental homicide were not punishable (Ex. 21:13-14; 22:2-3; Num. 35:15-18, 20-24; Deut. 19:4-5, 11-13; Josh. 20:3). Murder was not just a social crime; it was a very serious offense toward God. Man was created in God's image and, hence, killing a person was akin to attacking God. The execution of the murderer was therefore required (Gen. 9:6). Moreover, murder pollutes the land; therefore, execution of the murderer was necessary so that the stain would be removed (Num. 35:33-34). The agent of execution was the victim's next of kin (Num. 35:19, 25-27; Deut. 19:6; Josh. 20:5; Judg. 8:18-21; 1 Sam. 14:5-11). In order to avoid the execution of those who killed in self-defense or by accident, the Bible instituted laws of asylum; the killer could take refuge at a sanctuary altar (Ex. 21:13-14; cf. 1 Kgs. 1:50-53; 2:28-34), or according to a different law, in a city set apart as a place of refuge (Num. 35:6-34; Deut. 4:41-43; 19:1-13; Josh. 20). The family member avenging the death could not execute the killer who had fled to one of these places until after judgment had taken place. If the killer was determined to have killed maliciously, he was turned over to the family member for execution (Num. 35:24-28; Deut. 19:11-13; Josh. 20:4-6). On homicide, see also Ex. 21:18-25, 28-32; Deut. 21:1-9, 22-23; 27:24).

Connected with murder were the laws dealing with injury of one's fellow. If one fought with and injured another person, the injurer had to compensate the injured for that person's loss of income and for the expenses of healing (Ex. 21:18-19). A slave whom a master struck and injured permanently was set free (Ex. 21:26-27). The law of talion ("an eye for an eye," etc.) in Ex. 21:23-25, where an injurer was to suffer as punishment an injury similar to that of the injured, could be literally carried out (this is the sense of Lev. 24:19-20; Deut. 19:19-21). But it is possible that a monetary fine often took the place of physical punishment. Only in the case of murder was physical punishment (i.e., execution; "life for life") demanded (Gen. 9:6; Lev. 24:17; Num. 35:31).

A sin of a magnitude near to that of murder was striking or otherwise showing disrespect for one's parents (Ex. 21:15, 17; cf. Deut. 27:16). The command to show respect to parents was of such fundamental importance that it finds a place in the Decalogue (Ex. 20:12 — Deut. 5:16; note the positive formulation; cf. Lev. 19:3). Elsewhere we learn that a child who acted rebelliously against his or her parents was subject to the death penalty (this penalty was dependent on the prosecution of the matter by the parents; Lev. 20:9; Deut. 21:18-21). See also Lev. 19:32.

Another very serious sin was adultery (Ex. 20:14 — Deut. 5:18; cf. Gen. 20:9; 39:9; Lev. 18:20; 2 Sam. 11). Adultery in the Old Testament context was sexual relations between a man, married or unmarried, and a woman who was betrothed or married to another man. The penalty for such was death for both parties (Lev. 20:10; Deut. 22:13-24). A betrothed or married woman who was raped entirely against her will was not subject to execution, but the rapist, of course, was (Deut. 22:25-27). Relations between a man, married or unmarried, and a woman who was not betrothed nor married was not adultery in the technical sense. Sanctions against such do not include the death penalty. Rather, in this case, the laws required the marriage of the couple if the woman's father allowed it and required the man to pay the bride price for the woman even if the marriage did not take place (Ex. 22:16-17; Deut. 22:28-29). It is important to note that though the death penalty was not required in this latter case, this does not mean that the act was not considered an affront to God and society. On this, see Gen. 34; Lev. 19:20-22; 21:9. On adultery, see also Num. 5:11-31.

Besides adultery, the Bible prohibits other sexual sins. According to the Covenant Code, one who commits bestiality was to be put to death (Ex. 22:19; cf. Lev. 18:23; 20:15-16; Deut. 27:21). The Pentateuch elsewhere prohibits incest (Lev. 18:6-18; 20:12, 14, 17, 19-21; Deut. 27:20, 22-23), homosexuality (Lev. 18:22; 20:13; cf. Deut. 22:5), relations with one's wife during menses (Lev. 18:19; 20:18; cf. Lev. 15:24), making one's daughter become a harlot (Lev. 19:29; 21:9; cf. Deut. 23:17-18), and remarrying a woman whom one had divorced, who had since been married to another man and had then been divorced from him (Deut. 24:1-4).

Kidnapping, like adultery, was a very serious crime and was to be punished by death (Ex. 21:16; Deut. 24:7). During biblical times, people kidnapped others so they could make money selling them as slaves (cf. the case of Joseph, Gen. 37:25-28, 36).

Related to the law against kidnapping were the prohibitions against theft in general. Indeed, because the command in the Decalogue ("Do not steal"; Ex. 20:13 — Deut. 5:19) uses the same verb as the kidnapping law in Ex. 21:16, and because the Decalogue is thought to be a listing of the most serious sins, some have thought that the Decalogue command has kidnapping solely in mind and not other kinds of theft. The prohibition in the Decalogue, however, seems to be directed at all kinds of theft (so also Lev. 19:11). The Covenant Code devotes much space to lesser cases of theft (Ex. 22:1-4, 7-9, 10-12). Generally speaking, the penalty for theft was two-fold restitution of the type of object stolen (22:4, 7, 9; but cf. v. 1). If one could not pay, he or she could be sold as a slave to pay for the theft (22:3). Related to laws of theft were the laws dealing with damage done to the property of one's neighbor and the compensation required for it (cf. 21:33-36; 22:5-6, 14-15; 23:4-5; see also Deut. 22:1-4). On theft see also Lev. 19:13.

Bible readers have long noted the relation of the prohibition not to covet the property and wife of one's neighbor (Ex. 20:14 — Deut. 5:21) to the laws of theft and adultery. This prohibition seeks to curb the desire that would lead to these other serious acts. This is an example of where biblical law sought to control attitudes and not just actions (cf. Lev. 19:17-18).

Several laws dealt with the proper treatment of those of a disadvantaged status or in a socially subordinate position. One who was a resident alien was not to be oppressed. The rationale for this was that the Israelites had a similar status in Egypt and therefore knew what it meant to be oppressed (Ex. 22:21; 23:9; cf. Lev. 19:33-34). The laws also warned of taking advantage of widows and fatherless children. These were particularly susceptible to exploitation or maltreatment because of their poverty and their lack of protection in an adult-male dominated society (Ex. 22:22-24; cf. Isa. 1:17; 10:2; Jer. 22:3). Lenders were not to charge interest from fellow Israelites (Ex. 22:25; Lev. 25:35-37; Deut. 23:19-20). Moreover, those to whom a debt was owed were not to take as a pledge for a debt their poor debtor's piece of clothing used at night (Ex. 22:26-27; cf. Deut. 24:6, 10-13). These laws were to prevent the financial exploitation of the poor. On the proper treatment of the poor and disabled, see Lev. 19:14; Deut. 10:18-19; 14:29; 15:1-11; 16:11-12, 14; 24:14-15, 17-18; 27:18-19.

Another law instituted in part for the benefit of the poor was the sabbatical year (Ex. 23:10-11; cf. Lev. 25:1-7; Deut. 15:1-11). The Israelites were not to plow and plant their fields in the seventh year. Instead, they were to let their fields grow wild, and whatever happened to grow in the field that year was to be for the poor. Other agricultural laws were instituted for the benefit of the poor (cf. Lev. 19:9-10; Deut. 23:24-25; 24:19-22; Ruth 2:2-3).

Another socially subordinated class was slaves. There were two classes of slaves in Israel: permanent slaves from the foreign populations in and outside of Israel, and temporary slaves who were Israelites who had become slaves to pay off their debts (see this distinction in Lev. 25:39-46). Ex. 21:1-11 deals with Hebrew (— Israelite) slaves. The male who entered slavery was to be released after six years. The female who was sold by her father to pay off a debt generally became a wife to her master or to one of his sons. Therefore, she was never released (cf. the interpretation of Ex. 21:1-11 by Deut. 15:12-18). The homicide laws treat the case of a master's culpability in killing a slave (Ex. 21:20-21). Since slaves were their masters' "money," masters had a right to beat them and thus force them to perform labor. But they did not have the right to beat them to death. If this happened, the guilty master would presumably be subject to the death penalty. On slaves, see also Deut. 23:15-16.

Finally, many laws legislated concerning the proper behavior of witnesses and judges in legal proceedings. Giving false testimony against another was prohibited (Ex. 20:16 [— Deut. 5:20]; Ex. 23:1-3). Deuteronomy prescribed that if one was found to be a lying witness, that person was to suffer the punishment of the crime which he or she was leveling against the other (Deut. 19:16-21). Judges were warned not to pervert justice (Ex. 23:6-8; Lev. 19:15; Deut. 16:18-20; 27:25).

The Furniture and Layout of the Sanctuary

The following discussion will briefly describe the arrangement of the portable sanctuary presented in chapters 25-31, 35-40 of Exodus, so that the reader may have an idea of its nature and function. 3 In our discussion of 33:7-11, 4 we observed that the portable sanctuary was to have its place in the middle of the Israelite camp (cf. Num. 2:2, 17). The area of the sanctuary was set off from the camp around it by a linen partition 100 cubits long and 50 cubits wide (Ex. 27:9-19; 38:9-20; a cubit is approximately 1 1/2 feet long). Inside this enclosure were located the Tent of Meeting and other pieces of sanctuary furniture. The entrances to the enclosure and the Tent itself had an eastward orientation (27:13-16; Num. 2:3).

The Tent of Meeting was composed of four different layers, one placed on top of the other. The first was the "tabernacle" (26:1-6; 36:8-13). This layer was visible on the inside of the sanctuary, and so it was finely decorated. The second layer was the "tent" (26:7-13; 36:14-19; 40:18-19). It was made of goat hair, just as many bedouin tents are today. The next layer was a covering of red-dyed rams' skins (26:14; 36:19; 39:34; 40:19). On top of this was a covering made from the skins of another animal (26:14; 36:19; 39:34; 40:19; what animal is intended by the Hebrew is uncertain). The Bible does not explicitly state what the dimensions of the Tent of Meeting were, but they can be deduced as being thirty cubits long, ten cubits wide and ten cubits high (cf. 26:15-23; 36:20-30). The inside of the Tent of Meeting was divided into two areas by a screen or veil (26:33; 40:3, 21), thus forming the adytum (i.e., the Holy of Holies, KJV, "most holy place") and the shrine (KJV, "holy place"). It appears that the dividing screen was placed twenty cubits from the entrance to the sanctuary, thus making the adytum a perfect cube, ten cubits in each dimension.

The ark of the covenant, over which were placed the kapporet (meaning unknown; some translate simply "cover"; KJV [incorrectly] renders "mercy seat") and cherubim (25:17-22; 37:6-10), is located in the adytum (25:10-16; 26:33-34; 37:1-5). In the shrine were found the table for the bread of presence (KJV, "shewbread"), on the north side (25:23-29; 26:35; 37:10-16; 40:22-23); the lampstand, on the south side (25:31-40; 26:35; 27:20-21; 37:17-24; 40:24-25), and the incense altar, in front of the screen (30:1-10, 34-38; 37:25-29; 40:26-27).

Outside the Tent of Meeting in the forecourt was the burnt-offering altar (27:1-7; 38:1-6; 40:6). On this altar, parts from the various animal and cereal offerings were burnt (cf. burnt offerings, Lev. 1:7-9, 12-13, 17; well-being offerings, Lev. 3:3-5, 9-11, 14-16; purgation [also called: sin] offerings, Lev. 4:8-11, 19-20, 26, 31, 35; reparation [also called: trespass or guilt] offerings, Lev. 7:3-5; cereal offerings, Lev. 2:2, 9, 16; 5:12 [this last case is a cereal offering which was brought as a substitute for a purgation offering]; 6:15, 19-23.

Somewhere between the opening of the Tent of Meeting and the burnt-offering altar was the laver (Ex. 30:18; 38:8; 40:7). This was used as a reservoir from which water could be taken for the priests to wash their hands and feet preparatory to their ritual duties (30:18-21; 40:31-32; the Hebrew in 30:19 and 40:31 says that the priests washed from the laver; i.e., they did not wash in it; cf. 2 Chron. 4:6).

A final remark about the layout of the sanctuary has to do with the exact placement of the Tent of the Meeting and the burnt offering altar. The Bible does not indicate their position within the court, but it appears that the intention was to have the offering altar in the exact center of the forecourt and the adytum (Holy of Holies) in the exact center of the back half of the court. This would place the entrance to the Tent of Meeting at exactly midcourt.
