# An Introduction to 1 and 2 Samuel

### An Introduction to 1 and 2 Samuel
Robert L. Millet, Kent P. Jackson, Studies in Scripture, Vol. 3: Genesis to 2 Samuel ( 1985)

AN INTRODUCTION TO 1 AND 2 SAMUEL

DAVID R. SEELY

The books 1 and 2 Samuel are a part of a continuous historical narrative that runs from Genesis through 2 Kings in the Hebrew Bible and recounts the history of God and his people from the creation of the world to the destruction of Jerusalem and the temple—an event that in many ways can be seen as the focal point of the history of the entire Old Testament. The destruction of Jerusalem in 587 B. C. was different than the destruction of any other city in the ancient world, as it was survived by a document—the Old Testament—which describes the origins of the Israelites, how they became the covenant people, and how Jerusalem became their capital and the site for the house of their Lord. This record also attempts to explain why, in spite of the exalted status of this people and the sanctity of their city, their own God punished them with invasion and exile and allowed his own house to be destroyed. Along with the numerous condemnations of Israel and the predictions of disaster found throughout its pages, the Old Testament also preserves promises of return and restoration with the hope that the lessons of the past could help God's people in the future to avoid a similar fate.

The key to the message of the Old Testament is covenant. God, through Abraham and Moses, called and elected a people to be a "holy" and a "peculiar" people (that is to say, "purchased through the blood of the Lamb," Deut. 7:6; 1 Pet. 2:9) and that through them "all the nations of the earth" would be blessed (Gen. 22:18). The people accepted the call to become his people—the children of God. They promised to obey his commandments and to live in such a way that he could live in their midst, and he promised to be their God and to protect and prosper them. From the very beginning, covenant defined the relationship of God with his people, and only through an understanding of covenant does the history of Israel have meaning. Obedience to the conditions of the covenant brought blessings, while disobedience resulted in disaster.

The record of God's people, preserved in scripture, recalls the history of this relationship. The memory of Adam in the Garden of Eden, the Exodus and the wanderings in the wilderness, as well as the dedication of the temple of Solomon, all dramatically demonstrate the blessings of the presence of God. At the same time the history of Israel recorded the inevitable result of disobedience—namely exile from the presence of God—from the Garden to the mortal world for Adam and Eve, from Samaria to Assyria and oblivion for the Northern Kingdom, and from Judah to Babylon for the Southern Kingdom. One of the tragic lessons taught by the Old Testament is that Israel was unable to learn the lesson of their own past even though it remained constantly before them—a sobering message for God's covenant people in any age.

1 and 2 Samuel span the period in this story from the time of the Judges, when because "there was no king in Israel, every man did that which was right in his own eyes" (Judg. 17:6) and when the twelve tribes of Israel were fighting for their very survival in the promised land, to the final years of King David. David was a brilliant and able monarch who had united the tribes of Israel into a mighty empire, and in whom God had founded an "eternal dynasty" through which would come the one and only true King. Although this is a very difficult time period to date with precision, these two books probably cover a time period of 100-150 years, somewhere between 1100 B. C. and 961 B. C. The books of Samuel describe how the change from tribal federation to organized royal state came about. The most prominent theme, of course, is the rise of the monarchy itself, which is alternately praised and condemned as it was to lead to Israel's greatness on the one hand and to contribute to her eventual demise on the other. Along with the monarchy was the establishment of the Davidic covenant, a covenant which was made not to Israel as a whole, but specifically to David and his house. A more subtle but equally important change in Israel is also quietly noted in the books of Samuel. One of the opening scenes of 1 Samuel takes place at a shrine at Shiloh—designated as the "House of the Lord" (1 Sam. 1:7)—which housed the Ark of the Covenant and was the site of annual worship and sacrifice for Elkanah and his family. A short section in 1 Sam. 4-7 records the story of the Ark as it was captured and returned to Israel, first to a temporary home at Kirjath-jearim and eventually to its permanent home in the capital of Judah at Jerusalem. 2 Samuel ends quietly with David's purchase of the threshing floor of Araunah, which was shortly to become the site for Solomon's temple—the final resting place for the Ark of the Covenant. The shift of worship from local shrines to the centralized worship at the temple was also a product of the monarchy and led to the supreme importance of Jerusalem as a political and religious center. In the books of Samuel, Jerusalem is transformed from a pagan Jebusite city to what remains even today the virtual center of the religious life of Israel—a symbol of the presence of God in the midst of his people.

Ultimately the work of the Lord is entrusted to and administrated by mortal men with all of their divinely endowed abilities as well as their mortal imperfections. The framework of the books of Samuel consists of the accounts of the lives of the key people: Samuel and Nathan—prophets; Eli, Zadok, and Abiathar—priests; and Saul and David—kings. The restructuring of the leadership in Israel under the monarchy gave new meanings to each of these offices. The establishment of a dynastic monarchy in Israel is introduced in the books of Samuel with the accounts of the wickedness of the sons of Eli and of Samuel, in spite of their divine callings and the righteousness of their fathers, and the tragic failure of Saul as the first king of Israel. The fact that righteous fathers can have unworthy sons and that the Lord's first anointed king was later rejected foreshadows the history of the dynastic monarchy founded by David. The consequences of the unrighteous acts of a king, a priest, or a prophet was to be counter-balanced by the other two of these three offices—a system of checks and balances. And the success of this interrelationship—especially between the king and the prophet—was the barometer by which the rise and the fall of the fortunes of Israel can be measured throughout its history. The books of Samuel record the early stages of these offices and the development of their relationships—for the better and for the worse.

This essay will briefly introduce the books of Samuel and their style, authorship, and text. It will serve as a preamble to the following chapters, which will discuss in much more depth the important characters and themes of 1 and 2 Samuel. The real value, of course, is in the biblical text itself, and it is hoped that these studies will ultimately lead the reader to appreciate them more.

Style and Authorship

The narrative of 1 Samuel is quite similar to the rest of the biblical prose found throughout the Bible. Generally, biblical prose is characterized by a series of many short episodes, often grouped together around a central theme or character. For instance, the narrative in 1 Samuel is roughly divided into three sections centering around the three major characters: Samuel (chapters 1-7); Saul and the rise of the monarchy (chapters 8-15); and the rise of David (chapters 15-31). David is the central character throughout the book of 2 Samuel, and the narrative is divided into the major periods of his reign: rise to kingship over Judah and Israel (chapters 1-5); the consolidation of the empire (chapters 6-12); the story of David's son, Absalom, and the problem of succession (chapters 13-20); and an appendix (chapters 21-24). For the most part, the narrative is presented in the third person, but it is vivid and captivating, as it often records the direct discourse of the speakers as well as their intimate thoughts and motivations.

Quite often the episodic style of the biblical writers and the intimacy of their narrative is taken for granted. The modern reader becomes accustomed to reading the text guided by the chapter divisions, which of course are determined by breaks in the narrative. While occasionally more than one episode is contained in a chapter, the chapter breaks almost always coincide with natural divisions between episodes. 1 And although the intimacy and immediacy of biblical narrative, as well as the episodic style, may seem foreign to a reader of modern history, it is quite familiar to the reader of literature of any age.

The method of biblical narrative has been compared often with the techniques of modern film-making, 2 where the audience is guided through the story by the omniscient and ever-changing perspective of the camera. It can focus on the inner grief and the joy of an individual like Hannah in the temple at Shiloh (1 Sam. 1-2), and then shift to the field of battle, the capture of the Ark, and to a foreign land where the conversation of the Philistine lords is recorded (1 Sam. 4-6). Or, in the story of Absalom, when the news of his death must be sent to his father, the audience is privy to the instructions that Joab gives the messengers who are to deliver the news and at the same time hears the conversation between David and his watchman who are awaiting the word (2 Sam. 18:19-33). The narrative can cover several years in a sentence or it can give a detailed description of a single day or hour in many episodes.

Emphasis in 1 and 2 Samuel can be created by juxtaposition, as with the interweaving of the righteousness and the rise of Samuel with the account of the blasphemy and demise of the sons of Eli (1 Sam. 2-3), or in the several intertwined episodes of the unsuccessful attempts of Saul to kill David with the instances where David was presented with the opportunity to harm Saul but chose to respect the Lord's anointed (1 Sam. 18-27). The juxtaposition of David's scheming to arrange the death of Uriah, the husband of Bathsheba, with Uriah's unyielding loyalty and devotion to his king (2 Sam. 11:6-25) is also a classic example of this device.

Description ranges from the details given to the armour of Goliath (1 Sam. 17:4-7) to the terse but equally effective description of Nabal as a man "churlish and evil in his doings; and he was of the house of Caleb" (1 Sam. 25:3). The detailed descriptions of David mourning the death of Jonathan (2 Sam. 1:11-12) and Absalom (18:33-19:4) help to emphasize the mere formality of Bathsheba's "grief" recounted simply: "she mourned for her husband" (2 Sam. 11:26).

Another favorite device of the biblical writers is irony. Saul's desperation is vividly portrayed in his disguised visit to a medium which he himself had expressly forbidden (1 Sam. 28:3-10). A masterful use of irony in the account of Nathan's confrontation with the king creates one of the most dramatic moments in the Bible, climaxing in the prophet's declaration to David, "Thou art the man" (2 Sam. 12:7), turning David's righteous indignation into damning self-condemnation.

The author (or authors) of the book of Samuel, like the authors of most of the books of the Bible, is anonymous, and his identity does not appear in the text itself. 3 The Talmud preserves the Jewish tradition that identifies Samuel as the author of the first part of the book that bears his name. 4 It should be noted that the titles of many of the books of the Bible may simply reflect the name of the main character of the narrative, such as the books of Joshua, Ruth, Esther, etc., and not necessarily authorship. Since the death of Samuel is recorded in 1 Sam. 25:1 (about halfway through the complete book of Samuel), the Talmud claims that the book was completed by Gad the seer and Nathan the prophet. 5 This assumption is probably based on an interpretation of 1 Chron. 29:29, which mentions the book of Samuel the seer, the book of Nathan the prophet, and the book of Gad the seer as other sources for the acts of David outside the record found in 1 Chronicles. Since the books of Nathan and Gad are clearly not extant, it is possible that the book of Samuel referred to here is not the biblical Samuel but is also a lost record. Even if the reference is to the biblical book of Samuel, it would only imply the authorship of Samuel for the events of his own lifetime and would not necessarily establish the authorship of the entire work.

Besides these lost books, the book of Samuel itself refers to another presumably lost work of Samuel in which the prophet recorded the stipulations for the monarchy (1 Sam. 10:25) and which he laid up "before the Lord" (a phrase commonly used in the Bible to refer to the temple). Also mentioned is the book of Jasher (2 Sam. 1:18). Although these books appear to be completely lost, and any remnant of them preserved in the biblical Book of Samuel would be impossible to identify, the references to many different contemporary records, including those of Samuel himself, probably accurately describe the historical reality behind the final production of 1 and 2 Samuel as we know it today.

The long time period of several generations covered in the book of Samuel as well as the apparent presence of several distinct narratives—each of which seems to have an independent unity—suggests that the final version of the book is the product of a somewhat later editor (or editors) who edited, combined, and intertwined material from several different early sources, some of which appear to be nearly contemporary with the events described. There is ample evidence of later editorial remarks throughout the books of Samuel that indicate that a final editing may have been somewhat later than the time of the events described (see 1 Sam. 3:1: "and in those days"; 1 Sam. 5:5; 2 Sam. 6:8; 18:18: "unto this day"; and 1 Sam. 9:9: "beforetime in Israel"; etc.). These editorial comments are similar to the oft-repeated editorial phrase "and thus we see" found throughout the Book of Mormon (see 1 Ne. 17:3; Alma 28:13-14; 30:60; Hel. 3:27; etc.).

In spite of the fact that any identification of the original sources in Samuel is purely hypothetical, a brief outline of the commonly suggested narrative sources can be helpful in recognizing, defining, and understanding the literary structure and some of the major themes of the book itself as well as how the different themes relate to one another in the narrative. Some of the suggested narrative sources for the books 1 and 2 Samuel include: the Story of Samuel (1 Sam. 1-7); the Ark Narrative (1 Sam. 4-6); the Saul Cycle (1 Sam. 8-15); the History of David's Rise (1 Sam. 16:14-2 Sam. 5); the Transfer of the Ark (2 Sam. 6); the Ammonite War (2 Sam. 10-12); and the Succession Narrative (2 Sam. 13-20). 6

Text

Although the title of the Book of Samuel comes from the Hebrew tradition, its division into two books (1 and 2 Samuel) derives from the third-century B.C. Greek translation of the Bible called the Septuagint (LXX). That 1 and 2 Samuel were originally one book in the Hebrew text seems clear from three observations. 7 First, the Talmudic references (compiled from the fourth-sixth centuries A.D.) always suppose that there is only one Book of Samuel. Second, the Masoretes, the medieval editors of the Hebrew text, inserted notes at the end of each of the biblical books in order to facilitate meticulous transmission of the text. These notes only appear at the end of 2 Samuel and record that the book as a whole contains 1506 verses and note that the middle of this book is to be found in 1 Sam. 28:24. Third, and most convincing, is the evidence provided by the discovery of the Dead Sea Scrolls. Cave Four at Qumran preserved the great first-century-B.C. Samuel scroll (4QSama), which demonstrates quite dramatically that both books were written on one scroll with no break in the text. In fact, the division of the book into two parts in the Hebrew text only occurred in A.D. 1448.

Apparently the division into two books first occurred in the early Greek translation of the Bible. The Greek translation of the Hebrew text was nearly twice as long as the Hebrew, due in part to the fact that whereas Hebrew script only indicates consonants, the Greek language notates the vowels as well. In order to fit the longer books (Samuel, Kings, and Chronicles) onto the standard-lengthed scrolls, they were divided in half. Samuel was broken at the death of one of its main characters—Saul—which conveniently appears near the middle of the text. The Greek version numbered the books of Samuel together with the books of Kings, since the subject matter was very similar, and called the four books "1, 2, 3, and 4 Kingdoms"—apparently because they dealt with the history of both the Northern Kingdom (Israel) and the Southern Kingdom (Judah). The Latin version translated "Kingdoms" into "Kings," and the KJV, as well as most other modern Christian translations of the Bible, restored to the books of Samuel their Hebrew title but retained the Greek division—thus 1 and 2 Samuel and 1 and 2 Kings.

The Masoretic Text (MT; the Hebrew text that has come to us from ancient times) of Samuel is problematic in several ways. First, it appears that there are internal problems with the transmission of the Hebrew text which make it difficult to interpret and to translate. Second, the Hebrew text diverges significantly from the Greek version (LXX) as well as from many of the other ancient versions. There are several instances where the MT preserves text that is missing from the LXX, but for the most part the Hebrew text is shorter than the Greek (defective). The discussion of the book of Samuel for many centuries has included a debate whether the Hebrew text is shorter because of omissions made in the text in its transmission (a very common problem in the transmission of ancient and modern texts, called haplography), or whether the Greek is longer because of additions made by translators and editors (such as emendations, glosses, conflations, etc., also common in LXX versions of other biblical books) that have survived in spite of the constant attempts through the years to bring the Greek versions back into conformity with the surviving Masoretic tradition.

The discoveries at Qumran brought startling new evidence to the discussion. 8 Cave Four produced a large scroll of Samuel (4QSama) and fragments from two other Samuel scrolls (4QSamb and 4QSamc) in Hebrew, dating from the third to the first centuries B.C. and preserving a considerable amount of text. A comparison of the texts with the surviving Hebrew and Greek texts shows that in spite of variance with both, the Qumran texts more closely resemble the Greek text than the Hebrew of the Masoretic tradition. 9 The complexity of the evidence as well as the presence of much variation at Qumran itself makes it difficult to reconstruct conclusively the textual families of Samuel and how they relate to the different versions. But it does seem clear that the variations seen in the Greek version were already present in very early Hebrew manuscripts and that they are not necessarily products of the translation process. 10 This has led to renewed interest in the LXX version of Samuel as possibly containing valuable ancient material that may assist in an interpretation of the text.

It may be of interest here to note some of the significant differences—one of which may be of special interest to the LDS reader. 1 Sam. 1:11 discusses the nature of the vow of Hannah in dedicating her future child to the Lord. The passage reads (with the Greek additions italicized): "I will give him unto the Lord as a doton [grant or dedication] all the days of his life, and wine and strong drink he will not drink, and there shall no razor come upon his head." For a long time these additions were supposed to have been additions by a translator or editor to further confirm the interpretation of this vow as a Nazirite vow, as is specified in a similar passage of the dedication of Samson in Judg. 13:3-5. The Hebrew of 4QSama gives the Hebrew origin of this text, and the word for "dedication" is "Nazirite."

Another interesting variant found in the Greek as well as at Qumran is the height of Goliath being specified in 1 Sam. 17:4 as "four cubits and a span," which would be about six foot nine instead of the "six cubits and a span" of the Masoretic text—either of which would have been a giant in ancient times. This raises the interesting question whether the difference may be attributed to an "exaggeration" or a "rationalization."

Of interest to the LDS scholar is an addition in the LXX version of 1 Sam. 14:41-42, where Saul was casting lots to determine who had broken the vow to fast. The Greek preserves a short description of how the lots were cast and includes the information that the sacred lots used in this situation were none other than the Urim and Thummim.

The Joseph Smith Translation also contains some changes and additions that the Prophet made in 1 and 2 Samuel. Some of the more significant are the changing of the phrase "it repented me [the Lord] that I have set Saul to be king" to a phrase indicating that it was Saul who did the repenting (1 Sam. 15:11 and 15:35) and in the changing of the phrase "the evil spirit of the Lord" to "the evil spirit not of the Lord" (in 16:14-23; 18:10; and 19:9), both of which resolve obvious theological problems caused by the passages. A similar change is found in 2 Sam. 24:16, where "the Lord repented him" is changed to "the people repented." In addition to these (which are noted in the footnotes of the LDS Bible) there are some significant additions made to 1 Sam. 28:9-15, which recounts the encounter of Saul and the witch of En-dor. Joseph Smith added the appearance of "the words of Samuel" in the vision along with the appearance of Samuel himself. 11

Summary

No amount of discussion, exposition, or commentary can substitute for direct contact with the text. Serious study of the Bible must ultimately be centered on the text itself. It is possible to establish a personal dialogue with this great book that changes and progresses through the years as we bring new spiritual and intellectual understandings to its reading.

An awareness of the literary and aesthetic elements of the narrative sharpens our eyes and ears to the way the story is told, for often the messages of biblical narratives are not directly stated but are encoded into the stories themselves. On the one hand, a study of the problems of authorship and text helps us to remember the distance in time and space between the ancient world of the Bible and our modern world and should motivate us to accept the challenges of serious scripture study. On the other hand, an awareness of the complexities of the Bible leads us to a greater appreciation of the simplicity and accessibility, as well as the profundity and timelessness of its message, and the urgency to apply it to our own lives.

The books of 1 and 2 Samuel tell the history of a covenant people and describe an actual relationship between God and his people that existed many years ago. They were written and preserved with the hope that the record of the past can improve the relationship between God and his people in the present and the future.
