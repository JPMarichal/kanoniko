Introduction

The visions that John has seen thus far have disclosed the nature of both those who fight against God and those who come under their influence. It has also revealed the twofold results that happen to both: first, a pervasive and addictive materialism that permeates their souls so deeply that repentance is impossible; and second, the punishment that justly and truly destroys them. But what was it that led them down the road to addiction and destruction? In the next two chapters, God shows John exactly who and what it was.

The perspective from which John continues to write is where the future has become the past. He now looks back on events that are actually more than two millennia into his future. The perspective acts to assure him and his audience of the sureness of the events he records. It underscores the seriousness with which God expects John and his readers to take his message.

The textual unit that begins here runs through chapter 19. The first three versesâwhere the angel tells John that he will show him the judgment on the great whore (17:1â3)âfunction as the introduction not only to the vision in chapter 17 but to all those that follow through the coming of the great King, Christ Jesus, and the destruction of those who oppose him. The focus of chapter 17 is, however, not on the whore but on her beast. There are two reasons for that focus. First, we cannot fully understand the great whoreâs significance and power without knowing the beastâs. Second, it allows us to see what leads up to and finally causes her destruction.

This unit has two unique features. First, this is the only place in Revelation where a detailed interpretive segment is woven into the narrative. Second, we have here the first appearance of an interpreting angelâthough a stock feature in other apocalypses, in Revelation there are but two, here and in 21:1â22:10.1

Introduction

The visions that John has seen thus far have disclosed the nature of both those who fight against God and those who come under their influence. It has also revealed the twofold results that happen to both: first, a pervasive and addictive materialism that permeates their souls so deeply that repentance is impossible; and second, the punishment that justly and truly destroys them. The perspective from which John continues to write is where the future has become the past. He now looks back on events that are actually more than two millennia into his future. Conclusion

The prominence of the whore in the drama of the latter days shows that when men cease to worship God, they do not cease to worship. They turn to a false form of religion to establish meaning and direction in life. The Seer casts the great evil of the last days in a double form. One is that of the seductive and shameless harlot, reveling in the pain and blood of the righteous. The other is that of the great city Babylon, mother of abominations, dominating kings and kingdoms while crushing truth, virtue, and love. The image of the harlot and the city, taken together, represent the formation of a kind of state religion that is able to impose its will on the people; but it will not last long, for it carries with it the seeds of its own destruction.

Babylonâs demise comes from a surprising source. The Old Testament, like the Book of Mormon, is clear that Babylon and all those that fight against Zion will come down and that the Lamb is going to be behind it, but neither text reveals just how. The scenario seems to run thus: the Babylonian army will not be able to destroy the Lamb nor his people. That does not mean, however, that it will cease its destructive intent.
