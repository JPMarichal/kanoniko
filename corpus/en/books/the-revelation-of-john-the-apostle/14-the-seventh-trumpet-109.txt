Introduction

The setting of chapter 11 is the same as that of chapter 10. As it opens, the seventh trumpet remains mute; tension continues. Before it can break, John must perform a task growing out of the recommissioning he received through angelic authority in the previous chapter. The task is one that looks to the far future, the period just before the Second Coming when the enemies of Israel will march against her with power. They have but one intent: Israelâs total destruction. God shows John how his faithful followers will be saved, how many of the Jews will be spared, and what will become of the holy city.

In chapter 10, we saw Johnâs recommissioning to preach against the world. Here we see what he was commissioned to preach. His was a warning, in large part, about what would happen to those who first reject Godâs word and then turn on those who are under his covenantal protection. The message is, therefore, one of judgmentâa judgment commencing when Godâs forbearance and long-suffering finally cease to be virtues, a judgment that finally yields to the plea of the fifth seal martyrs: âWhen will you vindicate our testimonies against the wicked?â (6:9â11; 8:3â5; 9:13). The answer is âNow!â

That answer constitutes the second woe. During the second woe (or sixth trump) evil has its day. A great and wicked army marches, and myriads upon myriads of humans die. Even under such severe brutalization, the inhabitants of the world refuse to repent. Thus, the devastations do not act to bring them to sorrow unto repentance. As a result, the world persists in its bloodlust and warfare. Jerusalem becomes ever more the object of hatred, whose enemies want nothing less than her total destruction. At that point, Godâs two prophets appear on the scene to both assist and teach the Jews. Their ministry occurs during the second woe or time of the sixth trumpet. Chapter 11 examines the portion of that woe that looks specifically at Israel.

Analysis and Summary

This section reveals how the Saints in the last day, and more particularly those in the Holy City, are going to survive a horrific onslaught, as will be seen. They will come under Godâs measure, that is, his protecting power. This pericope echoes that in Ezekiel 40â42, where the prophet sees the temple measured with great care. We find the same symbolism used in Zechariah 1:16, where Israelâs salvation is assured by the use of the measuring line.18 The emphasis of the text is that ultimate protection exists only at the center of divine activity: within the Church and temple. Covenant making and keeping are the keys to survival.

Though the temple in Revelation is likely symbolic of the faithful community of the Saints everywhere,19 that does not preclude a real Jerusalem temple being in place. Indeed, a scripture clearly states that âit is ordained that in Zion, and in her stakes, and in Jerusalem, those places which I have appointed for refuge, shall be the places for your baptisms for your deadâ (D&C 124:36). As such, the measuring of the temple parallels that of the sealing of the Saints in 7:3â8, each denoting the same act but from a different aspect.20

For those outside the bounds of the covenant, those who have never entered into or have not been faithful, there is no celestial shelter. Proximity will not be good enough. The outer court will be in no better position than the city itself. There is a message here. Certainly the sanctuary is itself a symbol of the Lordâs people, those who are the living temple of God wherein his Spirit dwells (see 1 Cor. 3:16â17; 2 Cor. 6:16; Eph. 2:19â22). Analysis and Summary

This section of Revelation is particularly difficult to interpret. According to D&C 77:15, the mission of the two prophets that stand at the center of chapter 11 is to prophesy to the Jews. Modern revelation gives us nothing more. Distinguishing, therefore, between what is symbolic and what is not has proven to be most challenging. Below we give our best analysis of what the text is communicating. We realize, of course, that time will reveal what actually happens.

The Revelation makes clear the means by which the righteous in the Holy Land will be preserved. The Saints having been sealed, human agents with divine power will act for God as protectors (see D&C 77:15). The prophet Isaiah was aware of two prophets who would assist Israel in the last days. According to him, of all of Israelâs sons, âthere is none to guide herâ in the precarious situation she will face in the last days. Their designation as âsonsâ suggests they have some connection to Israel, but in this case, it is indirect. According to scripture, âthey are two prophets that are to be raised up to the Jewish nationâ (D&C 77:15). Note that these prophets do not come from the Jews but are raised up to them. Nonetheless, they have at least a spiritual kinship with Judah and, as will be shown below, they will provide the means for the temporal salvation of the Jews.

John symbolically reveals the two witnessesâ high position with God. They stand before Him as two olive trees and two lampstands.44 Like the imagery of lampstands found in chapter 1, its use is appropriate here. In the earlier use, the lampstands represented the various branches of the Church, whose purpose was to hold up the light of Christ. The same is true of the work of the two prophets: they are to hold up the light of the Lamb and show forth in word and deed his power. God used the same imagery in Zechariah 4:1â14 to assure his people that his temple would be built in spite of all appearances to the contrary. In Revelation, the imageryâs use is much the same. It testifies that in spite of all appearances to the contrary, the Lordâs spirit will operate and thus provide the way against overwhelming odds for his people to win the day. These two prophets have tremendous power over sky, water, and fire.45 Until their ministry is completed, they will be absolutely unstoppable and indestructible.

Elder McConkie offers an interpretation of who these two prophets may be, stating that âthese two shall be followers of that humble man, Joseph Smith, through whom the Lord of Heaven restored the fulness of his everlasting gospel in this final dispensation of grace. No doubt they will be members of the Council of the Twelve or of the First Presidency of the Church.â46

It would appear that their ministry marks the moment when God openly moves onto the stage of history, exposing for more and more to see his saving power toward those who are his and his destroying power toward those who are willfully not.

Along with this event comes a break with the rules by which he has played in the past. Both in the Old Testament and the book of Revelation, the rule has been lex talionisâthat the punishment should exactly fit the crime. The idea is best expressed as âeye for eye, and tooth for toothâ (Ex. 21:24). Now, however, just the desire to hurt will bring death. Such vengeance seems extreme until one remembers two points: first, the wicked are rejecting God in the face of the overwhelming revelation of his power; and second, that rejection expresses itself clearly in its murderous intent against the Lordâs two anointed prophets. The adamant hardness of these people, coupled with their desire to kill, is sufficient to bring the death penalty.

Johnâs description of their ministry echoes the days of Elijah, when on two occasions fire consumed troops sent to arrest him (2 Kgs. 1:9â12). Two is the canonical number necessary to sustain a charge at court (Deut. 19:15; 2 Cor. 13:1; compare Deut. 17:6; Num. 35:30), and the witnessesâ number underscores this symbolism. The two prophets will provide the dual testimony against their antagonists that will bring down the full wrath of God. They are called witnesses because they will bear testimony of Jesus Christ and his latter-day work. Various events have been connected to this ministry that may actually take place before they begin their work. These include the building of a latter-day temple in Old Jerusalem,49 the conversion of some of the Jews to the Lord,50 and the battle of Armageddon.51 It would appear that their mission will take place not long before the return of the Savior.52

The place of their death, as John notes, is metaphorically called Sodom and Egypt. That John identifies Jerusalem with such epithets goes a long way in explaining why the outer court of the temple, that is, the city as a whole, is given over to the Gentiles. In spite of all that has gone on, the vast majority of its people will not be converted. Even under the severe conditions brought on by Armageddon, they will not accept the witness. For three and a half days, the bodies, serving as war trophies, remain unburied. Analysis and Summary

Murder is the greatest and last resort of the wicked and the base of their power. But what happens when the murdered do not stay dead? The ultimate power of the unrighteous fails and with it all other forms of coercion and intimidation. The resurrection of the two witnesses, then, signals the end of satanic peopleâs hold on the world. Their reign of blood and horror is now finished.

With the resurrection of the two witnesses, which essentially hamstrings the wicked, God begins to move directly onto the historical stage. No longer working behind the scenes, his first act is to prove that he has final authority over life and death. In doing so, he takes away all fear engendered by gangsters, bullies, thugs, and strong menâthose who have intimidated and abused the weak for millennia.

The number ten indicates, among other things, the whole of a part.77 Thus, it may be that the fraction indicates that judgment begins at the moment of ascension upon all the wicked who fought against the witnesses. That John uses the figure seven thousand as the number devoted to death lends support to this idea. Taking the number in its qualitative dimension, it signifies, as in other places, fullness and completeness. Thus, those slated to die are killed during this judgment.

But there are many spared. These are very likely the Jews who the prophets have been sent to protect. Conclusion

Chapter 11 moves through the second woe and introduces the third. But it only introduces the third. There is no description of the final onslaught (only a statement that God wrests control of the world), and no judgment. All this must wait for chapters 16 and 19 for the last woe to break upon the world. With chapter 12, God starts anew, and this time he uses a different point of view to disclose additional details to help the reader understand why the world will end in fire. Analysis and Summary

This section of Revelation is particularly difficult to interpret. According to D&C 77:15, the mission of the two prophets that stand at the center of chapter 11 is to prophesy to the Jews. Modern revelation gives us nothing more. Distinguishing, therefore, between what is symbolic and what is not has proven to be most challenging. Below we give our best analysis of what the text is communicating. We realize, of course, that time will reveal what actually happens.

The Revelation makes clear the means by which the righteous in the Holy Land will be preserved. The Saints having been sealed, human agents with divine power will act for God as protectors (see D&C 77:15). The prophet Isaiah was aware of two prophets who would assist Israel in the last days. According to him, of all of Israelâs sons, âthere is none to guide herâ in the precarious situation she will face in the last days. Indeed, her âsons have fainted, they lie at the head of all the streets [helpless] as a wild bull in a net.â Therefore, help must come from outside. âThese two sons,â notes Isaiah, âare come unto theeâ (JST Isa. 51:17â20). Their designation as âsonsâ suggests they have some connection to Israel, but in this case, it is indirect. According to scripture, âthey are two prophets that are to be raised up to the Jewish nationâ (D&C 77:15). Note that these prophets do not come from the Jews but are raised up to them. Nonetheless, they have at least a spiritual kinship with Judah and, as will be shown below, they will provide the means for the temporal salvation of the Jews.

John symbolically reveals the two witnessesâ high position with God. They stand before Him as two olive trees and two lampstands.44 Like the imagery of lampstands found in chapter 1, its use is appropriate here. In the earlier use, the lampstands represented the various branches of the Church, whose purpose was to hold up the light of Christ. The same is true of the work of the two prophets: they are to hold up the light of the Lamb and show forth in word and deed his power. God used the same imagery in Zechariah 4:1â14 to assure his people that his temple would be built in spite of all appearances to the contrary. In Revelation, the imageryâs use is much the same. It testifies that in spite of all appearances to the contrary, the Lordâs spirit will operate and thus provide the way against overwhelming odds for his people to win the day. These two prophets have tremendous power over sky, water, and fire.45 Until their ministry is completed, they will be absolutely unstoppable and indestructible.

Elder McConkie offers an interpretation of who these two prophets may be, stating that âthese two shall be followers of that humble man, Joseph Smith, through whom the Lord of Heaven restored the fulness of his everlasting gospel in this final dispensation of grace. No doubt they will be members of the Council of the Twelve or of the First Presidency of the Church.â46

It would appear that their ministry marks the moment when God openly moves onto the stage of history, exposing for more and more to see his saving power toward those who are his and his destroying power toward those who are willfully not.

Along with this event comes a break with the rules by which he has played in the past. Both in the Old Testament and the book of Revelation, the rule has been lex talionisâthat the punishment should exactly fit the crime. The idea is best expressed as âeye for eye, and tooth for toothâ (Ex. 21:24). Now, however, just the desire to hurt will bring death. Such vengeance seems extreme until one remembers two points: first, the wicked are rejecting God in the face of the overwhelming revelation of his power; and second, that rejection expresses itself clearly in its murderous intent against the Lordâs two anointed prophets. The adamant hardness of these people, coupled with their desire to kill, is sufficient to bring the death penalty.

Johnâs description of their ministry echoes the days of Elijah, when on two occasions fire consumed troops sent to arrest him (2 Kgs. 1:9â12). Likewise, those who attempt to stop the two witnesses will be smitten, so powerful will be the Spirit of the Lord upon them.47 As the Lord said to Jeremiah, so it will be with these prophets: âI will make my words in thy mouth fire, and this people wood, and it shall devour themâ (Jer. 5:14).48 On this basis, the fire that issues from the mouths of the two prophets is testimony that condemns the heathen to destruction.

Two is the canonical number necessary to sustain a charge at court (Deut. 19:15; 2 Cor. 13:1; compare Deut. 17:6; Num. 35:30), and the witnessesâ number underscores this symbolism. The two prophets will provide the dual testimony against their antagonists that will bring down the full wrath of God. They are called witnesses because they will bear testimony of Jesus Christ and his latter-day work. Various events have been connected to this ministry that may actually take place before they begin their work. These include the building of a latter-day temple in Old Jerusalem,49 the conversion of some of the Jews to the Lord,50 and the battle of Armageddon.51 It would appear that their mission will take place not long before the return of the Savior.52

The place of their death, as John notes, is metaphorically called Sodom and Egypt. That John identifies Jerusalem with such epithets goes a long way in explaining why the outer court of the temple, that is, the city as a whole, is given over to the Gentiles. In spite of all that has gone on, the vast majority of its people will not be converted. Even under the severe conditions brought on by Armageddon, they will not accept the witness. Indeed, they will reject even these prophets and oppose those who follow them. Perhaps it is some of these, working with the beast, who engineer the witnessesâ death. Overall, due to their refusal to repent and accept the Savior, the Jews will feel the full weight of the beast and its wicked minions.

The Lord has already revealed the full scope of the two prophetsâ power and the worldâs reaction in chapters 8 and 9. Though the text does not specify who unleashes the trumpet plagues described in those chapters, it appears to be these two prophets. Consider the following conceptual and lexical parallels. The text of Revelation gives the prophets a twofold ministry. First, they are to hold off the enemy at least for a time, thus helping to preserve the Jews temporally. Though not previously mentioned, in chapter 13 the beast will come more fully into view. There John reveals the beast is the minion of Satan and has worked for centuries to promote his ways. In the last days he will inspire his own minions to direct their full fury against the prophets.

The text suggests that it takes the beast some time to realize exactly who he is up against, but when he does, he will then direct his forces to move specifically against the two prophets. And he will win, perhaps with help from certain rebellious Jews or Christians.

The death of the two prophets will cause ecstasy among the wicked. The brilliant light of truth will burn the eyes and scorch the hearts of these people who had grown to love the dark. The invincibility of these prophets stood as a witness to the truth, truth that tortured because it threatened the very foundation upon which the philosophy of the wicked sat: that materialism was the reason for existence and that one could take happiness in wickedness.

The imagery of gift giving is telling here since in the Greco-Roman world, one never gave anything of value without expecting something in return.62 Thus, even at the moment of triumph, many take advantage of the situation and attempt to enrich themselves. Even now, greed and avarice find a place.

With the prophetsâ death, there appears to be nothing to stop the victory of evil. The beast from the pit has overcome. He has proved himself mightier than God. No wonder his lackeys rejoice. This is the moment of triumph; the beast and his army are victorious. But are they?

The Resurrection and Ascension of the Two Witnesses (11:11â14)

For three and a half days, the bodies, serving as war trophies, remain unburied. Among ancient people, such treatment was a means of mocking and generating great shame toward the unburied, their families, and all they stood for. The time reference, three and one-half days, seems symbolically fitting, for this is the period when evil rules unchecked. However, revelries will be suddenly cut short; celebrations turn to shock. In an instant, before the carousing multitudes, the spirit of life will flow into the bodies of the witnesses, and they will rise into heaven.63 The result will be consternation such as has seldom been seen on this planet. Analysis and Summary

Murder is the greatest and last resort of the wicked and the base of their power. But what happens when the murdered do not stay dead? Instead, this chapter ends by focusing on the praise and glory given to God as he begins to publically reign and personally destroy the remaining wicked. A crescendo of disaster, greater than that yet shown, is about to usher in the great and terrible day of the Lord. But that must wait while God rounds out the picture with additional information. So God breaks the narrative just at the point when Christ moves to center stage. With chapter 12, God starts anew, and this time he uses a different point of view to disclose additional details to help the reader understand why the world will end in fire. To do so, he more clearly portrays the foundation, development, depth, breadth, and final end of earthly evil.

Here at the end, when evil will at last die, the two witnesses play their part. They are Godâs agents who answer the prayer of the martyrs, âWhen will you avenge our testimonies?â The two prophets bear witness by word and deed, but their work serves only to harden an intractable and impenitent enemy. The rejection of that witness, expressed most forcefully and irrevocably with the prophetsâ martyrdom, initiates the final judgment against the inhabitants of the earth so clearly described in chapters 9, 16, and 19.

Here in chapter 11, John reveals the agents by whose acts and authority the trumpet plagues he has so vividly described are actually unleashed. Revelation shows us that the pattern God has used in the pastâusing human agents to bring about his objectivesâwill continue in the future.

Two excellent examples of how God uses human agents come from Old Testament times. The first is the flood story, where âNoah found grace in the eyes of the Lord; for Noah was a just man, and perfect in his generationâ (Moses 8:27). At that time, âthe earth was corrupt before God, and filled with violenceâ (Moses 8:28). These wicked inhabitants of the earth not only refused to yield to Noahâs cries for repentance and his threats of a coming disaster, but also plotted his death (Moses 8:24, 26). In response, Noah turned to God, who responded, âI will destroy man whom I have created, from the face of the earth, both man and beast, and creeping things, and the fowls of the air; for it repenteth Noah that I have created them, and that I have made them; and he hath called upon me for they have sought his lifeâ (Moses 8:26). Noah, Godâs prophet on earth, acted as his agent and initiated the flood against that hardened and willfully wicked generation. The second example is the Exodus story. Moses, acting as Godâs agent, came with power and warning. Pharaohâs rejection of Mosesâ testimony brought plague after plague. In every instance, Moses responded to the will of God and acted as his agent (see Ex. 12â14).97

Admittedly, in Revelation, the angels of God are the explicit executors of the judgments, but they are responding to the prayers of the Saints (8:4). It would seem, at this point, that the two witnesses are acting as the executors and answering, at least in part, the Saintsâ plea. Thus, the angels respond to the directions and authority of these two prophets, and in this way the pattern of a chain of agents continues. Their task is to give the willfully wicked a chance to repent, to turn back, to cease from their designs of destroying the earth and Godâs people. That witness will include signs such that only the most deliberately self-blinded can reject. Refusing this last chance to reform, even resorting to murder, they make their destruction sure.

Some may be concerned because the day of the Lord is filled with such horror, but it has its purpose. Nothing unclean can enter into the Lordâs presence and survive.98 The glory of God is about to sweep the earth so that the Holy Cities can be established with their paradisiacal gardens and temples. Therefore, evil must come to an end.

It is of note that because of the great destructions, by the time the Lord comes, there will be very little evil left to put to an end. Throughout Revelation we have seen âthe self-destructive nature of evil. But God cannot allow this to act as an impersonal nemesis, a self-operating moral principleâ sweeping away all in its path. To do so would allow the powers of evil to carry all the inhabitants of the earth down with them to utter ruin. God would be left with a hollow, Pyrrhic victory, resembling defeat far more than victory. Since Godâs victory must also be manâs victory, it must be won through righteous human agents exercising faith in God. Evil must be allowed to combine its forces against the Saviorâs people and then fall back in utter defeat through the Saintsâ faith, trust, and loyalty.99

The hard battle between the forces of good and evil is what the second woe is all about. As the third comes, the Saints ride with their Christ on the clouds and move against the remnant of evil in total faith toward victory. Those whose souls have lain beneath the altar find that they are not only vindicated but also that they are the vindicators. As a scripture states, âthe day cometh that shall burn as an oven, and all the proud, yea, and all that do wickedly shall burn as stubble; for they that come shall burn them, saith the Lord of Hostsâ (JSâH 1:37).100

An important lesson taught clearly in chapter 11 is that proximity is not good enough. Broadening but not breaking the symbolism God used, those in the outer court of the temple were not bad people. Rather they were friends, semi-active members, and others who preferred the comfort of the doorstep to that of the front room. Alas, they will find that lack of antagonism and even friendliness will not do in troubled times. Safety will be found in only full commitment. It is covenant making and covenant keeping that puts the seal of safety on the heads of the faithful.
