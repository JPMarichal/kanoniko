Revelation continues that story through the end of time. It shows the active involvement of the Lord in the ongoing progress and, unfortunately, regress of his Church and its people. Thus, Revelation was not just for John and the Saints of the seven churches but for other Saints then and now. Analysis and Summary

Verses 1â3 act as a title and testimony of the work. It is at 1:4 that the actual letter begins with the salutation or greeting. Though the Seer was writing a letter of comfort and warning to the surviving remnants of the Church in his day, at least in Asia Minor, his use of the number seven suggests the message was meant to go much further not only geographically but also temporally.66 If that is the case, then the seven churches become symbols of the Church of God anytime, anywhere, and the message is applicable wherever Saints face similar conditions. Further, since the number seven was tied to both the cosmic and the divine, its use points to God as the source of the message and calls the reader to have trust and faith in his word as recorded by John.

Verse 1:6 focuses on the end result of the Resurrection and supremacy of the Lord: he is able to make his followers kings and priests unto God.67 The seven servants mentioned twice in Revelation (JST 1:4, 20) had apparently achieved these ranks and attendant blessings. Part of the problem comes from a misunderstanding of the term. The Greek word á¾Î´Î·Ï (hadÄs) denoted the abode of departed spirits. During the Homeric period, Hades was viewed as a dreary, foreboding place. Over time, however, people began to question this view. Many, especially the Pharisees, saw it as a temporary abode where souls awaited final judgment and resurrection. The idea that both good and bad people would inherit the same kind of afterlife, however, seemed to conflict with the justice of God. By New Testament times, some Jews believed Hades was composed of four chambers: one for holy martyrs, one for the righteous, one for the wicked who did not receive their just due in life, and one for the willfully evil who had suffered violent deaths.102 Over time, more and more came to view Hades as only a temporary holding place, unlike Gehenna that was eternal in nature.103

The region was viewed as having gates that held the inhabitants in.104 Only keys could open those gates. Keys give access or control and, thus, symbolize authority. Analysis and Summary

Chapter 1 closes with a reiteration of the commandment for John to write and identifies the immediate audience for whom he was to do so. The Lordâs final words, however, expand Johnâs assignment and give him the outline of the remainder of the book. He must write âthe things you have seen, both which are and which are going to happen afterwardsâ (1:19). The things âwhich areâ deal with the condition of the seven churches that act as the impetus of the warning John is to write (chapters 2â3). The things that âare going to happenâ comprise most of the rest of the book and bring its message from the former to the latter days.

Conclusion

The central message in chapter 1 hinges on the authenticity of Johnâs call and underpins the entire book of Revelation. It is summarized in the shared title of both the Father and the Son, namely, âthe Almighty.â The Lectures on Faith, a series of lectures published under the direction of Joseph Smith in 1834, explain the inferences of the word. There it states that God is âover all, from everlasting to everlasting, the creator and upholder of all things.â These words give an accurate description of one who is almighty. The lecture goes on to say that when people believe that God has this power, they can âput their trust in him, so that in this respect their faith can be unwavering.â108 The lecture explains that it is necessary for a person to believe in God and in his power, âfor if, in the first instance, he [the Saint] did not believe him to be God, that is, the creator and upholder of all things, he could not center his faith in him for life and salvation, for fear there should be a greater than he who would thwart all his plans, and he, like the gods of the heathen, would be unable to fulfill his promises.â109 It is the need to trust and the assurance that God will not let the Saint down in that trust that is at the heart of what John wanted his readers to understand.

One scholar noted, when addressing the importance of this belief,

These attributes are faithfully and steadfastly expressed in his personal dealings and relations with us as father, creator, savior, exemplar, and friend. Revelation teaches that the ultimate power is God, not something independent of him and, therefore, morally based. Further, that power stands as the seat of his almightiness.

One way the Savior used this power was to make those who followed him kings, queens, priests, and priestesses; in short, he bestowed upon them, in Mormon vernacular, the fullness of the priesthood. Receiving this, the couples made their calling and election sure and were thus sealed into eternal life. His use of this sealing power showed how immediate and intimate he was to these people. Their reward did not have to wait for the far future. They received the assurance of salvation right then and there because his power was operating then and there.

So far as the message in Revelation is concerned, the importance of these Godsâ almightiness is this: The Father and Son are not simply active in history; they are the driving force behind it. Admittedly, human agency also shapes history. But because the Gods are almighty and therefore create the stage, set in order the cast of characters, orchestrate the events, and determine all limits, neither human nor satanic agency can overrule their predesigned plans (see D&C 3:1â3). They really are the Almighty. Therefore, the Saints can put their full faith in them, a message that the Saints in Johnâs day desperately needed to hear.

The chapter as a whole celebrates another aspect of the almightiness of the exalted Christâhis victory over death and hell. More importantly, it showcases his power, especially that expressed in his fully won victory over all his enemies. It concludes with images emphasizing his closeness to his Church and its leaders. God gave this revelation during the darkest period of his churchâs history. Introduction

Part of the problem comes from a misunderstanding of the term. The Greek word á¾Î´Î·Ï (hadÄs) denoted the abode of departed spirits.
