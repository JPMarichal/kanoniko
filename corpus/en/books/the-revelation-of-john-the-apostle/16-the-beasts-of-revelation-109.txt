Analysis and Summary

In chapter 11, John introduced âthe beast.â In chapter 13, he reveals its genesis, nature, and objectives. It is the minion of the dragon who came out of the sea to do his will. Its work will be more fully explored in chapter 17, where it becomes the steed upon which the whore called âBabylonâ rides. Some of the discussion and development, therefore, will have to wait until that chapter. Nonetheless, much can be stated here.

Through the power of symbols, God very clearly describes the nature and disposition of this archenemy. Its home is the sea.40 Already, we have seen that the sea can represent chaos and malevolence. Often in the Old Testament, it portrays unsettled political and social conditions, the kind of stormy circumstances out of which revolution and tyranny arise.41 The Revelator understands that the oceans really symbolize that which lies beneath and feeds these forces: the inhabitants of the world. It is important to note that it is these who initially spawn the beast and continue to give it wholehearted support.

It stands as an imitation of the Savior, the Lamb of God. The imitation is very apparent. As the Lamb receives power and authority from God, symbolized by receiving the scroll (5:7, 9), so too does the beast receive power from the dragon (13:2); as the Lamb rules over tribes and nations (5:9), so too does the beast (13:7); as the cherubim give honor to the Lamb (5:12), so too do the inhabitants of the earth to the beast (13:4); finally, as the Lamb bears the marks of slaughter (5:6), so, too, does the beast (13:3).

The symbolism of a sea monster reveals much about the historical reality. As noted above, Joseph Smith stated, âThe beasts are spoken of to represent the kingdoms of the world, the inhabitants whereof were beastly and abominable characters; they were murderers, corrupt, carnivorous, and brutal in their dispositions.â42 Thus it is those systems, institutions, and governments that stand in opposition to the kingdom of God. It is important to understand that neither a single institution nor a specific collection of them is meant. To try to identify the beast (or any of the figures called âheads,â âcrowns,â or, in later chapters, âkingsâ) with specific entities, powers, or persons does injustice to the symbol since it portrays all the transhistorical entities that oppose the Lord and his people across time and culture. The seven heads of the beast, then, represent the whole of those people and institutions that make up the great and abominable church of the devil. The adversary mimics wholeness or completeness as a means of deceiving even the elect. His kingdom arose during the late first and second centuries AD but has continued forward from that time. It includes all those immoral entities across time and space right up to the Second Coming that oppose and often fight against God, the Saints, and righteousness.

At times these immoral entities have had more power and been more influential than at others. Certainly the Roman Empire, with its pervasive emperor cult and willful persecution of the Saints during the first few centuries of the meridian era, marks a high point in the power of the beast. Over the centuries, however, other nations, institutions, and governments have taken its place in promoting the agenda of the dragon and making life difficult for Godâs people, some of them, ironically, even bearing the standard of Christ.

Since the symbol, however, connotes those forces opposed to God and righteousness, it is possible that an institution could actually move in and out of the great and abominable church of the devil. Certainly there have been religious systems that have, in the past, lost their spiritual moorings and have amassed and abused great political power. Some of these, however, have since been forced back to their ecclesiastic roots and found their spiritual souls once more.

It is important to note, moreover, that all the forces that make up the beast have a commonality. These organizations were and are grounded on a common philosophy and general ideology, seek specific objectives, and use a similar methodology. All possess an air of superiority anchored in pride, ambition, vanity, and the acquisition of wealth and power. For many of these people, money alone is not the main objective. It is, however, as many believe, a convenient way of keeping score. What they want is power, and the fuel that drives their collective engines is derived neither from mortal ego nor human spirit but from the dragon, that is, Lucifer in all his power.

This beast has seven heads and ten horns, each crowned with a diadem. At first glance the image is that of the dragon, but there is an important difference. The old serpent had but seven diadems that he wore upon his heads. The beast, on the other hand, has ten crowns, each placed upon a horn, suggesting that the entities the crowns represent rest their power on brute force.44 The beast, however, does not overshadow the dragon in either power or rule. The number of crowns reveals that the beast actually has less power than the dragon, seven representing a whole while ten represents only a portion.45 Thus, the beast comes under the authority of the dragon, drawing its power from him. Even so, the likenesses cannot be overlooked. This beast of prey is thoroughly diabolic: he possesses the power, authority, and very image of the devil,46 and his objective is to devour humans spiritually.

A curious aspect of the beast, from which has risen no end of speculation, is that one of its heads was mortally wounded yet the beast survived (13:3).47 This wound unto death mirrors that of the Lamb in chapter 5. Comparing the beast and the Lamb gives insight into what God meant to convey by use of the image. There are striking parallels between the two: (1) both have swords; (2) both have horns; (3) their followers carry their name on their foreheads; (4) they have authority, given time, over nations, kindreds, tongues, and peoples; and (5) both are universally worshipped. In sum, as the dragon is the antithesis of God, the beast is the converse image of Christ. Satan bestows on the beast his throne, power, and great authority. The Savior shares these same privileges with the Father. The beast receives authority over every nation, tongue, tribe, and people (13:7); the Lamb holds this same domain. Excursus on the Anti-Christ

Many works on Revelation use the term âanti-Christâ as a synonym for the beast. Such a designation really does not work. Admittedly, from the early Christian period on, some Christians felt that an evil individual would arise in the last days who would oppose God.58 The idea is based on the logical assumption that the antichrist is the exact counterpoint to the Lord himself. The problem is that there is no mention of an antichrist anywhere in Revelation. The term shows up only in 1 John 2:18â22; 4:3; and 2 John 1:7, and in each instance it identifies certain heretics living in Johnâs day who taught false doctrines about Jesus, most notably those who ascribed to the docetist position (defined below).59 In Revelation, as has been shown, the beast is not a person but rather transhistorical entities that oppress the righteous.

The menacing nature of the term, however, has made it catch on. A number of people, over the ages, have kept alive the idea of eschatological opponents of Christ.60 On this subject, two LDS scholars have noted that

the Greek preposition anti, roughly translated, means instead of. It also carries the meaning of âface to faceâ or mirror image. Today, many believe in the anti-Christ, the definite article being important, for it points to a definite person. However, as noted, these people have taken the idea out of context and transformed and imposed it on the last days. It is not scriptural.

There is another view that fits better with Revelation and which has been promulgated from the earliest times. This is that antichrist (note the absence of the definite article) is an evil power or spiritual influence that permeates the last days, inspiring those who succumb to its spell to preach false doctrines and do the work of devils.62 Indeed, John warned the Saints that âevery spirit that confesseth not that Jesus Christ is come in the flesh is not of God: and this is that of antichristâ (1 Jn. 4:3).63 Thus, the spirit of antichrist is that which teaches false doctrines about Christ. It is best to understand âantichrist,â then, as the malevolent, evil intellectual climate that permeates the last days and is promulgated by the dragon and his minions.

The technical name for the activity is gematria (×ÖµÖ¼××Ö·××¨Ö´×Ö¸×), which describes a system of assigning a numerical value to a word or phrase. Most ancient people did not have a separate numbering system and alphabet, so letters also served as numbers. The game falls into two types. The first uses the system for exegetical purposes to draw out hidden meanings in religious and secular texts. Conclusion

Through the two visions in this chapter, John reveals the three major enemies that will fight against God from the days of Christ to his Second Coming. The central issue is worship, and the tension of the work turns on who will accept or reject the mark of the beast. The battle is for the souls of men and, therefore, the mark, expressed as the number 666, reveals the object of worship of those who wear it: the beast.

The large number of possibilities along with all the conflicting solutions argues against the number pointing to a particular individual or institution. Further, it would be a singular exception to the pattern set in Revelation for a number to point to something so immediate, confined, and finite as just one entity. Analysis and Summary

In chapter 11, John introduced âthe beast.â In chapter 13, he reveals its genesis, nature, and objectives. It is the minion of the dragon who came out of the sea to do his will. Its work will be more fully explored in chapter 17, where it becomes the steed upon which the whore called âBabylonâ rides. Some of the discussion and development, therefore, will have to wait until that chapter. Nonetheless, much can be stated here.

Through the power of symbols, God very clearly describes the nature and disposition of this archenemy. Its home is the sea.40 Already, we have seen that the sea can represent chaos and malevolence. Often in the Old Testament, it portrays unsettled political and social conditions, the kind of stormy circumstances out of which revolution and tyranny arise.41 The Revelator understands that the oceans really symbolize that which lies beneath and feeds these forces: the inhabitants of the world. It is important to note that it is these who initially spawn the beast and continue to give it wholehearted support.

It stands as an imitation of the Savior, the Lamb of God. The imitation is very apparent. As the Lamb receives power and authority from God, symbolized by receiving the scroll (5:7, 9), so too does the beast receive power from the dragon (13:2); as the Lamb rules over tribes and nations (5:9), so too does the beast (13:7); as the cherubim give honor to the Lamb (5:12), so too do the inhabitants of the earth to the beast (13:4); finally, as the Lamb bears the marks of slaughter (5:6), so, too, does the beast (13:3).

The symbolism of a sea monster reveals much about the historical reality. As noted above, Joseph Smith stated, âThe beasts are spoken of to represent the kingdoms of the world, the inhabitants whereof were beastly and abominable characters; they were murderers, corrupt, carnivorous, and brutal in their dispositions.â42 Thus it is those systems, institutions, and governments that stand in opposition to the kingdom of God. It is important to understand that neither a single institution nor a specific collection of them is meant. To try to identify the beast (or any of the figures called âheads,â âcrowns,â or, in later chapters, âkingsâ) with specific entities, powers, or persons does injustice to the symbol since it portrays all the transhistorical entities that oppose the Lord and his people across time and culture. It is important to note, as Elder McConkie states, that the symbol shows âtheir dealings toward the saints and the cause of righteousness.â Further, âthe beast does not represent all the world, but only selected âkingdoms.ââ43 The number seven, as noted before, represents that which is whole or complete. The seven heads of the beast, then, represent the whole of those people and institutions that make up the great and abominable church of the devil. The adversary mimics wholeness or completeness as a means of deceiving even the elect. His kingdom arose during the late first and second centuries AD but has continued forward from that time. It includes all those immoral entities across time and space right up to the Second Coming that oppose and often fight against God, the Saints, and righteousness.

At times these immoral entities have had more power and been more influential than at others. Certainly the Roman Empire, with its pervasive emperor cult and willful persecution of the Saints during the first few centuries of the meridian era, marks a high point in the power of the beast. Over the centuries, however, other nations, institutions, and governments have taken its place in promoting the agenda of the dragon and making life difficult for Godâs people, some of them, ironically, even bearing the standard of Christ.

Since the symbol, however, connotes those forces opposed to God and righteousness, it is possible that an institution could actually move in and out of the great and abominable church of the devil. Certainly there have been religious systems that have, in the past, lost their spiritual moorings and have amassed and abused great political power. Some of these, however, have since been forced back to their ecclesiastic roots and found their spiritual souls once more.

It is important to note, moreover, that all the forces that make up the beast have a commonality. These organizations were and are grounded on a common philosophy and general ideology, seek specific objectives, and use a similar methodology. All possess an air of superiority anchored in pride, ambition, vanity, and the acquisition of wealth and power. For many of these people, money alone is not the main objective. It is, however, as many believe, a convenient way of keeping score. What they want is power, and the fuel that drives their collective engines is derived neither from mortal ego nor human spirit but from the dragon, that is, Lucifer in all his power.

This beast has seven heads and ten horns, each crowned with a diadem. At first glance the image is that of the dragon, but there is an important difference. The old serpent had but seven diadems that he wore upon his heads. The beast, on the other hand, has ten crowns, each placed upon a horn, suggesting that the entities the crowns represent rest their power on brute force.44 The beast, however, does not overshadow the dragon in either power or rule. The number of crowns reveals that the beast actually has less power than the dragon, seven representing a whole while ten represents only a portion.45 Thus, the beast comes under the authority of the dragon, drawing its power from him. Even so, the likenesses cannot be overlooked. This beast of prey is thoroughly diabolic: he possesses the power, authority, and very image of the devil,46 and his objective is to devour humans spiritually.

A curious aspect of the beast, from which has risen no end of speculation, is that one of its heads was mortally wounded yet the beast survived (13:3).47 This wound unto death mirrors that of the Lamb in chapter 5. Comparing the beast and the Lamb gives insight into what God meant to convey by use of the image. There are striking parallels between the two: (1) both have swords; (2) both have horns; (3) their followers carry their name on their foreheads; (4) they have authority, given time, over nations, kindreds, tongues, and peoples; and (5) both are universally worshipped. In sum, as the dragon is the antithesis of God, the beast is the converse image of Christ. Satan bestows on the beast his throne, power, and great authority. The Savior shares these same privileges with the Father. The beast receives authority over every nation, tongue, tribe, and people (13:7); the Lamb holds this same domain. In 17:8, John states that the beast âwas and [now] is not and, and will come.â (our Rendition). This mimics the description of the Savior in 1:4 as he âwhich is, and which was, and which is to come.â The Lamb is Lord of lords and King of kings, and the beast seeks the same positions. In short, the beast is the spiritual antitheses of Christ and his kingdom. Thus, the beast not only wears the name of blasphemy upon its heads, but it also embodies the name by counterfeiting the attributes of Deity.48

It is here that we once again see the value of using symbols to reveal truths. Though the beast represents real institutions and powers, it also parodies the Savior himself, thereby exposing his enemiesâ purpose and appeal. It is clear that Revelation identifies the beast as the grand nemesis, the chief opponent, and the counterfeit savior operating across time against the Lord.

Therefore, as noted above, it is unwise to identify the beast with one individual or one institution. Just as the Lambâs work spans the ages, so too does that of the dragon. Just as the Lord sets up his institutions to carry out his work, so does Satan. The beast represents all of these as they worked through the ages and more particularly in the last days to fulfill the dragonâs objectives.49 Thus, it would appear best to link the beastâs resuscitation to the continual rising of oppressive states and institutions used by the devil in spite of the fact he knows full well he has already been defeated; the death blow to his kingdom is the Resurrection of the Lord.50

Let us point out that the dragon, being immortal, cannot actually die; he can only be incarcerated. His institutions, however, can end. The old dragon had been cast out of heaven but came to the earth. With the crucifixion of the Lord, the devil actually began to lose his domain (see Matt. 12:22â29; John 12:31â33; with Rev. 1:5; 5:9). The process would take millennia and, in the end, an act of God. For the time being, the devil has been given time. Even so, some of his institutions have failed already.

Excursus on the Anti-Christ

Many works on Revelation use the term âanti-Christâ as a synonym for the beast. Such a designation really does not work. Admittedly, from the early Christian period on, some Christians felt that an evil individual would arise in the last days who would oppose God.58 The idea is based on the logical assumption that the antichrist is the exact counterpoint to the Lord himself. The problem is that there is no mention of an antichrist anywhere in Revelation. The term shows up only in 1 John 2:18â22; 4:3; and 2 John 1:7, and in each instance it identifies certain heretics living in Johnâs day who taught false doctrines about Jesus, most notably those who ascribed to the docetist position (defined below).59 In Revelation, as has been shown, the beast is not a person but rather transhistorical entities that oppress the righteous.

The menacing nature of the term, however, has made it catch on. A number of people, over the ages, have kept alive the idea of eschatological opponents of Christ.60 On this subject, two LDS scholars have noted that

the Greek preposition anti, roughly translated, means instead of. It also carries the meaning of âface to faceâ or mirror image. The image in the mirror, looking back at you, is face to face with you. It looks like you. Yet it has no substance. It is a counterfeit of you, in a sense. It only appears to be you. Through the centuries thereafter, the term antichrist became Anti-Christ, and by Joseph Smithâs day it referred to those who opposed Christ.61

Today, many believe in the anti-Christ, the definite article being important, for it points to a definite person. However, as noted, these people have taken the idea out of context and transformed and imposed it on the last days. It is not scriptural.

There is another view that fits better with Revelation and which has been promulgated from the earliest times. This is that antichrist (note the absence of the definite article) is an evil power or spiritual influence that permeates the last days, inspiring those who succumb to its spell to preach false doctrines and do the work of devils.62 Indeed, John warned the Saints that âevery spirit that confesseth not that Jesus Christ is come in the flesh is not of God: and this is that of antichristâ (1 Jn. 4:3).63 Thus, the spirit of antichrist is that which teaches false doctrines about Christ. Still, it never manifests itself as the hypothesized âanti-Christ,â the eschatological figure with near superhuman powers that plays such a big part in some reconstructions of the events of Revelation. It is best to understand âantichrist,â then, as the malevolent, evil intellectual climate that permeates the last days and is promulgated by the dragon and his minions.

Scholars estimate that 90% of Roman coins were minted to pay the Roman army and navy and everyone else scrambled to provide them services so they could get a hold of these gold, silver and bronze coins with the markings of the government. Many carried coinage in their right hands, the culturally correct hand for the Romans, (the mark on the hand?) and had coinage always on their mind (the mark on the forehead?).

Greco-Roman coins were marked with the image of the city-state, governing leader, or emperor often coupled with religious symbols to stress their piety or in some cases, divinity. Conclusion

Through the two visions in this chapter, John reveals the three major enemies that will fight against God from the days of Christ to his Second Coming. The central issue is worship, and the tension of the work turns on who will accept or reject the mark of the beast. The battle is for the souls of men and, therefore, the mark, expressed as the number 666, reveals the object of worship of those who wear it: the beast.

The large number of possibilities along with all the conflicting solutions argues against the number pointing to a particular individual or institution. Further, it would be a singular exception to the pattern set in Revelation for a number to point to something so immediate, confined, and finite as just one entity.
