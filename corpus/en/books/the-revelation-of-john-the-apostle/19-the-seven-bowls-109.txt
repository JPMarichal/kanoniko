The Voice from the Temple

The vision begun in chapter 15 forms the backdrop for that of 16. John has already heard the victorious Saints sing their song of triumph (15:2â4). In this way, the Seer assures his readers of their own eventual victory, and that they have nothing to fear from the judgments that John will now disclose to them. Indeed, as he lets his readers know, God is in his temple and directing all that will happen. In fact, the Saints have nothing to fear precisely because God is controlling all that is going on. Even the plagues they will now hear about are under his control. Thus, the assurance found in chapter 15 steels his people against the doom that will grip the ungodly as the vision continues.

Chapter 14 closed with the picture of wrath contained in the symbolism of the harvest of the vintage. Why does chapter 16 go through the whole thing again? It is likely to emphasize both the intensity and the finality of the coming judgment.1 This suggests why the Lord chose symbols different from those he used in chapter 14 and also in 8 and 9 to depict the same judgments. Analysis and Summary

As 16:12 shows, the sixth angel prepares the world for war by removing its impediments. As the angel pours out his bowl, the effect excites into greater motion the three great world antagonists: the dragon, the beast, and the false prophet. In nonsymbolic terms, this is the moment when Satan and those earthly institutions under his control, directed by the philosophy that drives them, begin to make their last move. He can do this because God has lifted all restraining influences. In symbolic terms, the Euphrates River dries up.60

The nature of the symbols used in this chapter, as in others, suggests they reflect actual, if not specific, conditions. Bizarre weather patterns, scorching heat, large rivers drying up, famine, and plague reveal nature itself working with God in his recompense upon rampant wickedness.61

Analysis and Summary

With the seventh bowl, a voice resounds from the throne in the temple, declaring, âIt is done!â The combination of the throne and temple suggests the absolute authority by which God accomplishes his will: He invokes both kingly and priestly authority. With the pouring out of the seventh bowl plague, God finally and fully accomplishes his designs. As John said in 11:18, the time has come to destroy those who would destroy the earth. The vision here, then, is the destruction of all the corrupt worldly systems that results from the battle called Armageddon.

In vivid language, John describes the power God unleashes against the wicked: thunder, lightning, tumult, and finally an earthquake of unprecedented proportions (compare Dan. 12:1). âFor, with you saith the Lord Almighty, I will rend their kingdoms; I will not only shake the earth, but the starry heavens shall trembleâ (D&C 84:118). In reality, all this is nothing less than the preparation of the earth for its paradisiacal state.

And he shall utter his voice out of Zion, and he shall speak from Jerusalem, and his voice shall be heard among all people; and it shall be a voice as the voice of many waters, and as the voice of a great thunder, which shall break down the mountains, and the valleys shall not be found. He shall command the great deep, and it shall be driven back into the north countries, and the islands shall become one land; and the land of Jerusalem and the land of Zion shall be turned back into their own place, and the earth shall be like as it was in the days before it was divided. And the Lord, even the Savior, shall stand in the midst of his people, and shall reign over all flesh. (D&C 133:21â25)

The earthquake causes the fall of the cities of the nations: âAnd the great city was divided into three partsâ (16:19). The focus appears to be on Babylon, divided into thirds, symbolic of the fact she has come under divine wrath that strikes her to her destruction. Men have long stood in fear of the power of the earthquake. Here this power, in the service of God, causes the world to rock such that the entire civilization of the dragon falls at the blow. To use another symbol, Babylon herself comes down, her division becoming a mortal wound.

Associated with the seventh bowl, John sees one last plague of tremendous proportions. This one is not directed at the cities and nations under the power of spiritual Babylon as was the earthquake. The new threat is directed at the people who inhabit those cities. âAnd there fell upon men a great hail out of heaven, every stone about the weight of a talentâ (16:21). From the days when Moses brought this plague upon Egypt, hail has served as both the symbol and the reality of Godâs judgment upon hardened humankind.86 Here the weight of each stone is about 90 pounds.87

Through this symbol, John describes the pounding power that will bring all men to their knees. But, sadly, it will not bring penitence. For most, that will take the fury of hellfire and may be as long as a thousand years; for the sons of perdition, it will never come.

Once again John shows how hardened these people have become. They can only curse God and wish to die. Even in their agony, however, they use the lie embedded in their blasphemy in an attempt to bring sympathy to themselves and to coax others to their cause. They have created a spiritual darkness so deep that now no light can get through. As a result, they are beyond even the thought of repentance. Little wonder the angels declared that Godâs judgments against these callous, bitter, and obdurate souls are entirely proper and just.

Conclusion

After being brought three times through the period of judgment, we finally come to the end with the declaration of God, âIt is finished!â With those words, all judgment against the world suddenly ceases. The earth dwellers refused to respond to the cry of repentance and have now faced the consequences. Chapter 16 describes those consequences. But how did conditions get so bad that the Lord had to act in such a harsh manner? Indeed, what undergirded the conditions that led to such horrible slaughter? Finally, why was it that the earth dwellers, in spite of all the horror, were unable to repent?

The Voice from the Temple

The vision begun in chapter 15 forms the backdrop for that of 16. John has already heard the victorious Saints sing their song of triumph (15:2â4). Chapter 14 closed with the picture of wrath contained in the symbolism of the harvest of the vintage. Why does chapter 16 go through the whole thing again? Analysis and Summary

As 16:12 shows, the sixth angel prepares the world for war by removing its impediments. As the angel pours out his bowl, the effect excites into greater motion the three great world antagonists: the dragon, the beast, and the false prophet. The nature of the symbols used in this chapter, as in others, suggests they reflect actual, if not specific, conditions. Bizarre weather patterns, scorching heat, large rivers drying up, famine, and plague reveal nature itself working with God in his recompense upon rampant wickedness.61

Analysis and Summary

With the seventh bowl, a voice resounds from the throne in the temple, declaring, âIt is done!â The combination of the throne and temple suggests the absolute authority by which God accomplishes his will: He invokes both kingly and priestly authority. With the pouring out of the seventh bowl plague, God finally and fully accomplishes his designs. As John said in 11:18, the time has come to destroy those who would destroy the earth. The vision here, then, is the destruction of all the corrupt worldly systems that results from the battle called Armageddon.

In vivid language, John describes the power God unleashes against the wicked: thunder, lightning, tumult, and finally an earthquake of unprecedented proportions (compare Dan. 12:1). âFor, with you saith the Lord Almighty, I will rend their kingdoms; I will not only shake the earth, but the starry heavens shall trembleâ (D&C 84:118).
