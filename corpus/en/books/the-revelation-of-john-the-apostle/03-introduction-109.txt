How did such a strange, even bizarre book even make its way into the Bible? Frankly, by the skin of its teeth. Authorship

Though some Christians in the third century did not accept the work, it is easy to see why first- and second-century Saints liked it. First, it sounded very much like the Jesus they knew. It was the leaders a century later who felt the contrast between the Jesus in Johnâs Gospel and that in Revelation was too great to have been written by one man. Their problem was, however, that they did not look deeply enough. The theme in both works is one of salvation, but the means is different. During his first coming, the Lordâs objective was to set in motion the salvation of individuals through setting up his Church and spreading the gospel message. At his last coming, his purpose is to save the earth and the righteous by destroying those who would destroy them (see 11:18).

The point is that the earliest Saints did not see a marked contrast between the Jesus of Revelation and he of the Gospels. To the multitudes, he spoke in parables to hide his message from those who would accept neither it nor him, but not so his disciples. Though these mysteries were carefully guarded, later generations knew that they had existed. As late as the fourth century, a number of Christian leaders confirmed that some important material had never made it into the Gospel accounts. The bishop of Caesarea, Basil the Great, assured his readers that important material had deliberately been left out of the New Testament record but was preserved secretly in the traditions of the Apostles.9 Irenaeus, bishop in Lyon, writing in the late second century, gave a peek into what some of these teachings were. In his work The Demonstration of the Gospel, which he said he wrote so that people could âhold to the faith without deviation,â10 he included information dealing with âthe seven heavens, the powers and the archangels, the relationship of the cherubim and seraphim to the Word and Wisdom of God and the role of the seven-fold Spirit.â11 All this echoes material found in Revelation. The second reason the earliest Christians accepted the work was because it showed Jesus in a new light, thus rounding out their understanding of him found in the Gospels and other writings. Indeed, in Revelation, they found neither the suffering servant of Mark nor the new Moses of Matthew. In this work, they found the divine and glorified Christ working out Godâs purposes for the salvation of his people in the last days and then ushering in the grand millennial era when all would know God. Those of Johnâs readership who had converted from paganism and now suffered for their decision reached to their Lord for strength and vindication. So when did doubt arise concerning the authenticity of the work and of Johnâs authorship? Johnâs work has come forth as a testament of the foreknowledge of God, giving many hope and assurance through nearly two millennia. Dating and Interpretive Approaches

As with the other works in the New Testament, dating the text is important as a means of establishing authenticity and context, the latter greatly helping with understanding the purpose and message of a work. With Revelation, dating the piece has been greatly influenced by the various authorsâ interpretive approaches. Since the two go hand in glove, we will consider both together. The treatment here is brief since the Restoration greatly helps with this issue, so belaboring all the points of view seems superfluous. Nonetheless, a review is necessary in order to place the LDS position in its niche.

A number of scholars have placed the creation of the basic material in Revelation a number of years before the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70. The foremost of these is Margaret Barker, who argues that much of the material came from items written and gathered during the years of the tenth Jubilee, which began about AD 16 and ended about AD 66. That period was filled with great struggles for the early Church. The Christians looked to older prophecies, many in the Old Testament, as well as some generated in their own time, to help put their period in perspective and understand their current situation and mission. These prophecies they interpreted as describing an imminent coming of the Lord for which the Saints were to prepare. Barker concludes that much of the material and the central message of the major pieces that later made up the work looked exclusively to the beliefs of the Church during the first few decades after the Saviorâs death, but she adds that John the Beloved later developed a new interpretation of this material that looked to a future coming of the Lord, which he compiled while on the Island of Patmos.26 Thus, this view sees the creation of the material that would eventually make up the book reaching as far back as Old Testament times to just before the fall of Jerusalem. As attractive as this thesis is, the evidence is not compelling and, therefore, few follow Barkerâs lead.

The Preterist School

Another school, called âthe Preterists,â places the writing of the whole of Revelation just before the fall of Jerusalem. The word means âpast in fulfillment.â The Preterists interpret all biblical prophecy as having been fulfilled shortly after they were delivered. Thus, none look to the distant future.27 So far as Revelation is concerned, these commentators fall into two main camps.

The first interprets Revelation as predicting the fall of Jerusalem to the Romans in AD 70 and, therefore, insist it was written before that date. As an example of their view, they point to 11:1â2, which, they say, shows the temple was still standing when John wrote because, they insist, that imagery would mean nothing if the temple were destroyed. Further, 11:2 and 11:8 refer to âthe holy cityâ and âthe great city,â which, they maintain, can only mean Jerusalem as it stood just before the Roman siege, because after that date it became little more than an army outpost for decades.

As a further example, in 17:9, the work mentions âseven mountains,â which these people take as meaning Rome. The text further states that the mountains also represent seven kings, of whom the sixth was then in power. Some speculate that the first of these âkingsâ was Julius Caesar, making the sixth Nero, the original persecutor of the Church and the ruler when Vespasian began the siege against Jerusalem. Others insist that the first king was Augustus, making the sixth Servius Sulpicius Galba, who reigned briefly after Neroâs suicide and died in AD 69 shortly before Jerusalem fell. He was followed in quick succession by Otho, Vitelius, and finally Vespasian, whom they count as the seventh; he ruled when Jerusalem actually fell, and he ordered the state of Judea be destroyed.

They believe John wrote to spur Christian resistance to Jewish persecution with the promise that the Jewish nation would fall because of its wickedness. All the Christians had to do was hold true to their faith and the apostate Jewish nation, personified as Babylon the Great, would fall and God would vindicate his followers as true Israel. If they held out for only a few years, then Christianity would be firmly in place and Godâs kingdom permanently established on earth. All of this, they feel, was realized with the fall of Jerusalem.

The strength of this view is that it militates against prophecy failed. For millennia, some Christians have felt that the Parousia was imminent, and countless clergy have predicted the date of Christâs Second Coming only to be proved wrong. As a result, over time many Christians have lost faith in the Bible and in the prophecies it contains and left the faith. The Preterist position saves both prophecy and faith by showing that the prophecies were indeed fulfilled, but shortly after they were given. No prophecy, they aver, looked to the far future but were addressed to the specific challenges faced at the time by the people to which they were addressed.

The problems with this view are many. A major one for this camp is shoehorning all the events taking place in Revelation into a few short years. Preterists inadequately explain many of the images in the book because there are no historical parallels to be found, particularly for the great battles that end with the Second Coming of the Savior. They also disagree on other points, such as the one noted above concerning the identity of the seven kings. In short, as with much of their interpretation of the symbols, they have to use excessive force to get things to fit.

The second camp into which the Preterists fall holds that the book looks a bit further out, even to the fall of Rome. The workâs purpose, they feel, is to assure the Christians of the final triumph of Godâs kingdom over that of the unholy Roman Empire and to encourage its readers to resist Romeâs influence. Compromise with this âbeastâ would be deadly because all who did so would go down when it went down. They believe that the prophecies in Revelation were fulfilled in the fifth century when Rome ceased to be a world power. They readily admit that some items in Revelation do not fit historical reality, but that was because, they say, John simply got his facts mixed up.28

The strength of this view is that it does not force all the events recorded in Revelation into a few short years. The view also allows the work to be written after the fall of Jerusalem and at a time when the antagonism between Christianity and Rome was escalating. Further, âBabylon the Greatâ fits much better as Rome than it does as Jerusalem. Even better is the idea that the work was written to encourage the Saints to hold out against the seductions of Rome and the forces of persecution. Nonetheless, there are significant problems. Chief among these is the fact that they sidestep points in Johnâs work that do not fit their thesis. For example, the victory of the people of God and the overthrow of evil as depicted in the last chapters were never realized. Thus, much of the imagery is not handled. Further, because they blame inconsistencies on Johnâs ineptness, they cast suspicion on the authenticity of the work. Another major problem is that, like the other camp, their view of Godâs judgments is simply too narrow. The judgments in the Apocalypse are clearly worldwide and directed not at one nation but many, and not one people but all the âinhabitants of the earthâ who oppose God.

The Historicist School

The next school is that of âthe Historicists.â This school is more amorphous than that of the Preterists, having considerably more branches. Where these come together is that they see the prophecy in Revelation as being fulfilled throughout history up to modern times. The seals, trumpets, woes, bowl plagues, and so on describe various eras of history in a somewhat chronological order, including the fall of Rome to the Visigoths, the invasion of Christian lands by the Muslims, the rise of the Catholic Church, the corrupted medieval papacy, and various European and world wars.

The strength of this view is that it spreads out the images in Revelation across time and geography and therefore allows for a more universal interpretation of the events. Also, its adherents do not have to put the creation of the work before the fall of Jerusalem, and therefore they can look for a period that better matches the conditions John describes. The major problems are three: first, it centers only on Europe and the west, leaving out the rest of the world, including the Middle East; second, the proponents interpret Revelation from their particular point of view in history and cannot agree with one another, because each one limits the images in Revelation to historical events he or she propounds; and finally, their position makes Revelation have no real significance or relevance to Johnâs initial audience, the Christians of the first century.

The next school is that of âthe Futurists.â This one, like âthe Preterists,â can be divided into two main camps, though both see most of the events described in Revelation as happening immediately preceding the Second Coming.29 The most popular form is âdispensational futurism,â which views the images in Revelation as quite literal and chronological such that the direction history will take is clearly spelled out. The strengths of the Futuristsâ positions are many, but the primary one is that it allows for a universal judgment of the earth that fits nicely with the context of Johnâs writings. The Idealist School

Lastly, there is âthe Idealistâ camp. This group holds to the idea that Revelation has a timeless appeal because the message itself is, indeed, timeless.30 The book should not be tied too closely to actual history. Rather, it portrays the struggle between good and evil in all ages and holds out the promise that, in the end, right will indeed prevail. The strength of this position is that it makes the book relevant to any and all ages and, more especially, for those living in times when the righteous go through periods of intense stress and maltreatment. Further, this position gives the book universal appeal for people in all ages, and the fact that it does not promote an imminent Parousia means it bypasses the concerns that created the Preterist camps. Those who propound this view are very good at highlighting the workâs focus on the war between good and evil. But its weaknesses are just opposite that of the Historicists and Preterists in that they leave the images amorphous and lifeless with no historical basis. Thus the message remains vague and the images unnecessarily unaccounted for. Further, it leaves no way to check the view against any kind of standard, therefore making it impossible to test.

Evidence from the Text

So what does Revelation itself tell us concerning the period in which it was written and what eras it covers? Evidence from the book points strongly to the last decade of the first century. First, the book shows that the Christians were under strong pressure to participate in the cult of emperor worship, notably in the province of Asia (see 13:4â8, 15â16; 14:9â11; 15:2; 16:2; 19:20; 20:4). Though the practice of bestowing divine honors on Roman emperors reached all the way back to Julius Caesar (d. 44 BC), the practice took some time to become empire-wide. The eastern empire was predisposed to the idea of mortal rulers having divine qualities because, for millennia, the Egyptians and Mesopotamians had worshiped their leaders as gods. When the Greek Ptolemies conquered Egypt in the third century BC, they took full advantage of that belief to hold on to their position. Their cousins, the Seleucids, did the same in the eastern lands they conquered. Even so, it took time for Romans to feel comfortable with the idea, but when they finally did, the Christians felt Romeâs power.

The earliest documented cases of the Roman government actually moving against Christians come near the beginning of the second century, during the reign of the Emperor Trajan. However, Johnâs letters to the seven Churches show that the persecution had been going on for about two decades before that, pointing to Revelationâs creation not before the fall of Jerusalem as the Preterists hold, but to the time of Domitian. Weighing all the scholarship to date, Sweet has accurately concluded that âthe earlier date [for the writing of Revelation] may be right, but the internal evidence [which the Preterists and others present] is not sufficient to outweigh the firm tradition stemming from Irenaeus,â which puts the workâs creation during the last decade of the first Christian century.31

The Structure of Johnâs Work

The structure of Revelation, as with nearly all of its other aspects, is highly debated. Structure deals with how the various parts relate to the whole. Because Johnâs master work is extremely complex, it does not yield itself to easy structural formulations. One view sees John building his work around distinct visions having seven elements each. The visions are disparate units carrying their own message and do not present a strict chronological sequence.35 Another view argues for a chiastic structure with the central point put somewhere between chapters 11 and 14. The first section (roughly chapters 4â11) deals with human history and the conditions that Christians face in the past, the present, and the near future. The second section (roughly chapters 12â19) deals with eschatology and the situations they will face as this era moves into the end-time.36 Yet another view takes the visions as presenting a chronological sequence with each segment revealing the next stage in world history.37 The last view, for our purposes, takes the later visions as recapitulations of earlier ones but from a different perspective. For example, the bowl plagues in chapters 15â16 recapitulate the trumpet plagues in chapters 8â9.38 Thus, the revelation, as it were, doubles back on itself.

Though all these views have something going for them, the lack of agreement among scholars shows that none are thoroughly convincing.39 This situation exacerbates a difficult interpretive challenge. Do the events associated with the opening of the seven seals, the trumpet pestilences, and the bowl plagues represent apocalyptic descriptions of essentially the same events but from different standpoints (recapitulation theory) or represent a linear unfolding of historical/prophetic events leading through the last days to the final triumph of God and his people (chronological theory)? Evidence from the Restoration suggests that a modified recapitulation and roughly chiastic view is the more likely structure. To cite a few examples, D&C 77:15 identifies two prophets (whose ministry is recorded in Revelation 11) as latter-day ministers to the Jewish nation. Their ministry appears to be part of the last great battle described in Revelation 16 and 19, thus uniting those two events. D&C 77:7 defines the seven seals in Revelation 6 as representing sequential millennial periods that cover the whole of human history. That paradigm suggests that Revelation as a whole focuses primarily on the sixth and seventh seals. D&C 77:12â13 states that the events recorded in chapters 8 and 9 of Revelation are âto be accomplished after the opening of the seventh seal, before the coming of Christ.â That statement ties the events described in those chapters to those recorded in chapters 15â16 and 19, which most scholars agree describe conditions just before and during the Second Coming. The events recorded in Revelation chapters 12 and 13 separate the two pieces of the revelation that report on end-time conditions. These chapters look to proceedings beginning in Johnâs day and set the ground for a better understanding of why the last day events play out as they do. Given the above, it would appear that the format of Revelation does repeat some events but from a different perspective, thus bringing out important lessons that the Lord does not want his people to miss. This commentary points out the various pericopes and shows how they tie together.

Interpretive Methodology

The book of Revelation deserves serious attention. God gave the vision and preserved it for a purpose. He meant his Saints to understand it. It is not, however, a book for the spiritually faint-hearted or intellectually lazy, mainly because one cannot use a straightforward approach in tackling it. The problem was how to get the message, so much of which was designed for those living in the last days, through the editors of the great and abominable church. God knew how.

The Work as an Apocalypse

John did not choose the form of the vision; God did. The form is now called âapocalyptic.â This genre of literature received its name from the first word of the book of Revelation, apocalypsis.41 The word apocalypse comes from the Greek á¼ÏÎ¿ÎºÎ¬Î»ÏÏÎ¹Ï, which means revelation or disclosure. This title is quite fitting since one of the main signatures of apocalyptic literature is that it reveals or discloses heavenly secrets to its readers. Apocalypses were first written by a segment of the Jews, although the Anchor Bible Dictionary suggests that it may be possible that there are traces of apocalypticism in Akkadian literature.42 A quantity of nonbiblical apocalyptic literature begins showing up in the third century BC. The majority of apocalypses were written between the third century BC and the first century AD. In the first century AD, the early Christians identified strongly with the Jewish apocalypses and began to adopt them.

Most apocalypses contain a vision or dream had by the writer of the text. This dream is often narrated by a guide who shows the writer around the celestial realm. These dreams are often eschatological and thus deal with the future and portray latter-day conditions as dualistic. During the current evil age, righteousness will battle evil and win due to divine intervention that will bring in a glorious future age. Both God and Satan have agents that work for them and strive to push their objectives. One characteristic of this literature is its use of symbolism which the reader is expected to interpret. The point of the whole is that God is in control of all that goes on, he has foreseen everything that is to happen, and he has prepared the way of escape for his people.43

The Basic Theological Foundation of Apocalyptic Literature

A basic theological foundation forms the ground which gives apocalyptic literature a unique religious perspective.44 This is composed of three elements. First, it is nearly always eschatological. The word denotes that branch of theology that deals with future events, especially those associated with the Second Coming and the millennial era. In looking at a God-derived future, apocalyptic eschatology and prophetic eschatology share common ground,45 that is, both are predictive. The two, however, have different but not mutually exclusive panoramas. The panoramic view of prophetic eschatology looks at history flowing from the present to the future without interruption. In it, heaven works primarily behind the scenes, albeit through earthly prophets, to bring about Godâs ends. The panoramic view of apocalyptic eschatology looks at a future in which history is not only disrupted but discontinued to bring about a brand new history. In it, heaven does not work behind the scenes but moves inexorably onto center stage, climaxing in the destruction of the wicked, the full revelation of Christ, and the introduction of his millennial reign. Apocalyptic eschatology thereby denies humankind the ability to bring about continual peace and righteousness. Indeed, wickedness and war will increase until God brings it to a sudden and dramatic end. The final consummation therefore comes not through the unbroken flow of history but by its divine discontinuation and bringing back to earth paradisiacal glory (A of F 10).46

Second, apocalyptic literature is dualistic. That is, apocalyptic reality consists of two irreducible elements or opposing principles: good and evil. This dualism is not metaphysical but works on the historical and the temporal plane. The present age is subject to the powers of evil. Satan and his hosts reign. But this world will be propelled into a new age of perfect righteousness under the authority of ultimate goodness, the Lord Jesus Christ.

Third, a rigid determinism marks the whole. Revelation testifies that all things move in concert toward a divinely predetermined end. Everything is inevitable; nothing is left to chance. The problem of human agency or free will within the context of Godâs omniscience never surfaces. But there is a tacit insistence that Godâs ultimate victory is worked out within the framework of human freedom. Yet apocalyptic writers in general, and John in particular, are very pessimistic about humankindâs being able to combat evil and rise out of the present wicked world on its own.47 God alone has that power. Therefore, central to the apocalyptic framework is faith in God and in his power to control the future and turn all things to the blessing of the righteous.

The above point needs to be emphasized. There is a difference between the viewpoint found in Revelationâthat God has preordained future eventsâand the false idea that individual salvation is predetermined, leaving no room for agency.

The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints rejects the belief in predestinationâthat God predetermines the salvation or the damnation of every individual. The gospel teaches that genuine human freedom and genuine responsibilityâindividual agency in both thought and actionâare crucial in both the development and the outcome of a personâs life. Church doctrine rejects the strict dual option providing only heaven or hell as an outcome, since people vary widely in their levels of spiritual attainment. At the same time, Latter-day Saints recognize both the indispensable need for the grace of God manifested through Jesus Christ and the effective spiritual guidance that comes through divine foreordination.48

Having noted that, it must also be stressed that the book of Revelation, along with much of apocalyptic literature, places full trust in the unerring foreknowledge of God and the power of what he has foreordained. So far as events are concerned, nothing is left to chance; all history moves according to what God has predesigned. A foundation stone of apocalyptic, then, is what God has determined beforehand will come to pass, and the book of Revelation never backs away from this viewpoint.

The Churchâs position rests in part on Paulâs statement that God âwill render to every man according to his deedsâ and that âthere is no respect of persons with Godâ (Rom. 2:6, 11). Though Johnâs Revelation shares the three elements noted aboveâan eschatological outlook, strong dualism, and solid predeterminismâwith other Jewish and Christian apocalypses, it is unique. John is the earliest known Christian writer to actually produce an original apocalypse using this genre as a kind of divine code, and it worked well. Though there are more variant manuscript readings of the text of Revelation than of any other New Testament book, these are not sufficient to cause uncertainty on the meaning of a single paragraph taken as a whole.53

The Book as an Extended Allegory Whose Images Are Symbolic Representations of Reality

One must keep in mind that Revelation is an extended allegory. In these, symbols are used to represent reality. Each image always explains and illuminates some aspect of its subject. For example, in chapter 17, Babylon is compared to a richly decked-out courtesan. The picture carries the idea of one who promotes lust for money and willingly uses others to her own advantage. But Babylon is itself a symbol. The ancient city was the center of extravagant wealth and also the seat of corruption and slavery. Thus, the depiction is an excellent symbol for those degenerate and depraved institutions, governments, businesses, and churches that exist in the last days, all of which, for wealth, fame, and/or power, misuse and corrupt those under their control or influence.

In Revelation, we meet one symbol after another, all begging to be interpreted. Sometimes the book itself identifies what the symbol means. For example, the great whore mentioned above sits âupon many watersâ (17:1), which John learns represents âpeoples, and multitudes, and nations, and tonguesâ (17:15). Thus, the imagery of âmany watersâ shows the extent of the great harlotâs reach.

What is helpful is that when a figure is defined in one place, that meaning can be applied when the image shows up elsewhere. For example, the image of the dragon is defined as Satan (12:9), so when that image shows up again (13:4; 20:2), it is safe to assume it still refers to the devil.

When the book does not give the meaning of an object or motif, it is best to use both the immediate and broad contexts in which the image is found to determine the pictureâs meaning. Then the probable identification made at its initial appearance can reasonably be applied to its later ones. For example, in chapter 13, we meet the beast from the sea, which most likely represents those human institutions and powers Satan has under his control and to which he gives assistance. When the beast shows up again in chapter 17, we can assume it denotes the same institutions.

Also very helpful in determining the meaning of a depiction are the commonplace associations coming mainly from the Old Testament but also from certain cultural elements of those who were Johnâs audience. Thus, the Hebrew and Greek scriptures, Jewish writings including apocryphal and pseudepigraphical materials, early Christian sources, and Greco-Roman items can all illuminate the text.

Another important point when trying to decipher the depictions in Johnâs work is that sometimes images can be easily pictured but sometimes they cannot. A number are composed of jarring descriptions and unfeasible combinations and doing impossible activities. For example, when John first sees the Savior in chapter 1, he notes that he has a sword continually issuing from his mouth (1:16). Historically, such a thing has never and will never happen. Further, the Seer describes the beast in 13:1 as having seven heads but ten horns. For easy visualization, there are either not enough or too many horns. The four living creatures met in 4:6 are located simultaneously at the corners of Godâs throne and at the exterior of his dais. Thus, they occupy two places at the same time. In all cases, it is important to remember that the images are not to be drawn or even imagined; they are, however, to be interpreted. The point is that Johnâs masterwork was never intended for the canvas and brush but for the mind. As with the images found in Ezekiel, his depictions are riddles demanding to be solved.57 Often these jarring and unreal images, along with others, bring in a dynamic and interactive quality meant to engage the mind of the thoughtful reader.

One last note. Each image can have multiple points of comparison and, therefore, meaning. The concern is, of course, not to be carried away by these but to use only those that best apply. Our objective in this commentary has been to get to the core meanings and associations but not to go beyond them. Also, it is important to note that not every detail described by John has deep significance. Some of the visionâs particulars reflect what John saw and round out the image giving vividness and power to his presentation. Flaming eyes, feet burning as brass, or various colored stones are all means by which John catches and holds the mindâs eye. Their individual meaning must never be taken out of the context of which they are a part.

The Use of Numbers in Revelation

Before leaving this section, a word must be said about numbers as they appear in Revelation. The literature of the whole ancient world, not just that of the Jews, reveals a fondness for using numbers to communicate ideas. Why certain numbers became laden with symbolic meaning is unknown; in most cases this arose in prehistoric times and is now lost to us. It is of interest that there is a general consistency across cultures to the meaning of certain numbers. That is not to say that numbers are never used as numbers. Indeed, they mostly signify literal quantity. However, in certain contexts, especially in apocalyptic literature, they take on a qualitative meaning and thus express ideas. This is the case in Revelation. One should interpret numbers along with the other symbolic representations. Fortunately, they are used consistently. For example, the numbers 4, 7, and 1,000 denote aspects of wholeness: the first to the world, the second to totality or completeness, and the last to superlative greatness and perfection.64 The various chapters in this commentary will draw out the meaning of the numbers in their various contexts.

Nonrealism

Readers, as noted above, encounter abrupt changes, impossible combinations, and contradictions. The Language of Revelation

The book of Revelation, like the rest of the New Testament, was written in Koine Greek, an evolved and simplified form of classical Attic Greek. By the first century AD, it had become the lingua franca of the whole Roman Empire. It âtended toward shorter, simpler sentences,â and âsome of the syntactic subtleties [of classical Greek] were lost, or at least declined.â Although Koine did not have the precision and refinement of classical Greek, it replaced it with greater explicitness.71

There are some peculiarities in the Greek used in Revelation. Some scholars attribute these irregularities to Johnâs imperfect knowledge of Greek, and that may be so considering it was not his primary tongue. Making allowances, however, for the unusual nature of the apocalypse, some scholars have noted that the similarities between it and Johnâs Gospel are quite striking.72 In fact, most of the time John followed the rules of standard Hellenic Greek, indicating that he indeed had a good knowledge of the language. When these unusual forms do appear, it is almost always in the context of quotes or allusions to the Old Testament in its Greek translation, known as the Septuagint. Johnâs contemporary readers would have immediately recognized these allusions. We see a similarity in latter-day revelations. The Doctrine and Covenants is filled with both direct quotes and paraphrases from the Old and New Testaments.

Translation Philosophy and Methodology

As noted above, Koine Greek was the lingua franca of the Mediterranean world during the first century AD. It was a language that could express thoughts and ideas with plainness and clarity. However, because of the great differences in syntax, style, and grammar between ancient Greek and modern English, it is no easy task to produce a clear and understandable translation. An overly literal translation can easily lead to confusion and misunderstanding. While the King James Version of the Bible was a remarkable achievement for its time, there has been significant progress in our understanding of ancient Greek in the intervening four hundred years. Also, the King James Version used the Textus Receptus, a late sixteenth-century version of the Greek New Testament that was less accurate than the much earlier manuscripts used in modern critical editions of the New Testament today, such as The Nestle-Aland Greek New Testament73 and the Society for Biblical Literature Greek New Testament.74 Moreover, English itself has evolved and changed significantly in those four hundred years. For the above reasons, we felt that a modern English translation was an important and necessary part of this commentary. Each section of this commentary begins with the Greek text from the Society for Biblical Literatureâs Greek New Testament. This is followed by two parallel columns with the King James Version on the left and the Brigham Young University New Testament Rendition on the right. In our Rendition, we have tried to convey as clearly and simply as possible in modern English the sense of the ancient Greek text. When there is ambiguity in the Greek, for example, if a passage can be understood in two different ways, we have tried to preserve that ambiguity in English rather than force our own interpretation on it. For example, the first three words of the book of Revelation, á¼ÏÎ¿ÎºÎ¬Î»ÏÏÎ¹Ï á¼¸Î·ÏÎ¿á¿¦ Î§ÏÎ¹ÏÏÎ¿á¿¦ (apokalypsis iÄsou christou), are rendered âa revelation of Jesus Christ.â The genitive case of Jesus Christ is ambiguous. Is it an objective genitive (a revelation about Jesus Christ) or is it subjective (a revelation from Jesus Christ)? Our Rendition preserves that ambiguity. We have included copious notes and explanations so the reader can have a clear understanding of why we have translated a passage in a certain way. This is especially true when our Rendition differs from the KJV. Our goal has been to provide an English translation that is easy to read and understand while, at the same time, being as true as possible to the sense of the Greek text. The text of Revelation designates seven Asian churches as Godâs audience. These churches were Laodicea, Philadelphia, Ephesus, Smyrna, Pergamum, Thyatira, and Sardis. The Apostles scattered throughout the Parthian and Roman Empires proclaiming the gospel, John among them. Eventually he took up permanent residence in the city of Ephesus in present-day Turkey. The Church in Asia Minor

One would think that the Saints would have been secure, but such was not the case. As John labored there, oppression struck. According to certain ancient sources, the Roman emperor Domitian developed a strong dislike for the Christians and, like the infamous Nero, moved against them, but on a much larger scale.164 Eusebius, the early church historian, states that in the fourteenth year of his reign (ca. It is not hard to see why the Saints had trouble in Asia. The province was very important to Rome. It was so rich in natural and human resources that many Roman nobles viewed it as a proconsular plum that guaranteed wealth to whomever the emperor appointed as its overseer. Due to its excellent natural resources, good roads that connected it to both the east and west, and excellent ports that gave it access to the broader world, its cities had become centers of manufacturing, especially textiles. By and large, its upper crust inhabitants were rich, sophisticated, and cultured.

Making the province even more important was its position as the guardian of the eastern frontier of the Empire. And being part of the Roman Empire greatly benefited the Asians. Roman attention and largess kept the area strong, pacified, and fortified. Thus, both Rome and Asia Minor profited from their close ties.166

It is little wonder, therefore, that the area was the seat of emperor worship. âNowhere else in the Roman Empire did the provincials practise so devotedly and earnestly the cult of diva Roma and especially of the divinely-honored emperor.â167 Few of the regionâs leaders were willing to upset the status quo, and some among the prominent citizens were willing to force others to complyâeven if it meant using ostracism, harassment, and, when necessary, outright persecution to keep them in line.

Ephesus was, indeed, a good choice.168 With Pergamum and Smyrna, Ephesus stood as one of the three great cities of Asia Minor. Of the three, it had the largest commercial center due to its excellent harbor. Adding to its status was its position as the chief administrative headquarters of the area whose governor directed all the affairs of the province.169 The city was a center for the imperial cult, but its citizens were most proud of the large and beautiful temple dedicated to the Roman goddess Diana, or Artemis, as the Greeks knew her. Emperor Worship in Asia Minor

It took the people of Asia Minor little time to realize that monetary success was tied to joining the imperial religious bureaucracy. Domitian played on this, promoting emperor worship throughout the area as no ruler had done since Nero. He demanded the divine titles that went along with the role of Caesar, especially âour lord and god.â174 He also tied himself directly to the worship of the goddess Roma, thus making loyalty to the state and to the emperor one and the same. The rulers of many cities felt honored when he allowed them to build temples to the goddess Roma or to himself. Such acts of sycophancy gave them not only status but also access to imperial largess.175 Thereby, both civil and religious feeling promoted and defended the cult. In Asia proper, all the major cities but Philadelphia and Loadicea harbored imperial altars with their attendant priests, and all but Thyatira boasted imperial temples.176 Thus the area was a hotbed of imperial worship. Though the temples were the centers of such worship, a good deal of devotion went on in various formal venues, especially among the trade guilds.

The Role of Guilds and Trade Unions

There is little doubt that guilds and trade unions were both ubiquitous and strong in this region. The area was very rich in natural resources that supported a strong economy, much of it based on textiles. The area boasted many producers and exporters. Wool and flax production abounded and there were numerous carding and weaving facilities in places like Laodicea and Colossae. Sardis and Lydia cooperated to produce the red-dyed cloth and embroidered garments in high demand throughout much of the empire. The area also abounded in silver- and goldsmiths, leather workers, and tanners. Most of these combined to form guilds or trade unions to protect their jobs and promote their products.177

The usual practice at guild meetings was to offer up incense or libations to their patron deity and to the emperor. The act showed not only religious faith but also honored civic duty. For Greco-Roman citizens, anyone who refused these simple, holy, and honorific acts not only dishonored the state but could also bring down divine disfavor.

The Position of the Christians

While John labored in the province of Asia, conditions worsened for the Saints. As the Church moved into the last decade of the first century, anti-Christian sentiment grew ever stronger against them.178 Emperor Nero, decades before, laid the groundwork for the awful position in which the Saints shortly found themselves. He had decreed that merely being a Christian was a capital offense. Thus, a Saint who never broke a single governmental law could still be disenfranchised, banished, tortured, or executed.

Most of the caesars who followed Nero during the first century rarely harassed the Saints. On occasion there were localized and sporadic persecutions, but for the most part the Saints got along well and, as noted, the religion spread.179

Thus, the position of many Christians, as John wrote his Revelation, was not too bad. Nonetheless, most scholars still agree that Domitianâs ever-increasing demands to be worshiped as âlord and god,â especially toward the end of his reign, put ever-greater pressure on the Saints. John on the Isle of Patmos

Patmos, one of the Sporades Islands, lies about forty miles southwest of Ephesus; at the time, it was part of the territory of the city of Miletus. It is a small volcanic island, roughly butterfly-shaped, about ten miles long by six miles wide. Contrary to what some have said, it was not a Roman penal colony and there is no evidence anyone other than John was ever banished there.182 The Miletians used this island, along with two others, as a naval station housing a garrison to ward off pirates. That John was there opens the possibility that certain Roman officials may have viewed the social status of the Christian leadership as higher than previously believed. This possibility is best understood in light of the Roman practice of banishment.

Roman Forms of Banishment

The ancient sources all agree that John was exiled to Patmos. However, the Latin words exilium, or exsul, referred to a person leaving an area on either a voluntary or an involuntary basis; therefore, they do not tell us if John was banished to Patmos or went there of his own accord. It is noteworthy that the Roman legal system had a dual-penalty system that depended more on the social status of the individual than on the actual crime he committed. Those with proper dignitas, that is, rank or station, received more lenient sentences than those of lesser station. For example, a crime requiring the death penalty for a lower-class individual mandated merely banishment for a member of the upper class.183 Therefore, those with sufficient dignitas could often escape the death penalty even for capital crimes. They did not, however, get off scot-free. If the crime was of sufficient severity, even nobles were banished.

The law allowed for two types of compulsory banishment: relegatio and deportatio. The former was often temporary (perhaps lasting as little as three years) and did not necessarily include the forfeiture of citizenship or property. This took two forms: relegatio ad (also referred to as relegatio in) which was banishment to a place, often to an island or penal colony; or relegatio ab (also referred to as relegatio extra) which was restriction from a place, allowing a person to travel or live anywhere but in a certain province or area. Deportatio was much more onerous, designating a long-term banishment to a specific place along with loss of citizenship and property. Nobles often chose this sentence in lieu of death.184

Taking the facts of banishment into account, there are six possibilities that may explain how John came to Patmos. First, he may have decided to go to the island to preach the gospel to members of the garrison stationed there. Second, he may have gone under inspiration to have time to meditate in the seclusion necessary for the revelation. Third, a magistrate may have relegated him from the province (relegatio ab), and John went to the island voluntarily for refuge. Fourth, a magistrate may have banished him specifically to that island (relegatio ad) for a predetermined duration of time. Fifth, a magistrate may have relegated him to the island as a permanent exile with all loss of citizenship and any property (the extreme form of relegatio ad). Sixth and finally, a magistrate may have condemned him to death and, to avoid execution, he chose exile to the island (deportatio).

At his last coming, his purpose is to save the earth and the righteous by destroying those who would destroy them (see 11:18).

The point is that the earliest Saints did not see a marked contrast between the Jesus of Revelation and he of the Gospels. One of the reasons Johnâs work was copied and spread was because it sounded very much like the Jesus that walked with and taught them. His doctrine, he said, was based on information that he had seen and heard from heaven itself. âHe that cometh from above,â he said, speaking of himself, âis above all: he that is of the earth is earthly, and speaketh of the earth: he that cometh from heaven is above all. And what he hath seen and heard, that he testifieth; and no [natural] man receiveth his testimonyâ (John 3:31â32 with 1 Cor. 2:14). Such, however, was not the case with his disciples. They fully accepted his witness. As a result, he affirmed, âHenceforth I call you not servants; for the servant knoweth not what his lord doeth: but I have called you friends; for all things that I have heard of my Father I have made known unto youâ (John 15:15), which included materials meant for the disciplesâ ears alone.

To the multitudes, he spoke in parables to hide his message from those who would accept neither it nor him, but not so his disciples. To them he could say, âBlessed are your eyes, for they see: and your ears, for they hear. For verily I say unto you, That many prophets and righteous men have desired to see those things which ye see, and have not seen them; and to hear those things which ye hear, and have not heard themâ (Matt. 13:16â17). He assured them that âunto you it is given to know the mysteries of the kingdom of Godâ (Luke 8:10). These wondrous teachings, however, were kept within the inner circle of the disciples and few ever made it even into the Gospel records. Indeed, Paul confirmed to his readers that âwe speak the wisdom of God in a mystery, even the hidden wisdom, which God ordained before the world unto our glory: Which none of the princes of this world knew, . . . But God hath revealed them unto us by his Spirit: for the Spirit searcheth all things, yea, the deep things of Godâ (1 Cor. 2:7â8, 10).

Though these mysteries were carefully guarded, later generations knew that they had existed. As late as the fourth century, a number of Christian leaders confirmed that some important material had never made it into the Gospel accounts. The bishop of Caesarea, Basil the Great, assured his readers that important material had deliberately been left out of the New Testament record but was preserved secretly in the traditions of the Apostles.9 Irenaeus, bishop in Lyon, writing in the late second century, gave a peek into what some of these teachings were. In his work The Demonstration of the Gospel, which he said he wrote so that people could âhold to the faith without deviation,â10 he included information dealing with âthe seven heavens, the powers and the archangels, the relationship of the cherubim and seraphim to the Word and Wisdom of God and the role of the seven-fold Spirit.â11 All this echoes material found in Revelation. Therefore, it is not surprising that the earliest Christians had no problem believing that the Lord of Johnâs Gospel, who declared heavenly things to those who would hear, and the author of Revelation, where marvelous things were recorded, were both the same.

The second reason the earliest Christians accepted the work was because it showed Jesus in a new light, thus rounding out their understanding of him found in the Gospels and other writings. Indeed, in Revelation, they found neither the suffering servant of Mark nor the new Moses of Matthew. In this work, they found the divine and glorified Christ working out Godâs purposes for the salvation of his people in the last days and then ushering in the grand millennial era when all would know God. They rejoiced in his Second Coming, which would bring with it judgmentâreward for the righteous and punishment for the wicked. In Revelation they met Jesus the vindicator, the one who would avenge them before their enemies. It is important to note that their desire was never revenge but rather vindication. The former carries the strong implication of vindictiveness while the latter looks for recompense for injuries unjustly sustained. Those of Johnâs readership who had converted from paganism and now suffered for their decision reached to their Lord for strength and vindication. They believed that punishment for wrongdoing was not a spontaneous or unilateral decision by God alone. Rather, it was Godâs direct response to their appeal. This thought motivated them to raise their voices to him.12 Within the pages of Revelation, they came to understand that the Lord was very aware of their plight and that the day would come when he would respond not only to their desires but also to all those other Saints mauled and persecuted.

So when did doubt arise concerning the authenticity of the work and of Johnâs authorship? Initially, it came from objections raised by Dionysius, bishop of Alexandria, Egypt, during the mid-third century. He balked at the strong tradition that insisted that the work belonged to John the Beloved. And the tradition was very strong.13 First there was Papias, bishop of Hierapolis (near present-day Pamukkale in western Turkey), who wrote in the first third of the second century and, therefore, near the time of the workâs composition. He ascribed Revelation to âthe Elder Johnâ whom he knew personally.14 The next known person to mention the book was Justin, martyred in AD 165, who said clearly that Revelation was written by âJohn, one of the apostles of Christ.â15 The next writer was the aforementioned Irenaeus, bishop in Lyon. He was a follower of Polycarp (martyred in AD 155), who had personally known the Apostle. Irenaeus notes that the book of Revelation âwas seen . . . almost in my own lifetime, at the end of Domitianâs reign.â16 Also suggesting that John wrote Revelation was Clement of Alexandria (d. c. AD 215), who related that John was banished to the Isle of Patmos.17 Origen (d. c. AD 254), the great biblical scholar, did not hesitate to give credit for the work to John.18 Finally, Victorinus of Pettau (d. c. AD 305), who wrote the first full commentary on the Apocalypse, confirmed that John the Apostle received the revelation while on Patmos during the reign of Domitian.19 Circumstantially, Eusebius mentions that his oldest sources note that John returned to Ephesus at the end of Domitianâs reign âafter his exile on the island.â20

All these early sources tell but one story: John, who was banished to the Isle of Patmos, was the author of Revelation, which was written just before the turn of the first century. As noted, Dionysius was well aware of this tradition but dismissed it on the grounds that the stylistic and lexical differences between Johnâs Gospel (along with 1 John) and Revelation were so different that they could not have been authored by the same person.21 There must be, he insisted, another John who wrote the latter, perhaps John Mark who was mentioned in Acts 12:12; 15:37. The bishop had no problem accepting the work as inspired but insisted that it did not carry apostolic authority.22 Eusebius himself, based on his interpretation of his sources, also felt there were two Johns; one was the Apostle and the other was âthe Elder,â and it was the latter, he believed, who wrote the work.23 Over time, fewer and fewer eastern bishops were willing to accept the book as apostolic or even authentic.24 Therefore, when it came to decide which books should make up the canon, they wanted only those with sure apostolic authority included. That meant Revelation should be left out. The bishops of the west, however, did not believe Dionysiusâs analysis but clung to tradition, insisting on the apostolic authorship of the work and also insisting that it should be included in the canon. They won.

Though Dionysiusâs observations about the differences between the two texts is correct, he overlooked four factors which point to Jesusâ Apostle being the author: the first, as noted, was the very strong tradition that ascribed the book to John the Apostle; the second was the many similarities between Revelation and the Gospel; the third was the very different circumstances in which the two works were produced; and finally there was the fact that John was writing two different genres, one a gospel and the other an apocalypse. The latter two factors easily account for quite a number of the stylistic and lexical differences between the two texts.25

So where do Latter-day Saints stand on the issue of the authorship? We have scriptural insights that resolve the problem. To the prophet Nephi, writing around 592 BC, an angel revealed that âone of the twelve apostles of the Lambâ had been foreordained to this work. Indeed, he was to write specifically âconcerning the end of the worldâ (1 Ne. 14:20, 22). That is a good description of Revelation. Nephi went on to say that âthe name of the apostle of the Lamb was John, according to the word of the angelâ (1 Ne. 14:27). Nephiâs witness confirms the rightness of the position held by the western bishops and the appropriateness of placing the work in the Bible.

Johnâs work has come forth as a testament of the foreknowledge of God, giving many hope and assurance through nearly two millennia. Its message, for those who are willing to pay the price to understand it, continues to proclaim comfort. This is especially true for the Saints living during the last days. It does this primarily by assisting them in preparing for the Second Coming of the Son of the Most High. Indeed, there is no other people for whom the message of this book is more germane.

Dating and Interpretive Approaches

As with the other works in the New Testament, dating the text is important as a means of establishing authenticity and context, the latter greatly helping with understanding the purpose and message of a work. With Revelation, dating the piece has been greatly influenced by the various authorsâ interpretive approaches. Since the two go hand in glove, we will consider both together. The treatment here is brief since the Restoration greatly helps with this issue, so belaboring all the points of view seems superfluous. Nonetheless, a review is necessary in order to place the LDS position in its niche.

A number of scholars have placed the creation of the basic material in Revelation a number of years before the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70. The foremost of these is Margaret Barker, who argues that much of the material came from items written and gathered during the years of the tenth Jubilee, which began about AD 16 and ended about AD 66. That period was filled with great struggles for the early Church. The Christians looked to older prophecies, many in the Old Testament, as well as some generated in their own time, to help put their period in perspective and understand their current situation and mission. These prophecies they interpreted as describing an imminent coming of the Lord for which the Saints were to prepare. Barker concludes that much of the material and the central message of the major pieces that later made up the work looked exclusively to the beliefs of the Church during the first few decades after the Saviorâs death, but she adds that John the Beloved later developed a new interpretation of this material that looked to a future coming of the Lord, which he compiled while on the Island of Patmos.26 Thus, this view sees the creation of the material that would eventually make up the book reaching as far back as Old Testament times to just before the fall of Jerusalem. As attractive as this thesis is, the evidence is not compelling and, therefore, few follow Barkerâs lead.

The Preterist School

Another school, called âthe Preterists,â places the writing of the whole of Revelation just before the fall of Jerusalem. The word means âpast in fulfillment.â The Preterists interpret all biblical prophecy as having been fulfilled shortly after they were delivered. Thus, none look to the distant future.27 So far as Revelation is concerned, these commentators fall into two main camps.

The first interprets Revelation as predicting the fall of Jerusalem to the Romans in AD 70 and, therefore, insist it was written before that date. As an example of their view, they point to 11:1â2, which, they say, shows the temple was still standing when John wrote because, they insist, that imagery would mean nothing if the temple were destroyed. Further, 11:2 and 11:8 refer to âthe holy cityâ and âthe great city,â which, they maintain, can only mean Jerusalem as it stood just before the Roman siege, because after that date it became little more than an army outpost for decades.

As a further example, in 17:9, the work mentions âseven mountains,â which these people take as meaning Rome. The text further states that the mountains also represent seven kings, of whom the sixth was then in power. Some speculate that the first of these âkingsâ was Julius Caesar, making the sixth Nero, the original persecutor of the Church and the ruler when Vespasian began the siege against Jerusalem. Others insist that the first king was Augustus, making the sixth Servius Sulpicius Galba, who reigned briefly after Neroâs suicide and died in AD 69 shortly before Jerusalem fell. He was followed in quick succession by Otho, Vitelius, and finally Vespasian, whom they count as the seventh; he ruled when Jerusalem actually fell, and he ordered the state of Judea be destroyed.

They believe John wrote to spur Christian resistance to Jewish persecution with the promise that the Jewish nation would fall because of its wickedness. All the Christians had to do was hold true to their faith and the apostate Jewish nation, personified as Babylon the Great, would fall and God would vindicate his followers as true Israel. If they held out for only a few years, then Christianity would be firmly in place and Godâs kingdom permanently established on earth. All of this, they feel, was realized with the fall of Jerusalem.

The strength of this view is that it militates against prophecy failed. For millennia, some Christians have felt that the Parousia was imminent, and countless clergy have predicted the date of Christâs Second Coming only to be proved wrong. As a result, over time many Christians have lost faith in the Bible and in the prophecies it contains and left the faith. The Preterist position saves both prophecy and faith by showing that the prophecies were indeed fulfilled, but shortly after they were given. No prophecy, they aver, looked to the far future but were addressed to the specific challenges faced at the time by the people to which they were addressed.

The problems with this view are many. A major one for this camp is shoehorning all the events taking place in Revelation into a few short years. Preterists inadequately explain many of the images in the book because there are no historical parallels to be found, particularly for the great battles that end with the Second Coming of the Savior. They also disagree on other points, such as the one noted above concerning the identity of the seven kings. In short, as with much of their interpretation of the symbols, they have to use excessive force to get things to fit.

The second camp into which the Preterists fall holds that the book looks a bit further out, even to the fall of Rome. The workâs purpose, they feel, is to assure the Christians of the final triumph of Godâs kingdom over that of the unholy Roman Empire and to encourage its readers to resist Romeâs influence. Compromise with this âbeastâ would be deadly because all who did so would go down when it went down. They believe that the prophecies in Revelation were fulfilled in the fifth century when Rome ceased to be a world power. They readily admit that some items in Revelation do not fit historical reality, but that was because, they say, John simply got his facts mixed up.28

The strength of this view is that it does not force all the events recorded in Revelation into a few short years. The view also allows the work to be written after the fall of Jerusalem and at a time when the antagonism between Christianity and Rome was escalating. Further, âBabylon the Greatâ fits much better as Rome than it does as Jerusalem. Even better is the idea that the work was written to encourage the Saints to hold out against the seductions of Rome and the forces of persecution. Nonetheless, there are significant problems. Chief among these is the fact that they sidestep points in Johnâs work that do not fit their thesis. For example, the victory of the people of God and the overthrow of evil as depicted in the last chapters were never realized. Thus, much of the imagery is not handled. Further, because they blame inconsistencies on Johnâs ineptness, they cast suspicion on the authenticity of the work. Another major problem is that, like the other camp, their view of Godâs judgments is simply too narrow. The judgments in the Apocalypse are clearly worldwide and directed not at one nation but many, and not one people but all the âinhabitants of the earthâ who oppose God.

The Historicist School

The next school is that of âthe Historicists.â This school is more amorphous than that of the Preterists, having considerably more branches. Where these come together is that they see the prophecy in Revelation as being fulfilled throughout history up to modern times. The seals, trumpets, woes, bowl plagues, and so on describe various eras of history in a somewhat chronological order, including the fall of Rome to the Visigoths, the invasion of Christian lands by the Muslims, the rise of the Catholic Church, the corrupted medieval papacy, and various European and world wars.

The strength of this view is that it spreads out the images in Revelation across time and geography and therefore allows for a more universal interpretation of the events. Also, its adherents do not have to put the creation of the work before the fall of Jerusalem, and therefore they can look for a period that better matches the conditions John describes. The major problems are three: first, it centers only on Europe and the west, leaving out the rest of the world, including the Middle East; second, the proponents interpret Revelation from their particular point of view in history and cannot agree with one another, because each one limits the images in Revelation to historical events he or she propounds; and finally, their position makes Revelation have no real significance or relevance to Johnâs initial audience, the Christians of the first century.

The Futurist School

The next school is that of âthe Futurists.â This one, like âthe Preterists,â can be divided into two main camps, though both see most of the events described in Revelation as happening immediately preceding the Second Coming.29 The most popular form is âdispensational futurism,â which views the images in Revelation as quite literal and chronological such that the direction history will take is clearly spelled out. In brief, it sees the events as follows: the modern establishment of the state of Israel including an ingathering of the Jews; the rapture when the righteous Christians are taken into heaven; the coming of the reign of the anti-Christ; a seven-year period of tribulation and woe; the gathering of the great armies and battle of Armageddon; the Lordâs Second Coming with the temporary incarceration of the devil; Satanâs final rebellion at the end of the millennial era; the last battle between good and evil ending with the Godâs intervention; and, finally, the reign of the Son of God and his Saints in the eternal realm counterbalanced by the eternal punishment of all their enemies.

The second camp, referred to as âmodified futurism,â takes the images in Revelation much more figuratively. They reject the idea that the events represent a strict chronology and reject the idea of the âpretribulation rapture.â They do, however, see the Christian Church, the âtrue Israel,â as passing through a period of trials. Revelation chapters 4â8:1, they believe, cover the period from Christâs ascension up to the time immediately before the Second Coming. Chapters 8:2â22:4 describe the very last events before the Parousia. The plagues associated with bowls, trumpets, and seals look at the same time period but from different points of view, all in the future.

The strengths of the Futuristsâ positions are many, but the primary one is that it allows for a universal judgment of the earth that fits nicely with the context of Johnâs writings. Though the two camps differ on whether or not there is a ârapture,â they agree that nearly all the events recorded occur at the end-time. The weakness of this view is threefold. First, as with the Historicist view, interpreting the text in this way leaves little if any relevance for Johnâs first-century audience. Second, by holding to a more literal rendering of the images in Revelation, they misread the symbolic importance of many. And third, by playing on the imminence of the Second Coming, they open themselves up for predictions and disappointments that pushed the development of the âPreteristâ camp in the first place.

The Idealist School

Lastly, there is âthe Idealistâ camp. This group holds to the idea that Revelation has a timeless appeal because the message itself is, indeed, timeless.30 The book should not be tied too closely to actual history. Rather, it portrays the struggle between good and evil in all ages and holds out the promise that, in the end, right will indeed prevail. The strength of this position is that it makes the book relevant to any and all ages and, more especially, for those living in times when the righteous go through periods of intense stress and maltreatment. Further, this position gives the book universal appeal for people in all ages, and the fact that it does not promote an imminent Parousia means it bypasses the concerns that created the Preterist camps. Those who propound this view are very good at highlighting the workâs focus on the war between good and evil. But its weaknesses are just opposite that of the Historicists and Preterists in that they leave the images amorphous and lifeless with no historical basis. Thus the message remains vague and the images unnecessarily unaccounted for. Further, it leaves no way to check the view against any kind of standard, therefore making it impossible to test.

Evidence from the Text

So what does Revelation itself tell us concerning the period in which it was written and what eras it covers? Evidence from the book points strongly to the last decade of the first century. First, the book shows that the Christians were under strong pressure to participate in the cult of emperor worship, notably in the province of Asia (see 13:4â8, 15â16; 14:9â11; 15:2; 16:2; 19:20; 20:4). Though the practice of bestowing divine honors on Roman emperors reached all the way back to Julius Caesar (d. 44 BC), the practice took some time to become empire-wide. The eastern empire was predisposed to the idea of mortal rulers having divine qualities because, for millennia, the Egyptians and Mesopotamians had worshiped their leaders as gods. When the Greek Ptolemies conquered Egypt in the third century BC, they took full advantage of that belief to hold on to their position. Their cousins, the Seleucids, did the same in the eastern lands they conquered. Even so, it took time for Romans to feel comfortable with the idea, but when they finally did, the Christians felt Romeâs power.

The earliest documented cases of the Roman government actually moving against Christians come near the beginning of the second century, during the reign of the Emperor Trajan. However, Johnâs letters to the seven Churches show that the persecution had been going on for about two decades before that, pointing to Revelationâs creation not before the fall of Jerusalem as the Preterists hold, but to the time of Domitian. Weighing all the scholarship to date, Sweet has accurately concluded that âthe earlier date [for the writing of Revelation] may be right, but the internal evidence [which the Preterists and others present] is not sufficient to outweigh the firm tradition stemming from Irenaeus,â which puts the workâs creation during the last decade of the first Christian century.31

The LDS Position

So where do the Latter-day Saints fit in all this? The Prophet Joseph Smith provided the answer. He noted, âThe things which John saw had no allusion to the scenes of the days of Adam, Enoch, Abraham, or Jesus, only so far as is plainly represented to John, and clearly set forth by him. John saw that only which was lying in futurity, and which was shortly to come to pass.â He quoted 1:1â3 to show that the forepart of the vision concerned what was happening in the days in which John wrote. Next he quoted 4:1 to show that the rest of the vision pertained to the future. Speaking of the latter-day forces symbolized as beasts found in chapter 12, he noted that âJohn saw beasts that had to do with things on the earth, but not in past ages.â âWhen God made use of the figure of a beast in visions to the prophets,â Joseph Smith explained, âhe did it to represent those Kingdoms who had degenerated and become corruptâthe Kingdoms of the world, but he never made use of the figure of a beast nor any of the brute kind to represent his kingdom.â Joseph Smith went on to assure the Saints that âthe beasts which John saw had to devour the inhabitants of the earth in days to come.â He explained that âthe revelations do not give us to understand anything of the past in relation to the kingdom of God,â because nearly all of them looked to the future.32

His knowledge on this matter came, at least partially, from a revelation he received in 1832 while he was working on his revision of the Bible (now D&C 77). That revelation indicated that the material from chapter 7 on dealt with matters that would take place not long before the Second Coming, during the time symbolized as the sixth and seventh seals. Indeed, the events recorded in chapters 8, 9, and 11 were all to take place not long before the coming of the Lord in glory (see D&C 77:10â13). This revelation went counter to scholarly thinking in Joseph Smithâs day, most of which placed the events in chapters 7â9 and 11 in the past. The revelation clearly places them in the future and thus broke a long-standing point of view.33

Thus, the LDS position fits closest to that of the âModified Futuristsâ but also rests a bit on the âIdealist camp.â It recognizes the fact that some of the material applies only to Johnâs day, and therefore that the text was relevant to the earliest Christians. Also, as will be shown in the section dealing with the interpretation of Revelation, the LDS position does not take the images as literally as some Futurists do. Indeed, the information we have shows that the images are âsubjective, figurative, and symbolic rather than strictly objective, literal, and historical.â34 Thus, they need to be interpreted to be understood. Some may see taking this stand as opening the door to the problems of failed prophecy that lead to the creation of the Preterist school, but such is not the case. For Latter-day Saints, the Second Coming is a reality and an event that will take place in the not-too-distant future, but because we have living prophets, we are not a bit concerned about the prophecies not being fulfilled.

The Structure of Johnâs Work

The structure of Revelation, as with nearly all of its other aspects, is highly debated. Structure deals with how the various parts relate to the whole. Because Johnâs master work is extremely complex, it does not yield itself to easy structural formulations. One view sees John building his work around distinct visions having seven elements each. The visions are disparate units carrying their own message and do not present a strict chronological sequence.35 Another view argues for a chiastic structure with the central point put somewhere between chapters 11 and 14. The first section (roughly chapters 4â11) deals with human history and the conditions that Christians face in the past, the present, and the near future. The second section (roughly chapters 12â19) deals with eschatology and the situations they will face as this era moves into the end-time.36 Yet another view takes the visions as presenting a chronological sequence with each segment revealing the next stage in world history.37 The last view, for our purposes, takes the later visions as recapitulations of earlier ones but from a different perspective. For example, the bowl plagues in chapters 15â16 recapitulate the trumpet plagues in chapters 8â9.38 Thus, the revelation, as it were, doubles back on itself.

Though all these views have something going for them, the lack of agreement among scholars shows that none are thoroughly convincing.39 This situation exacerbates a difficult interpretive challenge. Do the events associated with the opening of the seven seals, the trumpet pestilences, and the bowl plagues represent apocalyptic descriptions of essentially the same events but from different standpoints (recapitulation theory) or represent a linear unfolding of historical/prophetic events leading through the last days to the final triumph of God and his people (chronological theory)? Evidence from the Restoration suggests that a modified recapitulation and roughly chiastic view is the more likely structure. To cite a few examples, D&C 77:15 identifies two prophets (whose ministry is recorded in Revelation 11) as latter-day ministers to the Jewish nation. Their ministry appears to be part of the last great battle described in Revelation 16 and 19, thus uniting those two events. D&C 77:7 defines the seven seals in Revelation 6 as representing sequential millennial periods that cover the whole of human history. That paradigm suggests that Revelation as a whole focuses primarily on the sixth and seventh seals. D&C 77:12â13 states that the events recorded in chapters 8 and 9 of Revelation are âto be accomplished after the opening of the seventh seal, before the coming of Christ.â That statement ties the events described in those chapters to those recorded in chapters 15â16 and 19, which most scholars agree describe conditions just before and during the Second Coming. The events recorded in Revelation chapters 12 and 13 separate the two pieces of the revelation that report on end-time conditions. These chapters look to proceedings beginning in Johnâs day and set the ground for a better understanding of why the last day events play out as they do. Given the above, it would appear that the format of Revelation does repeat some events but from a different perspective, thus bringing out important lessons that the Lord does not want his people to miss. This commentary points out the various pericopes and shows how they tie together.

Interpretive Methodology

The book of Revelation deserves serious attention. God gave the vision and preserved it for a purpose. He meant his Saints to understand it. It is not, however, a book for the spiritually faint-hearted or intellectually lazy, mainly because one cannot use a straightforward approach in tackling it. The reason is that God gave the visions in a kind of code. Both John and Nephi knew that to be the case,40 but Nephi articulated the reason why. He explained that the Bible would go through the hands of the âgreat and abominable church, which is most abominable above all other churches; for behold, they [will take] away from the gospel of the Lamb many parts which are plain and most precious; and also many covenants of the Lord [will they take] away.â This was done in a deliberate attempt to pervert the gospel and lead people astray (1 Ne. 13:14â28). The problem was how to get the message, so much of which was designed for those living in the last days, through the editors of the great and abominable church. God knew how.

The Work as an Apocalypse

John did not choose the form of the vision; God did. The form is now called âapocalyptic.â This genre of literature received its name from the first word of the book of Revelation, apocalypsis.41 The word apocalypse comes from the Greek á¼ÏÎ¿ÎºÎ¬Î»ÏÏÎ¹Ï, which means revelation or disclosure. This title is quite fitting since one of the main signatures of apocalyptic literature is that it reveals or discloses heavenly secrets to its readers. Apocalypses were first written by a segment of the Jews, although the Anchor Bible Dictionary suggests that it may be possible that there are traces of apocalypticism in Akkadian literature.42 A quantity of nonbiblical apocalyptic literature begins showing up in the third century BC. The majority of apocalypses were written between the third century BC and the first century AD. In the first century AD, the early Christians identified strongly with the Jewish apocalypses and began to adopt them.

Most apocalypses contain a vision or dream had by the writer of the text. This dream is often narrated by a guide who shows the writer around the celestial realm. These dreams are often eschatological and thus deal with the future and portray latter-day conditions as dualistic. During the current evil age, righteousness will battle evil and win due to divine intervention that will bring in a glorious future age. Both God and Satan have agents that work for them and strive to push their objectives. One characteristic of this literature is its use of symbolism which the reader is expected to interpret. The point of the whole is that God is in control of all that goes on, he has foreseen everything that is to happen, and he has prepared the way of escape for his people.43

The Basic Theological Foundation of Apocalyptic Literature

A basic theological foundation forms the ground which gives apocalyptic literature a unique religious perspective.44 This is composed of three elements. First, it is nearly always eschatological. The word denotes that branch of theology that deals with future events, especially those associated with the Second Coming and the millennial era. In looking at a God-derived future, apocalyptic eschatology and prophetic eschatology share common ground,45 that is, both are predictive. The two, however, have different but not mutually exclusive panoramas. The panoramic view of prophetic eschatology looks at history flowing from the present to the future without interruption. In it, heaven works primarily behind the scenes, albeit through earthly prophets, to bring about Godâs ends. The panoramic view of apocalyptic eschatology looks at a future in which history is not only disrupted but discontinued to bring about a brand new history. In it, heaven does not work behind the scenes but moves inexorably onto center stage, climaxing in the destruction of the wicked, the full revelation of Christ, and the introduction of his millennial reign. Apocalyptic eschatology thereby denies humankind the ability to bring about continual peace and righteousness. Indeed, wickedness and war will increase until God brings it to a sudden and dramatic end. The final consummation therefore comes not through the unbroken flow of history but by its divine discontinuation and bringing back to earth paradisiacal glory (A of F 10).46

Second, apocalyptic literature is dualistic. That is, apocalyptic reality consists of two irreducible elements or opposing principles: good and evil. This dualism is not metaphysical but works on the historical and the temporal plane. The present age is subject to the powers of evil. Satan and his hosts reign. But this world will be propelled into a new age of perfect righteousness under the authority of ultimate goodness, the Lord Jesus Christ.

Third, a rigid determinism marks the whole. Revelation testifies that all things move in concert toward a divinely predetermined end. Everything is inevitable; nothing is left to chance. The problem of human agency or free will within the context of Godâs omniscience never surfaces. But there is a tacit insistence that Godâs ultimate victory is worked out within the framework of human freedom. Yet apocalyptic writers in general, and John in particular, are very pessimistic about humankindâs being able to combat evil and rise out of the present wicked world on its own.47 God alone has that power. Therefore, central to the apocalyptic framework is faith in God and in his power to control the future and turn all things to the blessing of the righteous.

The above point needs to be emphasized. There is a difference between the viewpoint found in Revelationâthat God has preordained future eventsâand the false idea that individual salvation is predetermined, leaving no room for agency.

The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints rejects the belief in predestinationâthat God predetermines the salvation or the damnation of every individual. The gospel teaches that genuine human freedom and genuine responsibilityâindividual agency in both thought and actionâare crucial in both the development and the outcome of a personâs life. Church doctrine rejects the strict dual option providing only heaven or hell as an outcome, since people vary widely in their levels of spiritual attainment. At the same time, Latter-day Saints recognize both the indispensable need for the grace of God manifested through Jesus Christ and the effective spiritual guidance that comes through divine foreordination.48

Having noted that, it must also be stressed that the book of Revelation, along with much of apocalyptic literature, places full trust in the unerring foreknowledge of God and the power of what he has foreordained. So far as events are concerned, nothing is left to chance; all history moves according to what God has predesigned. A foundation stone of apocalyptic, then, is what God has determined beforehand will come to pass, and the book of Revelation never backs away from this viewpoint.

The Churchâs position rests in part on Paulâs statement that God âwill render to every man according to his deedsâ and that âthere is no respect of persons with Godâ (Rom. 2:6, 11). One of Godâs powers is âexpressed by the Greek words ÏÏÏÎ³Î½ÏÏÎ¹Ï (prognÅsis) and ÏÏÎ¿Î³Î¹Î½ÏÏÎºÏ (proginÅskÅ). The Book as an Extended Allegory Whose Images Are Symbolic Representations of Reality

One must keep in mind that Revelation is an extended allegory. In these, symbols are used to represent reality. Each image always explains and illuminates some aspect of its subject. For example, in chapter 17, Babylon is compared to a richly decked-out courtesan. The picture carries the idea of one who promotes lust for money and willingly uses others to her own advantage. But Babylon is itself a symbol. The ancient city was the center of extravagant wealth and also the seat of corruption and slavery. Thus, the depiction is an excellent symbol for those degenerate and depraved institutions, governments, businesses, and churches that exist in the last days, all of which, for wealth, fame, and/or power, misuse and corrupt those under their control or influence.

In Revelation, we meet one symbol after another, all begging to be interpreted. Sometimes the book itself identifies what the symbol means. For example, the great whore mentioned above sits âupon many watersâ (17:1), which John learns represents âpeoples, and multitudes, and nations, and tonguesâ (17:15). Thus, the imagery of âmany watersâ shows the extent of the great harlotâs reach.

What is helpful is that when a figure is defined in one place, that meaning can be applied when the image shows up elsewhere. For example, the image of the dragon is defined as Satan (12:9), so when that image shows up again (13:4; 20:2), it is safe to assume it still refers to the devil.

When the book does not give the meaning of an object or motif, it is best to use both the immediate and broad contexts in which the image is found to determine the pictureâs meaning. Then the probable identification made at its initial appearance can reasonably be applied to its later ones. For example, in chapter 13, we meet the beast from the sea, which most likely represents those human institutions and powers Satan has under his control and to which he gives assistance. When the beast shows up again in chapter 17, we can assume it denotes the same institutions.

Also very helpful in determining the meaning of a depiction are the commonplace associations coming mainly from the Old Testament but also from certain cultural elements of those who were Johnâs audience. Thus, the Hebrew and Greek scriptures, Jewish writings including apocryphal and pseudepigraphical materials, early Christian sources, and Greco-Roman items can all illuminate the text.

Another important point when trying to decipher the depictions in Johnâs work is that sometimes images can be easily pictured but sometimes they cannot. A number are composed of jarring descriptions and unfeasible combinations and doing impossible activities. For example, when John first sees the Savior in chapter 1, he notes that he has a sword continually issuing from his mouth (1:16). Historically, such a thing has never and will never happen. Further, the Seer describes the beast in 13:1 as having seven heads but ten horns. For easy visualization, there are either not enough or too many horns. The four living creatures met in 4:6 are located simultaneously at the corners of Godâs throne and at the exterior of his dais. Thus, they occupy two places at the same time. In all cases, it is important to remember that the images are not to be drawn or even imagined; they are, however, to be interpreted. The point is that Johnâs masterwork was never intended for the canvas and brush but for the mind. As with the images found in Ezekiel, his depictions are riddles demanding to be solved.57 Often these jarring and unreal images, along with others, bring in a dynamic and interactive quality meant to engage the mind of the thoughtful reader.

One last note. Each image can have multiple points of comparison and, therefore, meaning. The concern is, of course, not to be carried away by these but to use only those that best apply. Our objective in this commentary has been to get to the core meanings and associations but not to go beyond them. Also, it is important to note that not every detail described by John has deep significance. Some of the visionâs particulars reflect what John saw and round out the image giving vividness and power to his presentation. Flaming eyes, feet burning as brass, or various colored stones are all means by which John catches and holds the mindâs eye. Their individual meaning must never be taken out of the context of which they are a part.

The Use of Numbers in Revelation

Before leaving this section, a word must be said about numbers as they appear in Revelation. The literature of the whole ancient world, not just that of the Jews, reveals a fondness for using numbers to communicate ideas. Why certain numbers became laden with symbolic meaning is unknown; in most cases this arose in prehistoric times and is now lost to us. It is of interest that there is a general consistency across cultures to the meaning of certain numbers. That is not to say that numbers are never used as numbers. Indeed, they mostly signify literal quantity. However, in certain contexts, especially in apocalyptic literature, they take on a qualitative meaning and thus express ideas. This is the case in Revelation. One should interpret numbers along with the other symbolic representations. Fortunately, they are used consistently. For example, the numbers 4, 7, and 1,000 denote aspects of wholeness: the first to the world, the second to totality or completeness, and the last to superlative greatness and perfection.64 The various chapters in this commentary will draw out the meaning of the numbers in their various contexts.

The book of Revelation, like the rest of the New Testament, was written in Koine Greek, an evolved and simplified form of classical Attic Greek. It âtended toward shorter, simpler sentences,â and âsome of the syntactic subtleties [of classical Greek] were lost, or at least declined.â Although Koine did not have the precision and refinement of classical Greek, it replaced it with greater explicitness.71

There are some peculiarities in the Greek used in Revelation. Some scholars attribute these irregularities to Johnâs imperfect knowledge of Greek, and that may be so considering it was not his primary tongue. Making allowances, however, for the unusual nature of the apocalypse, some scholars have noted that the similarities between it and Johnâs Gospel are quite striking.72 In fact, most of the time John followed the rules of standard Hellenic Greek, indicating that he indeed had a good knowledge of the language. When these unusual forms do appear, it is almost always in the context of quotes or allusions to the Old Testament in its Greek translation, known as the Septuagint. Johnâs contemporary readers would have immediately recognized these allusions. We see a similarity in latter-day revelations. The Doctrine and Covenants is filled with both direct quotes and paraphrases from the Old and New Testaments.

Translation Philosophy and Methodology

As noted above, Koine Greek was the lingua franca of the Mediterranean world during the first century AD. It was a language that could express thoughts and ideas with plainness and clarity. However, because of the great differences in syntax, style, and grammar between ancient Greek and modern English, it is no easy task to produce a clear and understandable translation. An overly literal translation can easily lead to confusion and misunderstanding. While the King James Version of the Bible was a remarkable achievement for its time, there has been significant progress in our understanding of ancient Greek in the intervening four hundred years. Also, the King James Version used the Textus Receptus, a late sixteenth-century version of the Greek New Testament that was less accurate than the much earlier manuscripts used in modern critical editions of the New Testament today, such as The Nestle-Aland Greek New Testament73 and the Society for Biblical Literature Greek New Testament.74 Moreover, English itself has evolved and changed significantly in those four hundred years. For the above reasons, we felt that a modern English translation was an important and necessary part of this commentary. Each section of this commentary begins with the Greek text from the Society for Biblical Literatureâs Greek New Testament. This is followed by two parallel columns with the King James Version on the left and the Brigham Young University New Testament Rendition on the right. In our Rendition, we have tried to convey as clearly and simply as possible in modern English the sense of the ancient Greek text. When there is ambiguity in the Greek, for example, if a passage can be understood in two different ways, we have tried to preserve that ambiguity in English rather than force our own interpretation on it. For example, the first three words of the book of Revelation, á¼ÏÎ¿ÎºÎ¬Î»ÏÏÎ¹Ï á¼¸Î·ÏÎ¿á¿¦ Î§ÏÎ¹ÏÏÎ¿á¿¦ (apokalypsis iÄsou christou), are rendered âa revelation of Jesus Christ.â The genitive case of Jesus Christ is ambiguous. Is it an objective genitive (a revelation about Jesus Christ) or is it subjective (a revelation from Jesus Christ)? Our Rendition preserves that ambiguity. We have included copious notes and explanations so the reader can have a clear understanding of why we have translated a passage in a certain way. This is especially true when our Rendition differs from the KJV. Our goal has been to provide an English translation that is easy to read and understand while, at the same time, being as true as possible to the sense of the Greek text. We have, therefore, followed Bruce Metzgerâs dictum to be âas literal as possible, [but] as free as necessaryâ in order to communicate to the educated reader the meaning of the text.75

The Use of Revelation by LDS Church Leaders and Scholars

With a few exceptions, the leaders of the Church have referred to the book of Revelation very rarely. Even Joseph Smith did not use it often. During the nineteenth century, though, Presidents Brigham Young, John Taylor, and Wilford Woodruff and Apostles George Q. Cannon, Orson Hyde, and Charles W. During the twentieth century, a number of General Authorities cited passages from Revelation, but only two General Authorities, Joseph Fielding Smith and Bruce R. Elder McConkie was the first General Authority besides Orson Pratt to analyze Revelation in detail. Overall, it can be said that, until relatively recently, Revelation as a whole has generally not received detailed treatment by Church authorities and scholars. The Church in Asia Minor

One would think that the Saints would have been secure, but such was not the case. As John labored there, oppression struck. According to certain ancient sources, the Roman emperor Domitian developed a strong dislike for the Christians and, like the infamous Nero, moved against them, but on a much larger scale.164 Eusebius, the early church historian, states that in the fourteenth year of his reign (ca. It is not hard to see why the Saints had trouble in Asia. The province was very important to Rome. It was so rich in natural and human resources that many Roman nobles viewed it as a proconsular plum that guaranteed wealth to whomever the emperor appointed as its overseer. Due to its excellent natural resources, good roads that connected it to both the east and west, and excellent ports that gave it access to the broader world, its cities had become centers of manufacturing, especially textiles. By and large, its upper crust inhabitants were rich, sophisticated, and cultured.

Making the province even more important was its position as the guardian of the eastern frontier of the Empire. And being part of the Roman Empire greatly benefited the Asians. Roman attention and largess kept the area strong, pacified, and fortified. Thus, both Rome and Asia Minor profited from their close ties.166

Ephesus was, indeed, a good choice.168 With Pergamum and Smyrna, Ephesus stood as one of the three great cities of Asia Minor. Adding to its status was its position as the chief administrative headquarters of the area whose governor directed all the affairs of the province.169 The city was a center for the imperial cult, but its citizens were most proud of the large and beautiful temple dedicated to the Roman goddess Diana, or Artemis, as the Greeks knew her. Emperor Worship in Asia Minor

It took the people of Asia Minor little time to realize that monetary success was tied to joining the imperial religious bureaucracy. The Role of Guilds and Trade Unions

There is little doubt that guilds and trade unions were both ubiquitous and strong in this region. The area was very rich in natural resources that supported a strong economy, much of it based on textiles. The area boasted many producers and exporters. Wool and flax production abounded and there were numerous carding and weaving facilities in places like Laodicea and Colossae. The usual practice at guild meetings was to offer up incense or libations to their patron deity and to the emperor. The act showed not only religious faith but also honored civic duty. For Greco-Roman citizens, anyone who refused these simple, holy, and honorific acts not only dishonored the state but could also bring down divine disfavor.

While John labored in the province of Asia, conditions worsened for the Saints. John on the Isle of Patmos

Patmos, one of the Sporades Islands, lies about forty miles southwest of Ephesus; at the time, it was part of the territory of the city of Miletus. It is a small volcanic island, roughly butterfly-shaped, about ten miles long by six miles wide. Contrary to what some have said, it was not a Roman penal colony and there is no evidence anyone other than John was ever banished there.182 The Miletians used this island, along with two others, as a naval station housing a garrison to ward off pirates. That John was there opens the possibility that certain Roman officials may have viewed the social status of the Christian leadership as higher than previously believed. This possibility is best understood in light of the Roman practice of banishment.

Roman Forms of Banishment

The ancient sources all agree that John was exiled to Patmos. However, the Latin words exilium, or exsul, referred to a person leaving an area on either a voluntary or an involuntary basis; therefore, they do not tell us if John was banished to Patmos or went there of his own accord. It is noteworthy that the Roman legal system had a dual-penalty system that depended more on the social status of the individual than on the actual crime he committed. Those with proper dignitas, that is, rank or station, received more lenient sentences than those of lesser station. For example, a crime requiring the death penalty for a lower-class individual mandated merely banishment for a member of the upper class.183 Therefore, those with sufficient dignitas could often escape the death penalty even for capital crimes. They did not, however, get off scot-free. If the crime was of sufficient severity, even nobles were banished.

The law allowed for two types of compulsory banishment: relegatio and deportatio. The former was often temporary (perhaps lasting as little as three years) and did not necessarily include the forfeiture of citizenship or property. This took two forms: relegatio ad (also referred to as relegatio in) which was banishment to a place, often to an island or penal colony; or relegatio ab (also referred to as relegatio extra) which was restriction from a place, allowing a person to travel or live anywhere but in a certain province or area. Deportatio was much more onerous, designating a long-term banishment to a specific place along with loss of citizenship and property. Nobles often chose this sentence in lieu of death.184

Taking the facts of banishment into account, there are six possibilities that may explain how John came to Patmos. First, he may have decided to go to the island to preach the gospel to members of the garrison stationed there. Second, he may have gone under inspiration to have time to meditate in the seclusion necessary for the revelation. Third, a magistrate may have relegated him from the province (relegatio ab), and John went to the island voluntarily for refuge. Fourth, a magistrate may have banished him specifically to that island (relegatio ad) for a predetermined duration of time. Fifth, a magistrate may have relegated him to the island as a permanent exile with all loss of citizenship and any property (the extreme form of relegatio ad). Sixth and finally, a magistrate may have condemned him to death and, to avoid execution, he chose exile to the island (deportatio).

Johnâs own testimony is quite helpful in understanding why he was there. He states he âwas in the island that is called Patmos, for the word of God, and for the testimony of Jesusâ (1:9). Some have suggested, on the basis of the clause Î´Î¹á½° Ïá½¸Î½ Î»ÏÎ³Î¿Î½ ÏÎ¿á¿¦ Î¸ÎµÎ¿á¿¦ (dia ton logon tou theou), âfor the word of God,â that John expressed the purpose of his visit. The word translated âforâ is the Greek preposition dia. Some have suggested the phrase could be interpreted in a subjective or objective sense, that is, he was there because of the word from or about God and the testimony by or to Jesus. They conclude that he was there âin order to preach the word of God.â185 The problem is that the preposition does not govern the genitive case that would allow this interpretation. Instead it governs the accusative case, which connotes âcause or consequences.â Thus, he was at Patmos in consequence of refusal to deny his testimony of the Lord.186 This interpretation rules out possibilities one and twoâthat is, that he had gone there of his own accord for either meditative or proselyting purposes.

That opens the way for the third possibility, relegatio ad (which allowed for temporary banishment), because John clearly states that when he received the revelation, he âwas in the isle called Patmos.â Note the past tense that suggests that at the time he wrote he was no longer there but had been freed. However, a close examination of the passage suggests that this is not the case either. The aorist verb á¼Î³ÎµÎ½ÏÎ¼Î·Î½ (egenomÄn), translated âI was,â does not necessarily mean that John was no longer on Patmos. This tense is Johnâs choice as he presents background material setting up a current circumstance.187 Thus, his use of the tense does not necessarily mean he was no longer on the island, but rather he had been and perhaps still was residing there when the vision opened. If that were the case, he most likely would not have been suffering under relegatio ab since he would not yet have been released and would not seem to know if he would beâhence his need to write to the churches rather than visit them. If that is the case, it is also unlikely that John was suffering under relegatio ad (reason four) in which a magistrate banished him for a specific duration of time.

Johnâs words to the Saints that he was their âcompanion in tribulation, and in the kingdom and patience of Jesus Christâ (1:9) suggests that he had come to the island under harsh circumstancesâthat he had actually been banished there.188 That opens reasons five and six. Some object to either of these possibilities because they suggest that John belonged to the upper strata of Greco-Roman society. They insist that his being a Christian would preclude him from membership in any upper class anywhere in the Empire. Greco-Romans would simply not see him as having dignitas, and he therefore would not qualify for the special justice that nobles merited.189 The records, however, do show that some Christians did indeed belong to the elite strata. One piece of evidence is that a number of Christians were executed by beheading, a death reserved for upper-class citizens.190 Further, many Christians honored their leaders, whom they recognized as possessing very high dignitas. That some of those who did the honoring were themselves members of the upper class reinforced their religious leadersâ social rank. Some Roman governors may have observed this and acted accordingly. That seems to have been the case with John. If so, he was on Patmos either under the severest from of relegatio ad or under deportatio. Because he was a Christian leader, and being a Christian was a capital offense under Roman law, it is most likely he was on the island for reason sixâhe had chosen exile (deportatio, in this case) over death.191

But why Patmos, since it was not a penal colony? Provincial governors did not have the power to banish individuals to an island unless the island lay within the geographical bounds of their area.192 Though Patmos was not a penal colony, it was within the geographical jurisdiction of the governor of Asia and, therefore, a place to which he could exile a pesky Christian with sufficient dignitas to choose exile over execution in the dual system of punishment the Empire allowed.193

John was eventually released due to a Roman practice that allowed one emperor to give amnesty to the prisoners of his predecessor. According to Eusebius, such was the case with John. Upon Domitianâs death, the Roman Senate recalled all the dead emperorâs exiles, which freed John to return to Ephesus and continue his ministry.194

The Impetus to the Visions

Johnâs visions, however, seem to have come years before his release. The question is, what triggered the revelation? John states that he was âin the Spirit on the Lordâs dayâ when he heard âa great voice, as of a trumpetâ commanding him to write what the Lord was about to tell him and send it to the seven churches in the province of Asia. From the point of view of the text, it was God who precipitated the prophecy, not anything that directly happened to John. In short, the Seer was not seeking answers to which the revelation was a response. Rather, the vision was a result of his being in the spirit and thus prepared for what the Lord was about to show him.

Even so, there must have been circumstances that were either already threatening the Church or soon would be, which caused the Lord sufficient concern to send this forceful message. Many have argued that the impetus was Domitianâs persecutions and the threat these posed on the survival of the Church in Asia Minor. As noted previously, it has been the opinion of many scholars that, though Domitian did pressure people all over the empire to worship him, it was not nearly as forceful as early Christian sources have suggested.195 That would mean that the Christians were not under unusual pressure from that quarter. Even if Domitian did not launch an empire-wide pogrom, as some ancient sources insist he did, officials in those places where emperor worship was particularly strong, such as the province of Asia, would have moved forcefully against any who refused to fall in with their sycophancy and its expected rewards. For example, the letter to Smyrna shows the Christians there suffered dire poverty. Likely this came about because devout Saints found it difficult to make a living in this thoroughly pagan city. They could not participate in any jobs that were tied to the pagan temples and idolatry, work in any trades that were immoral, or join the guilds that required emperor worship. Further, hostile Gentiles and Jews probably boycotted their businesses and shops.196 The result was tribulation (2:9, 10, 22), the Greek Î¸Î»Î¯ÏÎ¹Ï (thlipsis, from the word Î¸Î»Î¯Î²Ï, thlibÅ), meaning âto press, compress, oppress, or afflict.â197 The Lordâs word choice suggests the deliberate and malicious persecution of the Saints.

The Position of the Christians

On the surface, it seems that the Saints could have escaped easily. All they had to do was offer a pinch of incense at the emperorâs shrine and mutter the empty formula âCaesar is Lord.â But, as noted, the faithful could not do that because they had but one Lord, and that was Christ. Those at Pergamum knew the cost of not conforming, for they had already lost the first co-religionist of many to martyrdom.198 Antipas, very likely the local leader, appears to have been publicly executed. Nothing is known of his life, but his enduring faith and refusal to bow to pagan pressures earned him a title that the Lord also held: âthe faithful witnessâ (compare 2:13 with 1:5).199

Even so, in Johnâs letters to the seven churches there is not a single hint that the major problem was with the imperial cult or those who administered it. The record does reveal such developments as Jewish harassment, local intolerance (especially on the guild level), and the influence of general pagan corruption, but no persecution specifically coming from the imperial cult.200 Still, something triggered the revelation. What was it?

Simply put, the Church itself was foundering. A look at the text suggests that the Church was foundering in six areas, only one of which was the outside pressure coming from persecution. The other socially related pressure was a willingness to compromise with paganismâincluding, on the part of some members, paying homage to the emperor. The other four factors were internal: the acceptance of unauthorized leaders, approval of doctrine that false prophets and apostles promulgated, halfheartedness and indifference, and the loss of love for the Church and its Master.

The Lordâs Message

Under these conditions, the Lordâs messages of warning to the seven churches were very necessary. In spite of the success of missionary work in the region, trouble brewed, because the area had become a hotbed of heresy. During his three-year ministry there, Paul had continually warned the Saints that after he left âshall grievous wolves enter in among you, not sparing the flock. Also of your own selves shall men arise, speaking perverse things, to draw away disciples after themâ (Acts 20:28â31). His words show that danger would come from two sources: the first would be âthe wolves,â those who joined the Church as a means of taking advantage of the Christians; the second would be the insiders, Christian leaders who would speak âperverse thingsâ and use their positions to persuade congregations to follow another gospel, leading many astray.201

Paulâs dire predictions proved true. It took only a few years for apostasy to spread over the area like a destructive flood. Thus he could say to Timothy in about AD 64, âAll they which are in [the province of] Asia be turned away from meâ (2 Tim. 1:15).

The apostasy began as false apostles and pseudoprophets breached the doctrinal levees that the Lordâs teachings set up and his appointed leaders had spent their lives protecting and promulgating.202 Entire congregations were ignorantly or willfully in danger of being swept away in the flood of falsehood that the illegitmate prophets and others promulgated. The messages to the seven churches show that the Savior was well aware of what was happening and that he was reaching out to save those islands where hope still remained. He gave each congregation specific and clear warnings. The most important warning was that he would abandon the churches unless they returned to him. He clearly told each congregation to stop the spread of heresy, repent, hold to the truth, and thereby gain salvationâor else he would remove the Church from among them.

The Seat of the Christian Crisis

Thus, it is clear that the crisis that triggered the revelation to John was less the social situation in which the Christians found themselves than the conflict within the Christian faith itself.203 There is no doubt that many Christians felt distressed because of their social situation. The kingdom of God was supposed to be on the earth and triumph over all its enemies, yet the reality was that Rome had the upper hand and seemed in position to destroy the divine kingdom. The revelation came to relieve the tension. Through the power of its symbolism, the various visions corrected the Christiansâ false expectations and gave them a clear vision of the present reality, a reality they would have to endure. Indeed, the Lord showed them that

all living beings are given a place in a dualistic structure. At the pinnacle of power on one side is God, the Pantocrator, ruler of all (1:8). On the other is Satan, the Dragon, who has power, a throne, and great authority (13:2). Allied with God is the Lamb who was slain (5:6). . . . Allied with Satan is the beast from the sea (13:1â2). . . . All the people of the earth are divided into two groups; those who have the seal of God on their foreheads and whose names are in the book of life (3:5, 12; 7:3; 20:4; 21:27; 22:4) and those who bear the mark of the beast and worship it (9:4; 13:8, 17; 14:9â11; 16:2; 20:15).204

In short, âthis literary tension reflects the political tension between the adherents of the kingdom of God and those of the kingdom of Caesar.â205 The problem centered on how to resolve the real-life tension. Some Christians wanted to do it by making compromises with âBabylon.â As noted, living in a city dominated by trade guilds worked to the disadvantage of Christians. Each guild had its patron deity, whom members worshiped at the guild meetings. A Christian laborer had two choices, neither good. On one hand, he could hold to his standards, refuse to join the guild, and thereby lose opportunity for employment, which resulted in isolation and poverty; on the other hand, he could compromise his standards and enjoy the fruits of membership. It appears that some Christians were not only giving in to this economic and social pressure but promoting their strategy as an advantageous solution to the problem. One was a woman whom John calls by the epithet âJezebel.â It appears that she developed a theology that appealed to those who opted for the compromises that let them escape persecution and enjoy the prosperity of the city. Her logic could even have cited Paulâs writings, because he taught that an idol was nothing, and therefore that eating sacrificial meat really posed no problem for believers (see 1 Cor. 8:4â6). Paul, however, had counseled against the practice because it opened the door to apostasy (1 Cor. 8:10â13). Jezebel seems to have ignored the Apostleâs constraints and openly advocated compromise for the good of security and prosperity. In doing so, she also led the way to spiritual death and the destruction of the Church.206

It is important, however, to resist the idea that Revelation reflects only the tension between Johnâs church and Rome, between the forces of apostasy active in his day and the ideals of the true Church. In short, it is far too shortsighted to see Revelation and its message as bound exclusively to the historical limits of Johnâs day; doing so dilutes the message of the visions. The reality was that they revealed the extended sweep of history and testified to the ultimate triumph of the Christian community at the end-time. The faithful of Johnâs day played a key role in laying down the foundation on which the rest of the drama would be played. Though tension with Rome is important in Revelation, the empire was but one physical manifestation of Satanâs kingdom on earthâthe kingdom that poses the real threat to the Church in any age. In Johnâs day, the pagan and uncompromising Roman magistrates were the executors of Satanâs kingdom. Thus, Revelation shows that a battleâor more precisely, a campaign, because the time was filled with many battlesâwas raging on two planes: the physical and the spiritual.

Through the message of Revelation, we see the Lord advising the Saints to view their day in a broader perspective. They should maintain the same attitude as Paul, who knew that the Christians âwrestle not against flesh and blood, but against principalities, against powers, against the rulers of the darkness of this world, against spiritual wickedness in high placesâ (Eph. 6:12). By keeping this perspective, they could understand the cosmic proportions of their struggle, making their current distress take on cosmic meaning and thereby become more bearable.

Summary

The major crises the Church faced, and the impetus to the revelation, was the apostasy brought on by yielding not just to Roman pressures but also to unholy spiritual forces. The real threat against which John wrote was neither the imperial cult nor the military and administrative might of Rome, although they were in the background. It was the overpowering forces of apostasy that prompted the revelation.207

The situation was critical. A false-prophetic circle arose in competition with the true prophets for ecclesiastical domination and theological acceptance. False apostles infiltrated the Church at Ephesus (2:2); at Pergamum a faction upheld what John called the âdoctrine of Balaamâ (2:14), probably a move to incorporate into church practice certain elements of the pagan religious system; while at Thyatira a false prophetess with a large number of followers seduced many with her teachings (2:20). Heresy was spreading everywhere.208 At this critical juncture, the Lord, through John, reached out in warning to the seven churches. The letters show the Lordâs mercy because, whatever happened to the several branches, the Lord promised faithful individuals eternal life since they had ears to hear.

Moreover, the letters also warn Saints in every age that Babylon (whether the old city of the Euphrates, Rome of the Tiber, or any of its modern counterparts) is the real enemy. John shows that compromise is a deadly sin. The Saints must not worship the dragon and his beast. They must not worship the great whore with all her glittering, alluring materialism or seductive immorality. The Saints must direct their loyalty exclusively to God and the Lamb, the only source of the sealing power and gift of eternal life. Trying to serve both will turn oneâs desires to dust. John himself remained faithful to his own Lord and Master, willing to suffer deportation to Patmos rather than compromise his testimony.

Though it is true that Patmos was not a formal penal colony, the right of a Roman magistrate to banish criminals to an island within his jurisdiction suggests John was, indeed, banished there as a penalty for being a Christian leader. As one authority has noted, it is likely âthat Johnâs presence on Patmos was the result of a capital penalty inflicted on him by Roman authorities.â209

That a Roman official banished him, rather than order his execution, suggests that the Christian leaders had gained a certain amount of dignitas in some provinces, and, therefore, could qualify for banishment rather than death. Such a condition suggests that the Christian movement, though many viewed it as a threat, was gaining a certain amount of cachet. Though the force of Rome stood against it, the Lordâs kingdom was demandingâand gainingâever more respect. Of course, some would view a more powerful Christianity as an even greater threat and move more fiercely against it. Even so, no earthly power could stop the movement.

Though a Roman magistrate may have hoped that banishing John to Patmos would weaken his influence and perhaps destroy the Christian faith, such was not the case. The island instead provided John with time to meditate and to reach out to his God. As a result, visions cameâand with them direction for the Church.

Some have claimed that John intended nothing more than keeping the Saints in line by holding out the promise of eternal reward.210 But Johnâs aim seems far more noble. It is true that the course of history he presents stresses the ultimate rather than the immediate triumph of goodness over evil, but he also urges the Saints to work out their salvation in the present and to place their trust in God, who was not yet ready to expose his mastery over history. Triumph was not immediately at hand. The Saints had to exercise faith through a hope that made no demands upon the present. They could not escape history; the work of God would continue for a long period within its confines. Even today, a characteristic of faith is a willingness to allow God to operate in his own way in the present, to take what comes, and to continue strong. God, through his Saints, will achieve ultimate victory. Johnâs readers had to accept the agony of the present as travail necessary to bring into being a glorious future.211

Even so, as one LDS scholar has noted, âThe Lordâs message, which John delivers, is primarily one of grace and peace, not of persecution and deathâgrace, which is made possible through the atoning sacrifice; and inner peace, which comes from a knowledge of oneâs relationship with God. It is through these that the Lord offers comfort and hope to his people.â212

Conclusion

Of John, the Savior said, âI will make him as flaming fire and a ministering angel; he shall minister for those who shall be heirs of salvation who dwell on the earthâ (D&C 7:6). Surely in writing Revelation, John partially fulfilled the Lordâs intent. He wrote a message of testimony and hope to those âheirs of salvationâ who lingered in his own day, but he also wrote for the âheirs of salvationâ living in the latter days. For those who understand, the flaming fire of his testimony still lights the way to the Lord and Savior of the world.

In writing this volume, it is our hope and intent to make his witness clear and compelling to the Saints of these last days. Our task has been to bring into the text those additional scriptures which give us an even better picture of the last days and the direction they will take. Knowing the Lordâs promise to the faithful that âunto you it shall be given to know the signs of the times, and the signs of the coming of the Son of Manâ (D&C 68:11), it is our hope that this work will be part of that disclosure and bring hope to all who read it.

The key to what the Saintsâ present attitude should be was well expressed by Elder Neal A. Maxwell, who said,

The informed chiliast [a person who anticipates the coming Millennium], which every Latter-day Saint should be, is one who firmly believes in the literal second coming of Christ, which will usher in a millennial reign, but who can meet the special challenges involved in both noticing the leaves [of the fig tree, see JSâM 1:38] and endure the heat of summer that follows. One is urged to notice, lest one be caught unawares. We may note merely two impressive indicators thus far: has any age seen more âsigns and wondersâ in the heavens than ours already has, with jet aircraft, manned and other satellites, and even journeys to the moon? Has any generation seen such ominous âvapors of smokeâ as ours with mushroom clouds over the pathetic pyres of Hiroshima and Nagasaki? (See D&C 45:41; Joel 2:30â31; Matthew 24:24; Acts 2:19). But, alas, even these may fall short of further fulfillments.

Yet a careful observer must also be aware of how many false readings there have been in bygone times as well as contemporary days, even by some of the faithful.

Our challenge in the days ahead, therefore, is one of balance, to notice the early warnings without over reacting and to move forward without slipping into the dulled heedlessness of the days of Noah (see Matthew 24:38â39). In those far distant days some doubtlessly jeered, or at least were privately amused, at the sight of Noah building the ark. Presumably, local mockery continued even when it began to rain torrentially and kept raining. But soon Noahâs ark was the only safe and sane thing in a bewildering and desperate situation.

Thus we are to ponder signs without becoming paranoid, and be aware without frantically keeping score between current events and scriptural expectations. The wise chiliast will use his or her energy in serving man instead of fretting and will resist secularismâs heavy seduction.213
