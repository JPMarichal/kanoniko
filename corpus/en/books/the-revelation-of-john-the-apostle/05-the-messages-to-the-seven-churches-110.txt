Background

Chapters 2 and 3 contain the Lordâs messages to each of seven churches located in the Roman province of Asia. This section will, therefore, act as an introduction to both.

It seems unlikely that the Lord addressed exactly seven churches, as noted in the preceding chapter, because of the symbolic nature of the number. Qualitatively it suggests that the revelation was given by Godâs divine power and carried its weight. Further, the number carried a universal nuance connoting wholeness or completeness. The message to the specific churches the Lord addressed was actually for all because the same general problems were faced by all. Thus each letter tells the readers to hear âwhat the Spirit is saying to the churchesââall of the churches.1

The warning was very necessary. In spite of the success of missionary work in the region, trouble brewed. Over time, the area had become a hotbed of heresy. During his three-year ministry there, Paul had continually warned the Saints that after he left âshall grievous wolves enter in among you, not sparing the flock. Also of your own selves shall men arise, speaking perverse things, to draw away disciples after themâ (Acts 20:28â31). His words show that troubles would come from two sources. The first would be the âwolves,â those who would join the Church as a means of taking advantage of the Christians. The other would be insiders, Christian leaders speaking âperverse things,â who would use their positions to persuade congregations to follow another gospel and, in the process, lead many astray.2

Paul was right. It took only a few years for apostasy to spread over the area like a destructive flood. Thus he could say to Timothy about AD 64, âAll they which are in Asia be turned away from meâ (2 Tim. 1:15). The fall began as false apostles and pseudoprophets, such as Phygellus and Hermogenes, breached the levies once secured by the Lordâs doctrine and his appointed leaders. Entire congregations were ignorantly or willfully in danger of being swept along in the flood of falsehood these false leaders promulgated. The message to the units showed that the Savior was well aware of what was happening and was reaching out to save those islands where hope still remained. He gave each congregation specific and clear warnings. The most important one was that he would abandon the churches unless they returned to him. He clearly told each congregation to stop the spread of heresy in its area, repent, hold to the truth, and thereby gain salvation or he would remove them.

The letters follow a general format, consisting of six parts, that gives balance and symmetry to the whole. The first is a divine commission composed of a clear statement that the message was from the Savior. Though he never uses his name, he does imply a number of descriptive titles, mostly drawn from chapter 1. Each points to some aspect of his work and ministry and serves to unite the message as a whole. They also give a description of the speaker that highlights an important aspect of his character or attributes and ties his message to the situation faced by the particular congregation.3 The second is a formal recognition composed of a complimentary opening introduced by the phrase âI know thy works.â This section looks to the past and present condition in each of the congregations and sets out a diagnosis. The third is a criticism or rebuke beginning with âI have something against thee.â Here the Lord identifies the major problem faced by each congregation. The fourth is an admonition designed to meet the particular challenge faced by that congregation. The fifth is a call to hear, designed to motivate the receptive to continue faithful. And the last is a promised blessing introduced by the words âto him that overcometh.â4 The promise gives hope for divine reward to each individual who stands faithful. The exceptions to this format are Smyrna (2:8â11) and Philadelphia (3:7â13), which receive no condemnation, and Sardis (3:1â6) and Laodicea (3:14â18), which receive no compliment.

Looking at the relationship of the letters, we see they form a chiasm composed of three groups: a, b, c, c, c, b, a. The first group comprises the first and last churchesâEphesus and Laodiceaâwhich were getting dangerously close to losing their identities as Christian churches. Salvation could come only if they repented and fully returned to their Lord. The second groupâSmyrna and Philadelphiaâwere the strongest, but even they were âpoorâ and had little power. The Lord could promise them only more trials and ask them to endure in faith with the promise of eternal salvation. The portion of the series dealing with promises and blessings is of particular interest. As we have noted elsewhere, âeach of the seven promises and blessings begins with the anaphoric expression to him that overcometh, and each features one or more temple images directed to those who do overcome. The Lordâs specific counsel also showed that he was not a distant, impersonal God, but an immediate and personal Savior who cared for both congregation and individual. Analysis and Summary

The luxurious city of Pergamum was the seat of Roman government in the province of Asia. Further, it was a center for Roman pagan practices. The temple to the deified Augustus and the elaborate altar to Zeus Soter, to name just two of the many shrines found in the city, give evidence of the cityâs devotion to its deities and Roman rulers. Sincere worshippers paid homage to their patron gods to keep or gain their favor. Even the less believing would have seen the advantages of adopting Roman gods, especially the deified emperor, as a way of ingratiating themselves to the major power of the day. By the turn of the century, all citizens and subjects were obligated by law to make offerings to the imperial god, the divine emperor; otherwise, they were condemned to death as traitors and enemies of the state. Both the devout and less devout were threatened by those forces which worked against the traditional gods. The Christians were a major force that threatened the status quo. The governorâs court was in Pergamum and to it the Christians were brought for trial. Here, it seems, they faced the âseat of Satan,â and here Caesar and Christ contended as in few other places.

The Lord assured these Saints that he knew âwhere thou dwellest.â Unfortunately, it was âwhere Satan dwellethâ (2:13). This fact accentuates the reality that where light and darkness dwell, there can be no peaceful coexistence. As a result, those who held fast to their testimony of the Lordâand that meant declaring it openly even before magistratesâcould expect the wrath of Satanâs agents to be brought against them.118

Among both Jews and Gentiles, most sacrifices did not require burning the entire animal (Lev. 1â5).119 Often the priests and participants received a portion, which they used in a sacramental meal that tied them ritually to their gods. Many held that the practice also brought the power or influence of the god or goddess on the participant.120 Paul was perfectly aware that the idea was false because idols, having no existence, could not extend any kind of spiritual advantage to the worshipper.121 Even so, Paul feared that certain Christians may not understand this principle and, seeing established members partaking of the meat, feel it was empowering, and thereby drift back into paganism. Paul therefore recommended that all abstain from the practice (see 1 Cor. 8:4â10).

There are four main theories as to what eating sacrificial meat meant: first, participating in actual pagan sacramental temple rites; second, accepting meat once offered to idols but given to participants during pagan celebrations; third, purchasing excess sacrificial meat from the temple meat market; and fourth, participating in sacral meals of clubs, guilds, or other associations.122 Of these, it is unlikely that the heretics were advocating something as blatant as participating in actual pagan temple rituals. More likely they were supplying a justification for the Christians to participate in the public festivals and then purchase sacrificial meats (like Latter-day Saints today justifying themselves in going to a sports bar because they do not have cable). These places, however, were immoral by any standard and therefore not conducive to the Holy Spirit. Because such was forbidden by apostolic authority, these heretics actually challenged the very seat of power of the Church.123

The Lordâs prohibition shows a close connection between eating sacrificial meat and immorality. Compromising on the former may have more easily lead people into the latter. Though throughout the Old Testament, the prophets used immorality as a symbol for idolatry (see, for example, Jer. 3:2; 13:27; Ezek. 16:15â58; 23:1â49; Hosea 5:4; 6:10), it is very likely that the Lord was prohibiting actual immoral acts. Some aspects of pagan worship were highly immoral and some cities had high levels of sacral prostitution. Those who propounded the doctrine of Balaam may have justified certain immoral acts.124

It is possible that the appeal of the Nicolaitans and those who followed the doctrine of Balaam was their promise of relief from persecution. Here we see the difference between the churches at Ephesus and at Pergamum. The former, with its overemphasis on complete doctrinal piety, had lost concern for the outside world, loving neither it nor those touched by it. At Pergamum the opposite was true. Here the Saints deemphasized doctrine, resulting in an overidentification with the world. Though they had withstood external pressure by Roman authorities, subtle internal pressures were leading the Saints to make dangerous compromises.125 It appears that the appeal of the doctrine of the Nicolaitans was that it offered a convincing rationale for compromising with paganism that would deflect its concerns (see below). The problem was that these compromises opened the door to practices that the Lord found repulsive because they broke both the spirit and letter of his law. Very likely the extreme cases actually led to sexual immorality but provided a salve for the conscience, thus thwarting recognition of the wrong and, therefore, repentance. Thus the Lord warned the congregation to resist these evils and repent or face his withering denialâthe sword of his mouthâat their divine judgment.

Once again the Lord did not make individual salvation contingent on that of the Church itself. Each person who heard and responded was promised the reward, including access to the new name or key word so necessary to gain eternal life. As Brigham Young told the Saints, certain ordinances âare necessary for you, after you have departed this life, to enable you to walk back to the presence of the Father, passing the angels who stand as sentinels, being enabled to give them the key words, the signs and tokens, pertaining to the Holy Priesthood, and gain your eternal exaltation.â126 Receiving the new name was the reward for resisting the world.

The idea of a new name is significant. Names in ancient societies identified not only the person but also his or her status; to be given a new name by another was to enter into a personal, even intimate, relationship that brought a higher status. Patronymics indicated that a person was a free man or woman and even of nobility. As such, he or she was in a position to inherit the family fortune. In the gospel sense, having the right name indicated one was an heir to eternal life.

That the believers will be given a white stone on which the new name is written does not mean the individual did not know the name before, only that it was now made permanent. Indeed, the Christian already had one new name, that of Christ himself, and receiving it brought consummate identification with the Lord and the promise of the final reward. Therefore it was all-important that the faithful stay true to the name or the reward could be lost.127

Taking the name of Christ did more; it brought them under Godâs protective power, one that did not guarantee protection from physical death, but rather from spiritual. That, coupled with the reception of the new name, signified that one was a member of the eternal community of the redeemed.128

The modern city of Ahisar sits on top of the ruins of ancient Thyatira. The New Testament city, located about thirty-five miles from the sea, sat along the banks of the Lycus River between the larger and more bustling cities of Pergamum and Sardis and was governed by the former. Analysis and Summary

Few Christians lived in Thyatira. There seems to have been just a scattered group meeting in house churches here and there in the city and therefore of little interest to the authorities. It may be for that reason that they were not persecuted like their spiritual brothers and sisters in Smyrna and Pergamum. In spite of their small numbers, the faithful Saints practiced their religion, showing forth loving service and winning the praise of the Lord. They were, however, in danger. The branch was not free from the waves of heresy that pounded on the footings of the Church in general, but they had additional challenges. Living in a city dominated by trade guilds worked to their disadvantage. Each guild had its patron deity that members worshipped at the guild meetings. Christian laborers had one of two choices, neither good. On the one hand, they could hold to their standards and refuse to join the guild, thereby losing opportunity for employment and resulting in isolation and poverty. On the other hand, they could compromise their standards, join the guild, and enjoy the community and prosperity but bring upon himself the ire of the Christian leaders and the Lord himself. It appears that some were giving in to some kind of compromise.

This willingness to compromise may have been fostered by the Nicolaitans. But the problem was compounded in Thyatira due to the seductive work of a very able and charismatic woman characterized as Jezebel. Unlike Judaism and many pagan religions, the Christians expanded the role that women played in the Church. Paulâs letters show that they prayed, taught, bore testimony, and ministered among the Saints (see Rom. 16:1â7; 1 Cor. 11:2â5; Philip. 4:3). Since most Christians, even toward the end of the first century, continued to meet in house churchesâthe larger homes of the more wealthy membersâthe women of those households had special status as the patroness (ÏÏÎ¿ÏÏÎ¬ÏÎ¹Ï, prostatis) of the congregation.153 This position, along with the Churchâs more liberal policy toward women, opened the door to misconduct. Certain overzealous women years before had pushed the limits of this policy, earning a very strong reprimand from Paul (see 1 Cor. 14:34â35). It would appear that Jezebel went beyond even these women, claiming she was the interpreter of Godâs will for the community.154

Her appeal seems to have come from her theology, which justified compromise with pagan religions and allowed the Saints to escape from persecution and enjoy the prosperity of the city. Her logic could have appealed to the writings of Paul, who taught that an idol was nothing and therefore eating sacrificial meat was really no problem (see 1 Cor. 8:4â6). Paul, however, had counseled against it because it opened the door to apostasy (1 Cor. 8:10â13). Jezebel seems to have ignored any constraints and openly advocated compromise for the good of security and prosperity. In this way she also led the way from spiritual fornication to physical.155

But as her epithet shows, she was guilty of more than false teachings. She seems to have been persecuting those who refused her seductions, including the Church leadership. The Lordâs words that he had given her âtime to repentâ suggest she had been active for some time, all the while refusing to heed counsel. If Jezebelâs teachings and the work of the Nicolaitans were not enough of a challenge for the Saints, it seems that certain heretics were claiming to possess the mysteries of godliness. With their false teachings, they brought a number to their standard. So disgusted was the Lord with this counterfeit worship that he branded it as âthe depths of Satan.â

The main task of the few faithful members was to hold on to the original gospel and works of charity. It is in this light that Paulâs statement âthat if thou shalt confess with thy mouth the Lord Jesus, and shalt believe in thine heart that God hath raised him from the dead, thou shalt be savedâ (Rom. 10:9) needs to be understood. Background

Chapters 2 and 3 contain the Lordâs messages to each of seven churches located in the Roman province of Asia. This section will, therefore, act as an introduction to both.

It seems unlikely that the Lord addressed exactly seven churches, as noted in the preceding chapter, because of the symbolic nature of the number. The warning was very necessary. In spite of the success of missionary work in the region, trouble brewed. Over time, the area had become a hotbed of heresy. Analysis and Summary

The Church in Ephesus, at the time John wrote, had kept itself free from the heresies that afflicted other congregations. One reason was that its leader was willing to put new doctrinal concepts to the test and, for those that failed, dismiss both the doctrine and its adherents. Further, the congregationâs members themselves showed a deep and abiding dislike for any such teachings. That they could distinguish them shows that at this early date, contrary to some modern thinking, the Church clearly understood the nature of the Lord, his mission, and doctrine.48 The Lord praised them highly for their stand.

The text shows that spiritual libertines, the Nicolaitans, were working in a number of the branches of the Church in Asia Minor. They seem to have set up a prophetic tradition that stood opposite to that of John and those associated with him. They refused to recognize his authority or that of the other authorized representatives of the Lord. Elder Bruce R. McConkie notes that these were

members of the Church who were trying to maintain their church standing while continuing to live after the manner of the world. They must have had some specific doctrinal teachings which they used to justify their course. . . . Whatever their particular deeds and doctrines were, the designation [Nicolaitans] has come to be used to identify those who want their names on the records of the Church, but do not want to devote themselves to the gospel cause with full purpose of heart. Thus, on July 8, 1838, the Lord said, âLet my servant Newel K. Whitney be ashamed of the Nicolaitane band and of all their secret abominations.â49

Many members in other congregations had been persuaded by the new seduction and not only left the truth but fostered the evil.50 The Ephesians resisted this hated heresy and won the Lordâs plaudits. Even so, they were on the brink of apostasy but, as stated, of another more subtle yet still deadly kind.

Because the congregation was on the brink of apostasy, it was necessary that they heed the Lordâs warning âto hear.â The admonition echoes Isaiah 6 and ties the Saviorâs warning and attendant command to idolatry. The words look back to the apostate Israelites who, like their blind and deaf idols, could no longer see or hear. So here the Church in Ephesus was on the brink of becoming, like the pagan idols, spiritually lifeless. That the Lordâs worry was fully justified and the warning was needed can be seen from the fact that some branches, as will be shown below, had already capitulated.51

Though the branch had been successful in warding off apostasy from the front door, defection was sneaking in through the back. It did not take the form of heresy but of loss of purpose. The communityâs determination to guard and perpetuate the truth seems to have produced a rigid and cold orthodoxy that left little or no room for Christlike compassion and love.52 Their devotion to legitimate truth was more determined than their love for the Lord. The Savior had told his followers years before that âby this shall all men know that ye are my disciples, if ye have love one to anotherâ (John 13:35). His doctrine and its application were meant to bring a loving unity. The Lord needed his people to be one with themselves and with him. He prayed for all those âwhich shall believe on me through their word; That they all may be one; as thou, Father, art in me, and I in thee, that they also may be one in us: that the world may believe that thou hast sent meâ (John 17:20â21). The Churchâs unity and love was, for the Lord, the primary outward witness that he was God-sent. The Churchâs expression of charity was therefore critical for his purposes. In Ephesus, however, it had grown cold. They would not forgive one another and, as the Lord warned in the latter days, âhe that forgiveth not his brother his trespasses standeth condemned before the Lord; for there remaineth in him the greater sinâ (D&C 64:9). The result for this congregation was a frozen and sterile environment in which the fruit of the gospel could not blossom, and therefore the church was near death.

The Lord promised to those who overcame that they would partake of the sweet fruit of the tree of life. The tree symbolism had particular relevance in Ephesus. The temple of Artemis/Diana was a tree shrine. Her devotees were drawn there to partake of her power and attendant quality of life. The Canaanite god Baal and his consort were also worshipped in groves. We see in Revelation that all such worship was but perversions of a truth. According to the Book of Mormon, the tree represents the pure love of the Christ (see 1 Ne. 11:21â22). One partakes of the tree by eating its fruit, that is, by accepting the Lord and his Atonement and thus achieving eternal life.53 Some have suggested that the tree of life represents the cross, but the focus in Revelation is on the redemptive power growing out of the Lordâs Atonement and the resultant restoration to Godâs presence; therefore, it looks beyond the cross.54 The understanding based on the Book of Mormon, however, that the tree represented the pure love of Christ, seems particularly apropos given the spiritual condition of the Ephesian Saints. Thus here, as one scholar noted, the âfact that [some of] the Ephesian Saints will partake of the fruit indicates that their sins will no longer impede their access to eternal life.â55

The Lord gave them a very direct warning. Unless they repented, and fast, he would come to them, but this would not be associated with the delights of the Second Coming that many anticipated. This coming would be immediate and bring a judgment that would blot out all remnants of the true Church.56

The remedy could come only through remembering and responding. They had to get back to what the gospel was designed to do. They were, after all, a lampstand created to uphold the Lord of light and proclaim his gospel and his love in unity. The light would allow others to see what the Church was all about and be drawn to its Lord. If the Christians did not spread the light, no matter how pure the doctrine, the Church would be left in darkness and die.

In addition to the message to the Church in general, there was a message to each individual who read or heard Johnâs words. It was that there were blessings that would come to each person who overcame the problems affecting the Church. It was most difficult being a Christian in Smyrna. Many were poor and their religion exacerbated this condition. Analysis and Summary

The luxurious city of Pergamum was the seat of Roman government in the province of Asia. Further, it was a center for Roman pagan practices. The temple to the deified Augustus and the elaborate altar to Zeus Soter, to name just two of the many shrines found in the city, give evidence of the cityâs devotion to its deities and Roman rulers. Sincere worshippers paid homage to their patron gods to keep or gain their favor. Even the less believing would have seen the advantages of adopting Roman gods, especially the deified emperor, as a way of ingratiating themselves to the major power of the day. By the turn of the century, all citizens and subjects were obligated by law to make offerings to the imperial god, the divine emperor; otherwise, they were condemned to death as traitors and enemies of the state. Both the devout and less devout were threatened by those forces which worked against the traditional gods. The Christians were a major force that threatened the status quo. The governorâs court was in Pergamum and to it the Christians were brought for trial. Here, it seems, they faced the âseat of Satan,â and here Caesar and Christ contended as in few other places.

The Lord assured these Saints that he knew âwhere thou dwellest.â Unfortunately, it was âwhere Satan dwellethâ (2:13). This fact accentuates the reality that where light and darkness dwell, there can be no peaceful coexistence. As a result, those who held fast to their testimony of the Lordâand that meant declaring it openly even before magistratesâcould expect the wrath of Satanâs agents to be brought against them.118

Among both Jews and Gentiles, most sacrifices did not require burning the entire animal (Lev. 1â5).119 Often the priests and participants received a portion, which they used in a sacramental meal that tied them ritually to their gods. Many held that the practice also brought the power or influence of the god or goddess on the participant.120 Paul was perfectly aware that the idea was false because idols, having no existence, could not extend any kind of spiritual advantage to the worshipper.121 Even so, Paul feared that certain Christians may not understand this principle and, seeing established members partaking of the meat, feel it was empowering, and thereby drift back into paganism. Paul therefore recommended that all abstain from the practice (see 1 Cor. 8:4â10).

There are four main theories as to what eating sacrificial meat meant: first, participating in actual pagan sacramental temple rites; second, accepting meat once offered to idols but given to participants during pagan celebrations; third, purchasing excess sacrificial meat from the temple meat market; and fourth, participating in sacral meals of clubs, guilds, or other associations.122 Of these, it is unlikely that the heretics were advocating something as blatant as participating in actual pagan temple rituals. More likely they were supplying a justification for the Christians to participate in the public festivals and then purchase sacrificial meats (like Latter-day Saints today justifying themselves in going to a sports bar because they do not have cable). These places, however, were immoral by any standard and therefore not conducive to the Holy Spirit. Because such was forbidden by apostolic authority, these heretics actually challenged the very seat of power of the Church.123

The Lordâs prohibition shows a close connection between eating sacrificial meat and immorality. Compromising on the former may have more easily lead people into the latter. Though throughout the Old Testament, the prophets used immorality as a symbol for idolatry (see, for example, Jer. 3:2; 13:27; Ezek. 16:15â58; 23:1â49; Hosea 5:4; 6:10), it is very likely that the Lord was prohibiting actual immoral acts. Some aspects of pagan worship were highly immoral and some cities had high levels of sacral prostitution. Those who propounded the doctrine of Balaam may have justified certain immoral acts.124

It is possible that the appeal of the Nicolaitans and those who followed the doctrine of Balaam was their promise of relief from persecution. Here we see the difference between the churches at Ephesus and at Pergamum. The former, with its overemphasis on complete doctrinal piety, had lost concern for the outside world, loving neither it nor those touched by it. At Pergamum the opposite was true. Here the Saints deemphasized doctrine, resulting in an overidentification with the world. Though they had withstood external pressure by Roman authorities, subtle internal pressures were leading the Saints to make dangerous compromises.125 It appears that the appeal of the doctrine of the Nicolaitans was that it offered a convincing rationale for compromising with paganism that would deflect its concerns (see below). The problem was that these compromises opened the door to practices that the Lord found repulsive because they broke both the spirit and letter of his law. Very likely the extreme cases actually led to sexual immorality but provided a salve for the conscience, thus thwarting recognition of the wrong and, therefore, repentance. Thus the Lord warned the congregation to resist these evils and repent or face his withering denialâthe sword of his mouthâat their divine judgment.

Once again the Lord did not make individual salvation contingent on that of the Church itself. Each person who heard and responded was promised the reward, including access to the new name or key word so necessary to gain eternal life. As Brigham Young told the Saints, certain ordinances âare necessary for you, after you have departed this life, to enable you to walk back to the presence of the Father, passing the angels who stand as sentinels, being enabled to give them the key words, the signs and tokens, pertaining to the Holy Priesthood, and gain your eternal exaltation.â126 Receiving the new name was the reward for resisting the world.

The idea of a new name is significant. Names in ancient societies identified not only the person but also his or her status; to be given a new name by another was to enter into a personal, even intimate, relationship that brought a higher status. Patronymics indicated that a person was a free man or woman and even of nobility. As such, he or she was in a position to inherit the family fortune. In the gospel sense, having the right name indicated one was an heir to eternal life.

That the believers will be given a white stone on which the new name is written does not mean the individual did not know the name before, only that it was now made permanent. Indeed, the Christian already had one new name, that of Christ himself, and receiving it brought consummate identification with the Lord and the promise of the final reward. Therefore it was all-important that the faithful stay true to the name or the reward could be lost.127

Taking the name of Christ did more; it brought them under Godâs protective power, one that did not guarantee protection from physical death, but rather from spiritual. That, coupled with the reception of the new name, signified that one was a member of the eternal community of the redeemed.128

There is, however, another nuance associated with the new name that should not be overlooked. In the Old Testament, Jehovah often bestowed a new name to show that a new and adoptive relationship had been established with him. The giving of the name stressed the idea that the Lord had redeemed the newly renamed one and brought him or her into his circle.129 Further, following Hebrew belief that names represent the essence of a person, receiving the new name showed that not only had oneâs status changed but also his or her essence.130 He or she not only had a new name but also was a new person in Christ (compare Rom. 6:3â11). As Dallin H. Oaks explained, âThe idea that a name changes when a personâs essence changes also helps to explain the scriptural teaching that a new name is given to all persons who come into the celestial kingdom.â131

As will be shown below, the situation of these celestial souls stands in contrast to others who also received a new name. That name, however, belonged to the satanic beast. It did not bring protection, for âthe smoke of their torment ascendeth up for ever and ever: and they have no rest day nor night, who worship the beast and his image, and whosoever receiveth the mark of his nameâ (14:11; for more, see Translation Notes there).

Analysis and Summary

Few Christians lived in Thyatira. There seems to have been just a scattered group meeting in house churches here and there in the city and therefore of little interest to the authorities. It may be for that reason that they were not persecuted like their spiritual brothers and sisters in Smyrna and Pergamum. In spite of their small numbers, the faithful Saints practiced their religion, showing forth loving service and winning the praise of the Lord. They were, however, in danger. The branch was not free from the waves of heresy that pounded on the footings of the Church in general, but they had additional challenges. Living in a city dominated by trade guilds worked to their disadvantage. Each guild had its patron deity that members worshipped at the guild meetings. Christian laborers had one of two choices, neither good. On the one hand, they could hold to their standards and refuse to join the guild, thereby losing opportunity for employment and resulting in isolation and poverty. On the other hand, they could compromise their standards, join the guild, and enjoy the community and prosperity but bring upon himself the ire of the Christian leaders and the Lord himself. It appears that some were giving in to some kind of compromise.

This willingness to compromise may have been fostered by the Nicolaitans. But the problem was compounded in Thyatira due to the seductive work of a very able and charismatic woman characterized as Jezebel. Unlike Judaism and many pagan religions, the Christians expanded the role that women played in the Church. Paulâs letters show that they prayed, taught, bore testimony, and ministered among the Saints (see Rom. 16:1â7; 1 Cor. 11:2â5; Philip. 4:3). Since most Christians, even toward the end of the first century, continued to meet in house churchesâthe larger homes of the more wealthy membersâthe women of those households had special status as the patroness (ÏÏÎ¿ÏÏÎ¬ÏÎ¹Ï, prostatis) of the congregation.153 This position, along with the Churchâs more liberal policy toward women, opened the door to misconduct. Certain overzealous women years before had pushed the limits of this policy, earning a very strong reprimand from Paul (see 1 Cor. 14:34â35). It would appear that Jezebel went beyond even these women, claiming she was the interpreter of Godâs will for the community.154

Her appeal seems to have come from her theology, which justified compromise with pagan religions and allowed the Saints to escape from persecution and enjoy the prosperity of the city. Her logic could have appealed to the writings of Paul, who taught that an idol was nothing and therefore eating sacrificial meat was really no problem (see 1 Cor. 8:4â6). Paul, however, had counseled against it because it opened the door to apostasy (1 Cor. 8:10â13). Jezebel seems to have ignored any constraints and openly advocated compromise for the good of security and prosperity. In this way she also led the way from spiritual fornication to physical.155

But as her epithet shows, she was guilty of more than false teachings. She seems to have been persecuting those who refused her seductions, including the Church leadership. The Lordâs words that he had given her âtime to repentâ suggest she had been active for some time, all the while refusing to heed counsel. It is little wonder, then, that the Lord promised her grave punishment and those who succumbed to her ways eternal suffering.156

If Jezebelâs teachings and the work of the Nicolaitans were not enough of a challenge for the Saints, it seems that certain heretics were claiming to possess the mysteries of godliness. With their false teachings, they brought a number to their standard. So disgusted was the Lord with this counterfeit worship that he branded it as âthe depths of Satan.â

The main task of the few faithful members was to hold on to the original gospel and works of charity. It was orthodoxy, not the cold and austere orthodoxy of Ephesus but the warm and inviting orthodoxy of Christ, that was to be their safeguard. Those who resisted the seductions of Jezebel and refused to enter into the depths of Satan were promised Christlike authority leading to rulership over heavenly kingdoms. Thus they had an acute need to stay true to their testimony, even though it might cost them their jobs or perhaps even their lives. It is in this light that Paulâs statement âthat if thou shalt confess with thy mouth the Lord Jesus, and shalt believe in thine heart that God hath raised him from the dead, thou shalt be savedâ (Rom. 10:9) needs to be understood. For these Saints, confession with the mouth was neither an easy road to grace nor an effortless path to salvation. Each time they bore witness, they did it with their lives on the line.
