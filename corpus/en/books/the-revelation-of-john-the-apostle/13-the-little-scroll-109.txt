Introduction

John has prepared his readers to expect, as they move into chapter 10, the final trumpet blast to fill the air, the final woe to strike the wicked. But that does not happen. The break is dramatic and, as such, helps stress the visionâs importanceâa moment of instruction and insight before the resolution of the crisis.

The vision is thus an interpretive parenthesis and serves a similar function to that found with chapter 7. The two parentheses are parallel. Where chapter 7 looked at how the Lord will protect his Saints from the latter-day onslaught, chapters 10 and 11 focus on the Saintsâ work, most especially that of John. Indeed, the purpose of chapter 10 is to reveal Johnâs personal ministry in all that is going to befall the world. It emphasizes his recommissioning and more fully clarifies his mission.

They do this through their unyieldingly righteous lives and the fearless witness they bear of the Lord and that which will befall an evil world. Second, they give God reason to respond. Analysis and Summary

As the vision continued, an unidentified heavenly voice commanded John to take the scroll, but it did not tell him what to do with it. That was left up to the mighty angel. Echoing Ezekiel 3:1â3, the angel instructs the prophet to eat the scroll with the understanding that, though it would be sweet to the taste, it would really upset his stomach. The angel makes the reason clear: the scroll symbolized Johnâs mission to âprophesy again about many peoples, and nations, and tongues, and kingsâ (Rendition 10:11).

The recommissioning dealt with Johnâs work that would extend over centuries, not just with his labors in the ancient time period. Joseph Smith and his scribe, Oliver Cowdery, were somewhat acquainted with Johnâs mission. While translating the Book of Mormon, they learned that God had commissioned John to âwrite concerning the end of the worldâ (1 Ne. 14:20â22). Further, the Apostle would be a minister âfor those who shall be heirs of salvationâ (D&C 7:6). In March 1832, Joseph Smith learned even more. The Lord revealed that the scroll âwas a mission, and an ordinance, for [John] to gather the tribes of Israelâ (D&C 77:14).43 In the June 1831 conference, Joseph Smith stated, âJohn the Revelator was then among the Ten Tribes of Israel who had been led away by Shalmaneser, king of Assyria, to prepare them for their return from their long dispersion.â44 The prophetâs words suggest he thought the ten tribes were in a single body. According to scripture, they have been scattered to all points of the compass from which they will eventually be gathered (see Isa. 11:12; 1 Ne. 22:25; 3 Ne. 16:5; D&C 33:6; 45:46, 96, 71; 110:11; 133:7; JSâM 1:37). Thus, John's work seems to be working in several areas to prepare the descendants living there for the in-gathering.

Given what modern revelation teaches, it is curious that Johnâs mission would turn bitter. In Ezekielâs case, the parchment he figuratively ate contained woes, lamentations, and judgments (Ezek. 2:9â10). Because it was the word of God, it pleased Ezekiel, but because it was harsh, it pained him.45 Johnâs mission seems to be similar. He would have the delight in assisting the gathering of Israel in the last days, but the angel also told him, âYou must again prophesy about [or âagainstâ] many peoples, nations, tongues, and kings.â46 Johnâs commission includes testifying not just about or against peoples but also about or against heads of state and, in doing so, giving them a clear warning. The term âmanyâ suggests the vastness of the field; not just to one empire but to a multitude. By witnessing the gospel, John participates directly in damning rebellious nations and their leaders. Therefore, their rejection of his message, in part, brings upon them the horrible judgments he saw while on the Isle of Patmos. The result will be bitter judgment, and Johnâs loving heart will feel it. Nonetheless, the bitterness will not overpower the ultimate sweetness of Godâs gracious acts that are designed to avenge the righteous lives and witness of the faithful (see 6:9â11; 18:4â7). Johnâs commandment to prophesy, to declare what God has foreshown, includes a heavy emphasis on understanding the importance of the present in light of that future reality. In short, God has revealed for Johnâs readers, both then and now, their and our situation. It is in light of what the future holds that the present must be properly understood and used. Thus, Revelation follows the Old Testament prophetic tradition that demands an immediate ethical response on the part of the faithful to the revealed word of God. The message is very clear: Now is the time to prepare.

Conclusion

This interlude helps explain why John received the Patmos vision. He was to play an active role in the events he recorded. Thus, God commanded him to devour the scroll, symbolic of mental digestion, and thereby internalize its contents. The Seer did so and by this means made a complete appropriation of his mission and the message he was to bear. This done, he was ready to act. The degree to which he internalized the message is illustrated by the revelation itself. The vision rings with clarity and purpose. It had become a part of John.47 It is at this point that John takes an active role in the vision. He is no longer just an observer, but an essential participant. His first official act opens chapter 11.

Introduction

John has prepared his readers to expect, as they move into chapter 10, the final trumpet blast to fill the air, the final woe to strike the wicked. But that does not happen. Conclusion

This interlude helps explain why John received the Patmos vision. He was to play an active role in the events he recorded. The degree to which he internalized the message is illustrated by the revelation itself. The vision rings with clarity and purpose.
