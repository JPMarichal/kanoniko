Analysis and Summary

As the pit is opened, smoke billows forth and obscures the light of the sun. Darkness reigns. Through this powerful symbol, John reveals the nature of the first thrust against humankind: a blow against the light. It is spiritual and intellectual darkness caused by subscribing to false philosophy and fallacious theology that first obscures the truth.27 The destroyer sends forth deceptions and delusions after the pattern he has successfully used before. The dualism that governs apocalyptic literature can be clearly seen here where only the negative response of the people to the trials of the last days is reported. But the reality is that there will likely be many who will repent and try to turn their lives around. Satan must mitigate the positive effects of the sixth seal and the first four trumpets. He knows how. At an earlier time, when none could ignore divine signs and many people began to believe, Satanâs smoke billowed forth: âFrom this time forth there began to be lyings sent forth among the people, by Satan, to harden their hearts, to the intent that they might not believe in those signs and wonders which they had seenâ (3 Ne. 1:22). Yes, âSatan did stir them up to do iniquity continually; yea, he did go about spreading rumors and contentions upon all the face of the land, that he might harden the hearts of the people against that which was good and against that which should comeâ (Hel. 16:22). His plan worked well. Under his influence men began to rationalize against the continuing fulfillment of the words of the prophets, saying, âSome things they may have guessed right, among so many; but behold, we know that all these great and marvelous works cannot come to pass, of which has been spokenâ (Hel. 16:16). History is now in the process of repeating itself.

Out of this smoke comes an appalling nightmare. The portrait of horror John describes focuses on a double monstrosity: the locust and the scorpion. The former of these dreaded engines of destruction ravage vegetation to such an extent that famine invariably follows.28 The Bible used this plague in a special symbolic sense that John follows: judgment against the rebellious.29 Infestations of these swarming creatures were so dreaded that, even on the Sabbath, the shophar (the ramâs horn trumpet blown only for sacred and very special occasions) would sound the alarm.30

These insect demons were often associated with darkness. This was not accidental. The cloud they form is often so compact that it obscures the light of the sun.31 However, Johnâs monsters do not cause the darkness; they result from it.32

The symbolism here parallels that in Joel 1â2, which describes a plague of natural locusts but with elements drawn from mythology. For example, they come, like Johnâs, from the north instead of the south or southeast as real locusts do (Joel 2:20). Joel and John are probably both picking up the Gog-Magog motif from Ezekiel 38â39 and Amos 7:1, where, in the Septuagint, a locust plague is specifically associated with the armies of Gog, that great leader of the anti-God hosts who fight against the Lord in the last days.33

John makes it clear that he is not describing real locusts when he states, âAnd they were told not to harm the grass of the earth, nor any green plant or tree, but only the people who did not have the seal of God on their foreheadsâ (Rendition 9:4). Their target, unlike real locustsâ, is people. But not everyone, only those who do not wear the seal of the living God upon their foreheads. This verse glances back at chapter 7 and the sealing of Godâs servants. Further, God forbids the locusts to kill, âbut to [only] torture them [that is, the unsealed people] for five monthsâ (Rendition 9:5).34

The worst outcome of a severe locust attack was famine. Here, however, the famine is not physical because the locusts may not attack âany green thingâ (9:4). Rather the effect is spiritual, revealing a famine of the soul. Godâs spirit is gone, leaving torment behind. âThe locusts cause and reveal to the wicked the hunger and emptiness of their hearts.â35

Note that the nature, the length of operation, and the target of the beasts are all specific. The locusts must not kill. God permits them only to cause spiritual and mental anguish.36 In short, they are tormentors. Their time limit is five months, the normal life expectancy of a locust. However, the time reference is likely symbolic; the Seer appears to be using it to show the time period in which these tormentors operate is both limited and short. Their target is confined to those men who do not bear the seal of the living God. The righteous have nothing to fear from these demonic hordes, for as Johnâs words show, there is a power moving behind the scenes, setting the limits, determining how far, how long, and at whom the torment will move. The plague, though driven by the minions of hell, is under sovereign control.37

Just how the locusts are able to torment the wicked is not known. But John insists that the anguish is real, for âin those days people will seek for death and will not find it. They will yearn for death but death will flee from themâ (9:6).

Once again, echoes from the Egyptian plagues give insight. As noted earlier, each plague challenged a specific Egyptian God and, in the end, proved them all false.38 This realization drove the Egyptians into severe psychological torment originating in a burning conscience tied to an unwillingness to repent (see Ex. 9:27â28, 35; 10:16â20). As the plagues served to harden the Egyptiansâ hearts, so too does the plague brought by the fifth trumpet. The scene is not pretty, but it is not as though there is no means of relief; righteousness is the price. For these men and women, that price is too high. Death appears much cheaper, the soft melting into nothingness. Of course, this is an illusion. Nevertheless, they will cling to it as their only hope of escape.

During the final battles that destroyed the Nephite nation, as blood and carnage swept through the land, âno man could keep that which was his own, for the thieves, and the robbers, and the murderers, and the magic art, and the witchcraft [that is, sorceries] which was in the landâ (Morm. 2:10). The locusts breed their sadistic torment and pour it out upon the corrupt. Godâs judgment is now ready to be fully unleashed and, ironically, its force comes without him having to do anything directly.

A note of caution seems to be in order here. An attempt to find modern war machines as Johnâs model instead of Old Testament parallels seems hermeneutically improper. Analysis and Summary

As the pit is opened, smoke billows forth and obscures the light of the sun. Darkness reigns. Through this powerful symbol, John reveals the nature of the first thrust against humankind: a blow against the light. It is spiritual and intellectual darkness caused by subscribing to false philosophy and fallacious theology that first obscures the truth.27 The destroyer sends forth deceptions and delusions after the pattern he has successfully used before. The dualism that governs apocalyptic literature can be clearly seen here where only the negative response of the people to the trials of the last days is reported. But the reality is that there will likely be many who will repent and try to turn their lives around. Satan must mitigate the positive effects of the sixth seal and the first four trumpets. He knows how. At an earlier time, when none could ignore divine signs and many people began to believe, Satanâs smoke billowed forth: âFrom this time forth there began to be lyings sent forth among the people, by Satan, to harden their hearts, to the intent that they might not believe in those signs and wonders which they had seenâ (3 Ne. 1:22). Yes, âSatan did stir them up to do iniquity continually; yea, he did go about spreading rumors and contentions upon all the face of the land, that he might harden the hearts of the people against that which was good and against that which should comeâ (Hel. 16:22). His plan worked well. Under his influence men began to rationalize against the continuing fulfillment of the words of the prophets, saying, âSome things they may have guessed right, among so many; but behold, we know that all these great and marvelous works cannot come to pass, of which has been spokenâ (Hel. 16:16). History is now in the process of repeating itself.

Out of this smoke comes an appalling nightmare. The portrait of horror John describes focuses on a double monstrosity: the locust and the scorpion. The former of these dreaded engines of destruction ravage vegetation to such an extent that famine invariably follows.28 The Bible used this plague in a special symbolic sense that John follows: judgment against the rebellious.29 Infestations of these swarming creatures were so dreaded that, even on the Sabbath, the shophar (the ramâs horn trumpet blown only for sacred and very special occasions) would sound the alarm.30

These insect demons were often associated with darkness. This was not accidental. The cloud they form is often so compact that it obscures the light of the sun.31 However, Johnâs monsters do not cause the darkness; they result from it.32

The symbolism here parallels that in Joel 1â2, which describes a plague of natural locusts but with elements drawn from mythology. For example, they come, like Johnâs, from the north instead of the south or southeast as real locusts do (Joel 2:20). Joel and John are probably both picking up the Gog-Magog motif from Ezekiel 38â39 and Amos 7:1, where, in the Septuagint, a locust plague is specifically associated with the armies of Gog, that great leader of the anti-God hosts who fight against the Lord in the last days.33

John makes it clear that he is not describing real locusts when he states, âAnd they were told not to harm the grass of the earth, nor any green plant or tree, but only the people who did not have the seal of God on their foreheadsâ (Rendition 9:4). Their target, unlike real locustsâ, is people. But not everyone, only those who do not wear the seal of the living God upon their foreheads. This verse glances back at chapter 7 and the sealing of Godâs servants. Further, God forbids the locusts to kill, âbut to [only] torture them [that is, the unsealed people] for five monthsâ (Rendition 9:5).34

The worst outcome of a severe locust attack was famine. Here, however, the famine is not physical because the locusts may not attack âany green thingâ (9:4). Rather the effect is spiritual, revealing a famine of the soul. Godâs spirit is gone, leaving torment behind. âThe locusts cause and reveal to the wicked the hunger and emptiness of their hearts.â35

Note that the nature, the length of operation, and the target of the beasts are all specific. The locusts must not kill. God permits them only to cause spiritual and mental anguish.36 In short, they are tormentors. Their time limit is five months, the normal life expectancy of a locust. However, the time reference is likely symbolic; the Seer appears to be using it to show the time period in which these tormentors operate is both limited and short. Their target is confined to those men who do not bear the seal of the living God. The righteous have nothing to fear from these demonic hordes, for as Johnâs words show, there is a power moving behind the scenes, setting the limits, determining how far, how long, and at whom the torment will move. The plague, though driven by the minions of hell, is under sovereign control.37

Just how the locusts are able to torment the wicked is not known. But John insists that the anguish is real, for âin those days people will seek for death and will not find it. They will yearn for death but death will flee from themâ (9:6).

Once again, echoes from the Egyptian plagues give insight. As noted earlier, each plague challenged a specific Egyptian God and, in the end, proved them all false.38 This realization drove the Egyptians into severe psychological torment originating in a burning conscience tied to an unwillingness to repent (see Ex. 9:27â28, 35; 10:16â20).
