Introduction

The interlude over, the story picks up where it left off in chapter 6. The Lamb breaks the seventh seal; the scroll of destiny lies open. Godâs testament, his will for the nations, stands ready to be executed. Judgment day has arrived. Using Johnâs model, six thousand years of indifference, of carelessness toward God, of transgression, of deliberate corruption of his ways, of willful rejection and sometimes slaughter of his prophets, of conscious rebellion and evilâall these will now receive their just compensation.

The landmasses, the waters, the firmament, and even the heavens move toward chaos. In short, de-creation blasts the cosmos.

Analysis and Summary

For millennia, the martyrs have been asking God, âHow long, O Lord, holy and true, dost thou not judge and avenge our blood on them that dwell on the earth?â (8:10). To their plea the Saints of the last days add their own. All these have demanded justice, and now justice, in the form of cataclysmic forces, is hurled against the earth. Nature responds with rumblings, thunders, lightnings, and the earthquake. With that, the de-creation of the earth begins.

Revelation chapter 8 picks up the account from where it left off in 6:17 and pushes the story forward. The interlude in chapter 7 answers the question asked at the end of chapter 6, âWho will be able to stand?â That question being answered, the vision returns again to look at what will happen to those who are not able to stand.

Even so, the message is indirect so that the inhabitants of the world must use the ear of faith to understand it. Most will not. That John places the commencement of destructions only after revealing that the Saints have been sealed up is no accident. It is because the sealing opens up divine powers that flow down and through them. As the destruction chapters open, the role the Saints play must be kept firmly in mind. That which moves the Lord to unleash the devastating powers is largely generated by the plea of the Saints. Moved by his desire to avenge his people, the Lord allows the first four angels to send their blast against the earth. In doing so, he shows his respect for the holy ones and their petition. It is of note that his action occurs simultaneously with the prayers. This fact tells us something of the content of those prayers. Not only are they a call for vengeance but also for grace. The first four plagues, as noted, are not really the beginning of retribution; they are more the last call for repentance, one last outreach with grace and mercy before God turns the world over to demonically inspired destruction.

One must therefore see in the Saintsâ prayer no craving for punishment. The righteous are, no doubt, pained due to the sins of the wicked, but they also cry out for mercy for the innocent who are victims of the wickedness. Thus, they are driven to beg God that in mercy he will take immediate action so the unjust suffering may come to an end.35

The First Four Trumpet Blasts (8:6â12)

Picking up where the seal series ends, John pushes Godâs work forward through the image of the sounding of the seven trumpets. Here we begin a unit that runs through the end of chapter 11. This arrangement is part of the deliberate and inspired literary artistry of the author, who is not trying to put events into their exact historical order. Instead, he shares with the reader various aspects of those forces that will be at play up to the Second Coming.

In the section of the vision under investigation here, he looks only at the beginning of the end. Translation Notes and Comments

8:6 seven trumpets: The plagues brought by the trumpets differ from those of the seals in chapter 6 and of the bowls in chapter 16. They are more broad than the former and less broad than the latter. Nonetheless, all give the same message: God is fully aware of the wickedness of the world and is moving against it. Here, the trumpets announce the direct involvement of God as he moves to destroy the wicked through either repentance or annihilation.39 The first phase of his movement, however, is directed not specifically at humankind, but at the earth. Such fits the latter-day pattern of de-creation dominating the scene. Here it follows Genesis. God first prepared the physical order and then introduced humankind into it. Now it is the physical that becomes disordered and then humankind is struck.

angels . . . prepared themselves to sound / angels . . . got ready to blow: As the plagues of the angel strike the earth, the other angels lift their trumpets to their mouths. The action serves to raise the tension and heighten the feeling of expectancy. Four of the angels follow the mighty angelâs devastations, calling down additional plagues. Again, the number four is symbolic of geographical totality; the whole earth will fall under the angelsâ censure, and thus, the need for the Saints to be sealed against these destructions. But it should be remembered that the prayers of the Saints have played an important role in initiating them. Their petitions, at least in part, have unleashed the wrath.40 But just what role the Church plays during this period is not disclosed. It is not in view in chapter eight, only the world is.

8:7 The first angel sounded, and there followed hail and fire mingled with blood / And the first angel blew his trumpet. And there was hail and fire mixed with blood: The first trumpetâs cry announces the beginning of the final stage of the war over the earth. Both fire and hail, a most unusual combination, strike the earth. The image reflects the seventh Egyptian plague, where a âfire mingled with the hail, very grievousâ fell all over the Pharaohâs domain and killed many people and animals, albeit the Hebrews were spared (Ex. 9:22â35). The fire gave evidence that the unusual and severe hailstorm was not simply a freak of nature, but the deliberate act of the divine. The image found in Revelation ratchets up that message. Not only is there a rain of ice and fire but also of blood.41 The imagery, which we need not take literally, evidences the divine behind the first strike against the earth. Here the weather turns vicious.

The Old Testament connected raging hailstorms with the judgments of God (see Josh. 10:11; Ps. 78:47; 105:32; Isa. 28:17). Revelation discloses that the earth has not seen the last of these (see below). The presence of fire shows that we are dealing with more than nature at her worst; rather this is a phenomenon driven by divine forces. The blood speaks to the destructive nature of the first strike.

and the third part / and a third: The fraction is used twelve times in the plague chapters, and each makes the same point: though much is destroyed, more is left. Thus, the fraction shows that these judgments are not yet final. The figure echoes Ezekiel chapters 4 and 5, where the prophet warns Jerusalem that because of her wickedness divine judgment is about to fall upon her. At that time, a third will die from pestilence, a third by famine, and the last third scattered. Therefore, the fraction should be understood less to denote an exact amount and more to connote Godâs partial judgment upon an intractable generation.

Analysis and Summary

A lot of work must be done before the Lord comes in glory. The beginning of the Millennium will see the finishing of that work.58 In Revelation, however, that beginning is not pleasant. It is marked by the terrible events recorded above and by the vicious ones studied below. Thus, the Second Coming of the Savior does not usher in the Millennium; the plagues of the seven angels do.59

Nonetheless, these events are part of an indispensable work that must be done before the Lord appears in glory. That work is not one of destruction for destructionâs sake. The plagues have a triple purpose. First, they begin the cleansing process of the earth. Second, they act to reclaim any who will repent under the hardships the plagues bring. Their combined effect should cause those blinded by materialism and immorality to seek for healing and understanding. Third, as noted, as each angel sounds, we hear echoes from Exodus 8â12. The primary aim of those plagues was not simply to discomfort Egypt. God revealed the main purpose to Moses, saying, âSee thou do all those wonders before Pharaoh, which I have put in thine hand and I will prosper thee: but Pharaoh will harden his heart, and he will not let the people goâ (JST Ex. 4:21). Indeed, he promised Moses that âPharaoh will harden his heart, as I said unto thee; and thou shalt multiply signs and wonders in the land of Egyptâ (JST Ex. 7:3). Godâs prescience proved true, for Pharaoh did react exactly as God saw he would, not only by hardening his heart but also âthe hearts of his servants,â thus allowing Jehovah to âshow these my signs before himâ (JST Ex. 10:1â2). In this way the plagues were judgments that God, through Moses, executed against Egypt due to their stubbornness against God. To emphasize, the plagues exposed the diamond hardness of the hearts of both Pharaoh and his countrymen. That hardness found its genesis, strength, and execution in an idolatry that led to persecution of the Hebrews. This idea is clearly borne out in the fact that Jehovah tailored each plague to challenge the power of a specific Egyptian god and thus expose its nothingness.60 Taken as a whole, these plagues formed a judgment and polemic against idolatry in all its aspects.61

Thus, they provide a lens by which we can see what the judgments in chapters 8 and 9 are all about. First, the Lord directs the trumpet blasts against a world adamant in its hostility towards the Lamb. Though the plagues are a call for repentance,62 an important by-product will be exposing the hardness of heart of the latter-day wicked, thus fully vindicating God in his judgments against them (see Translation Notes on 9:20â21). Like the Egyptians, as the intensity of the judgments increase, so also will the vehemence of those whom John calls âthe inhabitants of the earthâ (8:13; 12:12). Thus, what will drive some to the Lord will drive others away (9:20â21; compare Ex. 16:9, 11, 20, 21). Second, like the Egyptian plagues, they allow God to show forth his might, power, and omnipotence. Nothing the world can throw at him or his Saints will have effect. All worldly forces, as with the Egyptian gods, will be found to be nothing. As far as the combined human efforts are concerned, they will wither like grass before the sirocco.

It is important to remember that John has assured his reader that the righteous have already been taken care of. They stand safely behind the strength of their God. Thus, these trumpets do not sound the battle against the wicked until after the righteous have been sealed. The focus, then, of chapters 8 and 9 is on what happens to those who refuse the sealing power.

The act of the angel at the altar transitions the reader from the events associated with the seven seals to the destructions of the trumpets.63 There is, however, an important tie between the two chapters that becomes clear as we focus on the altar. We have seen it before in chapter 6, where it was associated with incense and the prayers of the Saints. In chapter 7, the Saints cry for vindication against their tormentors but cannot yet receive it for the roster of martyrs is not yet full. In chapter 8, vindications come because it is.

It is important to note, however, that even though these wild conditions will destroy many of the wicked and some of the righteous, the trumpet blasts are not designed to destroy the earth. They affect a significant proportion, but not all. Some twelve times the Seer limits the destruction to one-third, symbolically showing that its bounds have been set.64 It can go only so far. This suggests, as noted, that its purpose is not so much retribution as a last attempt to turn man to God.65

Even so, the plagues do attack and undo, in a very systematic way, the created order. As noted above, we are seeing here, as it were, the undoing of Genesis. Light, air, water, vegetation, and firmament are all hit and disorganized. Thus, in chapter 8, we meet a widespread, if partial, de-creation of the current, corrupted, and unrepentant temporal order. The destruction of this order is necessary because it cannot give birth to paradise.66 To say it in another way, the throes of this age are not the birth pangs to a new order, but the death rattle of an old and corrupt one. It is not to be resuscitated, but must die because of its hardened, and therefore irreversible and deadly, opposition to a new creation. Thus, there must be a new heaven and a new earth, not the rejuvenation of the old ones (21:1).

The connection of the plagues with Exodus pinpoints another important detail that explains the why of these judgments. As noted, it is the hardness of antagonistsâ hearts that will not let them yield to the punishing but nonetheless loving outreach of God. Exodus also reveals its cause, namely idolatry, the hard materialism that puts possessions ahead of people while enthroning selfishness and impounding Christlike love. The end product of such is the persecution of the righteous (see 16:2, 5â7).67 The great sorrow, or better, shame, of all this is that, as with Egypt, the plagues could end if hearts became contrite and broken. Because they will not, even nature must suffer. In the end, the old earth will be greatly damaged, likely beyond natural repair. But there is a power above that can repair her, and so a new day will come with a terrestrial and paradisiacal re-creation (see A of F 10).

Though mirroring natural events, albeit on a grand scale, the plagues do not arise soley out of natural phenomena such as hailstorm or volcanic action. Introduction

The interlude over, the story picks up where it left off in chapter 6. The Lamb breaks the seventh seal; the scroll of destiny lies open. Godâs testament, his will for the nations, stands ready to be executed. Judgment day has arrived. The First Four Trumpet Blasts (8:6â12)

Picking up where the seal series ends, John pushes Godâs work forward through the image of the sounding of the seven trumpets. Here we begin a unit that runs through the end of chapter 11. Translation Notes and Comments

8:6 seven trumpets: The plagues brought by the trumpets differ from those of the seals in chapter 6 and of the bowls in chapter 16. They are more broad than the former and less broad than the latter. Nonetheless, all give the same message: God is fully aware of the wickedness of the world and is moving against it. Here, the trumpets announce the direct involvement of God as he moves to destroy the wicked through either repentance or annihilation.39 The first phase of his movement, however, is directed not specifically at humankind, but at the earth. Such fits the latter-day pattern of de-creation dominating the scene. Here it follows Genesis. God first prepared the physical order and then introduced humankind into it. Now it is the physical that becomes disordered and then humankind is struck.

angels . . . prepared themselves to sound / angels . . . got ready to blow: As the plagues of the angel strike the earth, the other angels lift their trumpets to their mouths. The action serves to raise the tension and heighten the feeling of expectancy. Four of the angels follow the mighty angelâs devastations, calling down additional plagues. Again, the number four is symbolic of geographical totality; the whole earth will fall under the angelsâ censure, and thus, the need for the Saints to be sealed against these destructions. But it should be remembered that the prayers of the Saints have played an important role in initiating them. Their petitions, at least in part, have unleashed the wrath.40 But just what role the Church plays during this period is not disclosed. It is not in view in chapter eight, only the world is.

Analysis and Summary

A lot of work must be done before the Lord comes in glory. The beginning of the Millennium will see the finishing of that work.58 In Revelation, however, that beginning is not pleasant. It is marked by the terrible events recorded above and by the vicious ones studied below. Thus, the Second Coming of the Savior does not usher in the Millennium; the plagues of the seven angels do.59

Nonetheless, these events are part of an indispensable work that must be done before the Lord appears in glory. That work is not one of destruction for destructionâs sake. The plagues have a triple purpose. First, they begin the cleansing process of the earth. Second, they act to reclaim any who will repent under the hardships the plagues bring. Their combined effect should cause those blinded by materialism and immorality to seek for healing and understanding. Third, as noted, as each angel sounds, we hear echoes from Exodus 8â12. The primary aim of those plagues was not simply to discomfort Egypt. God revealed the main purpose to Moses, saying, âSee thou do all those wonders before Pharaoh, which I have put in thine hand and I will prosper thee: but Pharaoh will harden his heart, and he will not let the people goâ (JST Ex. 4:21). Indeed, he promised Moses that âPharaoh will harden his heart, as I said unto thee; and thou shalt multiply signs and wonders in the land of Egyptâ (JST Ex. 7:3). Godâs prescience proved true, for Pharaoh did react exactly as God saw he would, not only by hardening his heart but also âthe hearts of his servants,â thus allowing Jehovah to âshow these my signs before himâ (JST Ex. 10:1â2). In this way the plagues were judgments that God, through Moses, executed against Egypt due to their stubbornness against God. To emphasize, the plagues exposed the diamond hardness of the hearts of both Pharaoh and his countrymen. That hardness found its genesis, strength, and execution in an idolatry that led to persecution of the Hebrews. This idea is clearly borne out in the fact that Jehovah tailored each plague to challenge the power of a specific Egyptian god and thus expose its nothingness.60 Taken as a whole, these plagues formed a judgment and polemic against idolatry in all its aspects.61

Thus, they provide a lens by which we can see what the judgments in chapters 8 and 9 are all about. First, the Lord directs the trumpet blasts against a world adamant in its hostility towards the Lamb. Though the plagues are a call for repentance,62 an important by-product will be exposing the hardness of heart of the latter-day wicked, thus fully vindicating God in his judgments against them (see Translation Notes on 9:20â21). Like the Egyptians, as the intensity of the judgments increase, so also will the vehemence of those whom John calls âthe inhabitants of the earthâ (8:13; 12:12). Thus, what will drive some to the Lord will drive others away (9:20â21; compare Ex. 16:9, 11, 20, 21). Second, like the Egyptian plagues, they allow God to show forth his might, power, and omnipotence. Nothing the world can throw at him or his Saints will have effect. All worldly forces, as with the Egyptian gods, will be found to be nothing. As far as the combined human efforts are concerned, they will wither like grass before the sirocco.

It is important to remember that John has assured his reader that the righteous have already been taken care of. They stand safely behind the strength of their God. Thus, these trumpets do not sound the battle against the wicked until after the righteous have been sealed. The focus, then, of chapters 8 and 9 is on what happens to those who refuse the sealing power.

The act of the angel at the altar transitions the reader from the events associated with the seven seals to the destructions of the trumpets.63 There is, however, an important tie between the two chapters that becomes clear as we focus on the altar. We have seen it before in chapter 6, where it was associated with incense and the prayers of the Saints. In chapter 7, the Saints cry for vindication against their tormentors but cannot yet receive it for the roster of martyrs is not yet full. In chapter 8, vindications come because it is.

It is important to note, however, that even though these wild conditions will destroy many of the wicked and some of the righteous, the trumpet blasts are not designed to destroy the earth. They affect a significant proportion, but not all. Some twelve times the Seer limits the destruction to one-third, symbolically showing that its bounds have been set.64 It can go only so far. This suggests, as noted, that its purpose is not so much retribution as a last attempt to turn man to God.65

Even so, the plagues do attack and undo, in a very systematic way, the created order. As noted above, we are seeing here, as it were, the undoing of Genesis. Light, air, water, vegetation, and firmament are all hit and disorganized. Thus, in chapter 8, we meet a widespread, if partial, de-creation of the current, corrupted, and unrepentant temporal order. The destruction of this order is necessary because it cannot give birth to paradise.66 To say it in another way, the throes of this age are not the birth pangs to a new order, but the death rattle of an old and corrupt one. It is not to be resuscitated, but must die because of its hardened, and therefore irreversible and deadly, opposition to a new creation. Thus, there must be a new heaven and a new earth, not the rejuvenation of the old ones (21:1).

The connection of the plagues with Exodus pinpoints another important detail that explains the why of these judgments. As noted, it is the hardness of antagonistsâ hearts that will not let them yield to the punishing but nonetheless loving outreach of God. Exodus also reveals its cause, namely idolatry, the hard materialism that puts possessions ahead of people while enthroning selfishness and impounding Christlike love. The end product of such is the persecution of the righteous (see 16:2, 5â7).67 The great sorrow, or better, shame, of all this is that, as with Egypt, the plagues could end if hearts became contrite and broken. Because they will not, even nature must suffer. In the end, the old earth will be greatly damaged, likely beyond natural repair. But there is a power above that can repair her, and so a new day will come with a terrestrial and paradisiacal re-creation (see A of F 10).

Though mirroring natural events, albeit on a grand scale, the plagues do not arise soley out of natural phenomena such as hailstorm or volcanic action. Johnâs use of á½¡Ï (hÅs), âlike,â and á½Î¼Î¿Î¹Î¿Ï (homoios), âlike,â emphasize the symbolic nature of the images he describes (8:8, 10; 9:2, 3, 5, 7â10, 17, 19). The plagues represent severe eschatological judgments of which there is no equivalent in Jewish apocalyptic.68 The picture John paints, though echoing Exodus, is unique and meant to graphically show the tremendous power that will rampage upon the earth. In this way, God symbolized the cosmic destructions that he shall unleash. The separate plagues need not happen one after the other. It is more likely that the whole world will be set in commotion simultaneously.69

As noted, these attacks are directed primarily against nature. Nonetheless, humankind is seriously affected. When the grass and trees are hurt, it is wheat and fruit trees that wither and die.70 When the fresh waters are affected they become as wormwood, a very bitter herb that makes the water impossible to drink.71 The catastrophes apparently cause the atmosphere to become so polluted that sunlight and moonlight are significantly dimmed.72 Thus, de-creation has directly struck all but humankind. That comes next.

The transition works to highlight the severe intensity of the forces about to be unleashed against that segment John calls âthe inhabitants of the earth,â those who have found comfort in and fight for the permanence of this transitory order.
