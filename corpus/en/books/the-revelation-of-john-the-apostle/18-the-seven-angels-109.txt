Introduction

Chapter 14 closes with a description of immense bloodshed as the destroying angel harvests the earth. The forepart of chapter 15 completes that vision and opens the next. The completion of the first vision is a scene of victorious celebration. The celebrants are those who have overcome all aspects of Satanâs beast. It would be natural to think that such a happy scene, with the sweet song of victory filling the air, should end the tale. That, however, is not the case. Instead, a new vision opens. This one stands in sour contrast to the sweetness of the one just closed. Indeed, it introduces seven angels having the last plagues, which will violently strike the earth before the Second Coming. Since the theme at the end of chapter 14 is the final judgmentâsymbolized by the harvesting of the wheat fields and the vineyardsâhow can there be plagues after that? The answer is, there cannot. What John sees is a flashback that looks very carefully at those who constitute the grapes of the earth and therefore feel the fullness of Godâs wrath. In chapter 15, the Seer watches as destroying angels prepare to do their work. In this vision, we sense the book of Revelation moving irresistibly toward its climax.

Analysis and Summary

As already noted, throughout Revelation, the Lord has a pattern of describing the blessed and peaceful condition of the faithful before presenting the horrors unleashed against the ungodly. His pattern continues here as John briefly sees the blessed condition of the Saints before the bowls of wrath are poured out against the world. The vision centers on the final glorious reward of the victorious Saints as they âstand on the sea of glass,â having harps to praise God (15:2). We have already learned that a sea of glass represented the celestialized earth (see Translation Notes on 4:6). The symbol seems to be expanded here to include all those who will inhabit it. Through this imagery, the Lord assures his readers that the Saints will come through the fiery trialsâthose that will destroy the wickedâto their eternal glory.17 Thus, it is appropriate that they sing a victorâs song.

Like the Israelites who escaped the might of Pharaoh and his army through the direct intervention of Jehovah celebrated the occasion with a great song of triumph, so too do these victorious Saints. Because they, as Israel of old, won through no other weapon than the intervening power of God, the song is appropriately called the song of Moses.

While their song echoes a number of Old Testament doxologies, Revelation has combined them in such a way as to amplify this songâs central messageâthe Christianâs faithfulness allows God to exercise his power unto their salvation. Indeed, the song is sung by those who were delivered from the bruising power of the beast because they prevailed in their testimony of Christ. But John gives the song an even higher quality, noting that it is also composed by the Lamb. Both Saint and Son praise the powers of God, who, finally, revealed his might through the last plagues, which avenged those who have held true to his word and their testimony of the Lamb.

The song focuses on the works and the methods of God, which are amazing to humankind. His deeds amaze people in two ways: in their magnitude and in their unpredictability.18 At the same time they consist of two attributes: they are perfectly just and completely proper. Because of these, God is worthy of praise and honor.

To emphasize the greatness of God, President Brigham Young paraphrased the Father as saying,

I will show you that I am greater than you all, and I will put you to shame, and blast your expectations, and disappoint your calculations, and your attempts to injure my people will be foiled; for Zion shall arise, her glory shall be seen, and the kings of the earth shall enquire of the wisdom of Zion; and God shall be great, and His name shall be terrible among the inhabitants of the earth; and He will bring forth His kingdom and establish His government, and Jesus will come and rule, King of nations, as he does King of Saints.19

Though the song is a celebration of victory, it does not mention Godâs judgment upon his enemies. Rather, it concentrates on the righteousness of his redemptive acts through which âall nations shall come and worship before [him]â (15:4).20 The point of the praise is in the last clause, âfor thy judgments are made manifestâ or âyour righteous deeds have been revealedâ (15:4). At the time of Jesusâ coming, all will be revealed.21 Especially clear will be Godâs continued warnings of retribution against those who break his laws. The result is that all will be convinced to worship him. They will come to see that each of his laws were designed to bring them joy and life. Even those who do not receive these blessings will readily admit the fault was their own for three reasons: (1) the Father fully revealed his law, (2) he did his best to bring all people to him, and (3) he warned them of the consequences of disobedience.

The point is this: nothing can stop his will from being done. He executes it through the course of history and, in this way, constructs that history. In the end, justice will be done and judgment will have its total sway. All will be fairly and fully judged. Thus, the force of the song emphasizes the impossibility of anyone considering God has been unjust. It also emphasizes the impossibility of them not falling in reverence before him. In the not-too-distant future and without any coercion whatsoever, âevery knee shall bow, and every tongue confessâ the greatness of God and glorify his name (Rom. 14:11; Mosiah 27:31; D&C 88:104).

John again looks and sees the heavenly temple. Once before he has seen this celestial shrine (11:19); however, that time the vision focused on the ark, which held the covenant. Analysis and Summary

John again looks and sees the heavenly temple. Once before he has seen this celestial shrine (11:19); however, that time the vision focused on the ark, which held the covenant. However, when students really study the text and read it in its context, they find that, as another scholar stated, âthe concept of the wrath in the Book of Revelation is more profoundly Christian than that which is found in any other part of the New Testament.â47 The reason, as noted already, is that Godâs wrath is not a blind uncontrolled fury but the venting of a carefully controlled emotion. His wrath is directed at only a specific segment of society: those who would destroy the earth and with it his people (11:18). The warning was very clear and it gave the wicked a chance to repent, a merciful act of love.

Though the text does speak of God, moved by his ire, as an agent of destruction (implied in the image of the bowls of wrath; see 14:8, 10; 16:19; 18:3), he actually uses humans as his agents to achieve his appointed ends. Indeed, until the very end, the horror John reveals does not come from God but from those determined to destroy all who oppose them, especially the righteous. Here John and Mormon agree: âit is by the wicked that the wicked are punishedâ (Morm. 4:5). Because God does not coerce them in any way but rather pleads with them to be righteous and peaceable, these rebellious souls are fully culpable and therefore well deserve the brutalizing they give one another. But note that it is not what their loving God desires.

Though Babylon has given the nations what seems to be the wine of her impure passion, in reality it is the wine of Godâs wrath. As a result, it will backfire on her and, as we shall see, she will fall to the impure passions of her former lovers. Not satisfied with that, however, they turn on each other. The result will be a war frenzy so fierce and violent that it is described as blood to a horseâs bridles (14:20).48 The irony is that only by mass destruction can the Lord save the masses from destruction.

The Lord is justified in his act because the bruising of the earth does not come without widespread and clear warning. The book of Revelation is one piece of that warning, and a very strong one. Another strong piece, noted in the work, is the preaching of the gospel. Resolutely and relentlessly the warning voice is being proclaimed to the entire world. Only when the world rejects the testimony of both warnings does the bruising come. Rejecting both witnesses is a clear signal of the depth of depravity into which the world has sunk. That depth expresses itself in two ways: first, by those leadersâreally beasts in human guiseâwho are willing to destroy millions to get what they want, and second, by the refusal of their victims to repent and come to God. Revelation clearly shows how hardened people are becomingâa hardness so severe that it will spawn the plagues of the seventh seal described so graphically in chapters 8, 9, 14, and 16.

As early as 6:16, John makes his readers aware of the âwrath of the Lamb,â a wrath fully revealed in the plagues. Indeed, to the unrepentant, the Lamb warns he will âsmite you by the rod of my mouth, and by my wrath, and by my anger, and your sufferings [shall] be soreâhow sore you know not, how exquisite you know not, yea, how hard to bear you know notâ (D&C 19:15). The irony is that he himself has experienced the consequences of such wrath: âwhich suffering caused myself, even God, the greatest of all, to tremble because of pain, and to bleed at every pore, and to suffer both body and spiritâand would that I might not drink the bitter cup, and shrinkâ (D&C 19:18). Therefore, he knows of what he speaks and is most anxious to have people repent, accept him, live his commandments, and thereby fully escape the wrath.

The result of refusing his outstretched arm will be a frenzied war of blood and horror. But before that frenzy can destroy the world, taking all down with it, the Lamb will come in his wrath and destroy the destroyers of the world and in doing so save the earth and the righteous. When all is said and done, his wrath is the best friend of the righteous and even the wicked when it helps them repent. Therefore, ever standing behind Godâs wrath and perhaps even fueling it is love. Thus, divine anger is an attribute that should be praised.
