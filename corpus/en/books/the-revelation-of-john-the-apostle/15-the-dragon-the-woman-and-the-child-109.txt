Introduction

With the angelic rejoicing finished and the promise given at the blast of the seventh trumpet realized, the vision of the seven seals closes. But the revelation does not end. Immediately another series of visions begins. These, however, do not take up where the old series left off. Chapter 12 breaks the flow of the vision. The narrative that began in chapter 8 and ended in chapter 11 will not pick up again until chapter 15. Between the two visions and bracketed by them are three subtexts. The first deals with the war between the woman and the dragon, which includes a flashback to the premortal war in heaven (chapter 12). The second deals with the rise of the dragonâs assistants, the beasts from the sea and from the land (chapter 13). The last section deals with securing the Saints in Zion and the harvesting of the field and of the vineyard, with the attendant declarations by heavenly messengers (chapter 14). This interlude serves an important purpose: it gives the reader a deeper understanding of the forces and conditions that will force the Lord to move in wrath against the wicked.1

Chapter 12 serves as the pivotal point of the whole book. It is the key to understanding all that went before and all that comes after.2 Here, the ground of the conflict is exposed and explored. The enemy is introduced, his background given, and his purposes and methodology revealed. The details of the spiritual conflict between good and evil, God and Satan, Saint and persecutor, are brought into light. What chapters 1â11 introduced and implied, chapter 12 explains in greater detail. For the first time, the great fiend, the cunning initiator and promoter of the trouble of the Saints, the enemy of all righteousness, is named and branded. The stage is set for the events described in chapters 13â15. Thus, this chapter serves as the conclusion for the first half of the book and the introduction for the last half.3

This section begins with a flashback to Johnâs day. There is good reason for the flashback. As noted in the previous chapter, the reaction of Godâs latter-day enemies to his takeover of the world was one of anger. âEnragedâ might be a better word, for it reveals both the passion that spurred the Lordâs enemies to move against him and his people and their major objective.4 They were determined not to simply sit back and do nothing as the Lord moved to take over the earth. Motivated by rage, their primary objective was to waste the world, leaving God with little more than a smoldering cinder. Such malevolence is hard to fathom. Revelation, however, beginning at this point, retells the story to show how and why such hatred developed, the nature of the battle it will produce, and the warâs final outcome.

The cosmic combat myth, in which both mortal and divine elements fight each other for dominance over the earth, has formed a part of the human mythos from time immemorial. Often the antagonist has been depicted as a monster, serpent, or dragon representing chaos and sterility, while the protagonist has been depicted as a divine being or one honored by the heavens and representing order and fertility. Many people living in the ancient Near East, from the top of the Euphrates to the cataracts of the Nile, had their versions of the story, but the motifs of the legends remained remarkably the same.5 Many of these elements show up in chapters 12 and 13. Because of that, many of Johnâs early readers would have been very comfortable with this part of the vision.

Both the KJV and Rendition follow the usual format of Revelation by using 11:19 as the conclusion for the previous vision. However, as noted at the end of the last chapter, this verse actually serves as a very good transition between the two visions. It reveals Godâs intent to move more directly onto the stage of history. As this chapter shows, he does so through the establishment of his Church in Johnâs day.

Analysis and Summary

The war in heaven serves as the background for understanding the dragonâs attack on the man child and the woman. He knew who and what his enemies were. Satan exercised his energy first on thwarting the organization of the political kingdom of God on the earth. Once he had chased off that threat to his earthly sovereigntyâthe greater of the twoâhe turned on the woman. However, she received divine assistance and escaped into the wilderness. âThe two great wings of an eagleâ symbolize this assistance. The wings have a double meaning: rapid motion or flight, and divine deliverance.127 With these wings, the woman fled into the wilderness, symbolic of spiritual refuge.128 Both the Old and the New Testaments represent the desert as a place of provisional safety, of discipline, of waiting for the promises of God to be fulfilled.129 In this context, the desert illustrates that the Church has become inaccessible to humans.130

As the woman fled to her place of refuge, the dragon made one last desperate attempt to destroy her. Vomiting up water, his primary weapon, he endeavored to sweep her away in a flood. The earth, however, assisted the woman by swallowing the vast tide. Just what John intended is difficult to assess. Satan is the father of lies, that much is known for sure. In one way or another, everything that issues from his mouth conveys deception. Perhaps with a deluge of lies, not unlike those which the modern Church faces from its detractors, he tried to destroy the early Church.

But John could have had other ideas in mind. Floodwaters are a common metaphor in the Old Testament for tribulation as well as overwhelming evil (Ps. 18:4; Isa. 43:2). The term is used to denote oppressive nations such as Assyria and Babylon, as well as hostile nations generally.131 Oppression may be what John alludes to, especially in light of the heightened persecution of Rome against the Christian people at the time of the Seerâs writings. Introduction

With the angelic rejoicing finished and the promise given at the blast of the seventh trumpet realized, the vision of the seven seals closes. But the revelation does not end. Immediately another series of visions begins. These, however, do not take up where the old series left off. Chapter 12 breaks the flow of the vision. The narrative that began in chapter 8 and ended in chapter 11 will not pick up again until chapter 15. Between the two visions and bracketed by them are three subtexts. The first deals with the war between the woman and the dragon, which includes a flashback to the premortal war in heaven (chapter 12). The second deals with the rise of the dragonâs assistants, the beasts from the sea and from the land (chapter 13). The last section deals with securing the Saints in Zion and the harvesting of the field and of the vineyard, with the attendant declarations by heavenly messengers (chapter 14). This interlude serves an important purpose: it gives the reader a deeper understanding of the forces and conditions that will force the Lord to move in wrath against the wicked.1

Chapter 12 serves as the pivotal point of the whole book. It is the key to understanding all that went before and all that comes after.2 Here, the ground of the conflict is exposed and explored. The enemy is introduced, his background given, and his purposes and methodology revealed. The details of the spiritual conflict between good and evil, God and Satan, Saint and persecutor, are brought into light. What chapters 1â11 introduced and implied, chapter 12 explains in greater detail. For the first time, the great fiend, the cunning initiator and promoter of the trouble of the Saints, the enemy of all righteousness, is named and branded. The stage is set for the events described in chapters 13â15. Thus, this chapter serves as the conclusion for the first half of the book and the introduction for the last half.3

This section begins with a flashback to Johnâs day. There is good reason for the flashback. Revelation, however, beginning at this point, retells the story to show how and why such hatred developed, the nature of the battle it will produce, and the warâs final outcome.

The cosmic combat myth, in which both mortal and divine elements fight each other for dominance over the earth, has formed a part of the human mythos from time immemorial. Often the antagonist has been depicted as a monster, serpent, or dragon representing chaos and sterility, while the protagonist has been depicted as a divine being or one honored by the heavens and representing order and fertility. Many people living in the ancient Near East, from the top of the Euphrates to the cataracts of the Nile, had their versions of the story, but the motifs of the legends remained remarkably the same.5 Many of these elements show up in chapters 12 and 13. Because of that, many of Johnâs early readers would have been very comfortable with this part of the vision.

Both the KJV and Rendition follow the usual format of Revelation by using 11:19 as the conclusion for the previous vision. However, as noted at the end of the last chapter, this verse actually serves as a very good transition between the two visions. It reveals Godâs intent to move more directly onto the stage of history. As this chapter shows, he does so through the establishment of his Church in Johnâs day.

Analysis and Summary

The war in heaven serves as the background for understanding the dragonâs attack on the man child and the woman. He knew who and what his enemies were. Satan exercised his energy first on thwarting the organization of the political kingdom of God on the earth. Once he had chased off that threat to his earthly sovereigntyâthe greater of the twoâhe turned on the woman. However, she received divine assistance and escaped into the wilderness. âThe two great wings of an eagleâ symbolize this assistance. The wings have a double meaning: rapid motion or flight, and divine deliverance.127 With these wings, the woman fled into the wilderness, symbolic of spiritual refuge.128 Both the Old and the New Testaments represent the desert as a place of provisional safety, of discipline, of waiting for the promises of God to be fulfilled.129 In this context, the desert illustrates that the Church has become inaccessible to humans.130

As the woman fled to her place of refuge, the dragon made one last desperate attempt to destroy her. Vomiting up water, his primary weapon, he endeavored to sweep her away in a flood. The earth, however, assisted the woman by swallowing the vast tide. Just what John intended is difficult to assess. Satan is the father of lies, that much is known for sure. In one way or another, everything that issues from his mouth conveys deception. Perhaps with a deluge of lies, not unlike those which the modern Church faces from its detractors, he tried to destroy the early Church.

But John could have had other ideas in mind. Floodwaters are a common metaphor in the Old Testament for tribulation as well as overwhelming evil (Ps. 18:4; Isa. 43:2). The term is used to denote oppressive nations such as Assyria and Babylon, as well as hostile nations generally.131 Oppression may be what John alludes to, especially in light of the heightened persecution of Rome against the Christian people at the time of the Seerâs writings. Further, the waters represent chaos, the result of unchecked iniquity. Satan, once the kingdom of God was gone, secured his place as prince of the world. Seldom-checked and ever-growing iniquity would characterize his reign.132

Perhaps Satan tried through tribulation and unbridled wickedness to destroy the Church. Conceivably, he attempted to seduce and threaten or wear out the true Saints. The metaphor echoes Numbers 16:32, where Korahâs rebellion against Moses ended when âthe earth opened her mouth, and swallowed them up, and their houses, and all the men that appertained unto Korah, and all their goods.â133 Somehow the earth absorbed Satanâs lies and malice long enough to allow the Church time to secure that which was most precious before fleeing to that safety which awaits the righteous in God.

The wilderness, as the Revelator makes clear, was a place of protection where the woman was nurtured. The period of her rest would be one thousand two hundred sixty days or three and one-half years.134 This number, as has been shown earlier, represents a period of tremendous trial. The trial comes because it is the period of satanic rule.135 However, in each case where the number is used, the period ends in total victory for God. Thus, the woman, though forced to flee from the main stage of earthâs history, would return and triumph in the end. That time began on April 6, 1830, with the ârising up and the coming forth of my church out of the wildernessâclear as the moon, and fair as the sun, and terrible as an army with bannersâ (D&C 5:14; compare D&C 109:72â73).

But before that time, the Churchâs progress had ceased. It is fair to ask, however, what church? Since the temporal Church fell into apostasy, what church did God preserve and place in safe-keeping? Likely John refers to the Church of the Firstborn spoken of in the Doctrine and Covenants. There the Savior states, âI was in the beginning with the Father, and am the Firstborn; and all those who are begotten through me are partakers of the glory of the same, and are the church of the Firstbornâ (D&C 93:21â22). How one is begotten is clearly spelled out: faith, repentance, baptism, and reception of the Holy Ghost are prerequisite. Then one must be sealed through continual good works by the Holy Spirit of Promise. Those who do so become the Church of the Firstborn, being âpriests and kings [and for women, priestesses and queens], who have received of [Godâs] fulness, and of his gloryâ (D&C 76:50â70).136 In Johnâs day, this group comprised those who had ears to hear what the Spirit was saying to the churches. Heading the exiled or wilderness Church would have been John, assisted, very likely, by the three Nephite disciples. At least these four, but perhaps others also recruited for the cause, kept the keys alive and the work moving.137 Thus, God frustrated Satan in his attempt to destroy this Church.

Behind the scene stands Godâs omniscience. He knew what was going to happen and let his leaders in on it. Thus, Paul told the Saints in Thessalonica that the Apostasy was coming and that they must prepare. He confirmed his testimony some years later during a gathering of Church leaders to Miletus, saying, âAfter my departing shall grievous wolves enter in among you, not sparing the flock. Also of your own selves shall men arise, speaking perverse things, to draw away disciples after them. Therefore watch, and remember, that by the space of three years I ceased not to warn every one night and day with tearsâ (Acts 20:29â31). Armed with such foreknowledge, the early leaders were successful in getting the Church into the wilderness and away from the destroying powers of the adversary.

Having failed to rid the earth of the Church, the dragon turned upon the few remaining faithfulâthose who âkeep the commandments of God, and have the testimony of Jesusâ (12:17)âand made war on them. These two phrases serve to link conformity to Godâs will with faithfulness to Christ. Indeed, they define the essence of Christian faith as understood by the earliest Christians. Both parts were essential. As noted above, the commitment to commandment keeping likely represented the Ten Commandments with the additional higher law as taught by the Lord. The second part included more than the mere possession of a testimony. The Greek word translated as âtestimony,â Î¼Î±ÏÏÏÏÎ¯Î± (martyria), carries the nuance not only of possessing a witness, but more particularly of bearing that witness both in word and deed. Thus, the early Church did not see gospel demands as ancillary to faith in Jesus Christ but rather complementary to that requirement.138

It is in this period, when Satan raged against the seed of the woman, that Paulâs statement âwith the heart man believeth unto righteousness; and with the mouth confession is made unto salvationâ (Rom. 10:10; see also Translation Notes on Rom. 10:10) must be placed. Confessing oneâs Christian belief was fraught with danger. Satan, as the Book of Mormon prophet Nephi saw, had not been idle. Among the nations of the Gentiles he formed âa church which is most abominable above all other churches, which slayeth the saints of God, yea, and tortureth them and bindeth them down, and yoketh them with a yoke of iron, and bringeth them down into captivity.â Indeed, âfor the praise of the world do they destroy the saints of God, and bring them down into captivityâ (1 Ne. 13:5â9). Thus, to confess Christ was to come under the crosshairs of the devilâs servants and reap sorrow and pain. Little wonder, then, the Church had to flee into the wilderness to survive.

Conclusion

John seems to have written the book of Revelation during the period portrayed in chapter 12. The external Church was on the defensive. John warned her of what would happen if she failed to resist the floods of evil and persecution which Satan was loosing upon her. But to those who were true and faithful, the Lord held out hope. Indeed, the inner Church during the period of Johnâs writing was already escapingâin large part by means of hiding up its sacred materials (see D&C 7; JSâH 1:33â34)âto a place prepared by God, where it would be secure until God needed the keys to begin the work again.

In the meantime, the children of the faithful mother were in for a hard time. John showed in chapters 12 and 13 that the old dragon will call for others to assist himâthe beast from the sea and the beast from the landâto harass the faithful. Indeed, persecution and harassment will be the lot of the faithful to the end-time.

John reveals to his faithful readers that a strong, intelligent, willful, malevolent, and scheming spiritual force stands behind many of the earthly woes the Saints have faced and will face. Lucifer is ever active. His wrath and destructive intent have been initiated because of his loss of station in the celestial realm and heightened by his failure to destroy both the kingdom of God and the true Church.139 His feelings have been heightened because he knows he has but a short time to work. Therefore, he will bring all his forces to bear. Even so, he will not be able to prevail over Godâs people in any meaningful way. Godâs ever-present protective power shields his institutions and prepares them for their final victory.

For Johnâs readers, both ancient and modern, the point should be clear: any compromise with the world is actually a concession to Satan. Such a view should shock modern disciples out of spiritual complacency and move them to full-hearted resistance to the world and its allure. All should be motivated to full service to their God in keeping commandments and bearing witness. As the Lord said to each of the seven churches addressed in chapters 2 and 3, âHe who has ears to hear, let him hear [what the Spirit says].â140 The admonition still stands.
