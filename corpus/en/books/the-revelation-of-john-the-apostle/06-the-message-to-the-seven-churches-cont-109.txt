Sardis: In the sixth century BC, Sardis had been the most powerful city in the ancient Near Eastern world, yet at the time of Christ it had declined to the point that Ramsay could accurately describe it as âa relic of the period of barbaric warfare, which lived rather on its ancient prestige than on its suitability to present conditions.â1 The city was located about fifty miles east of Ephesus and a number of important trade routes passed through it, but more importantly, it was the terminus of the Royal Road. As a result, it was quite wealthy and able to support a standing population in excess of sixty thousand. From the spur of Mt. Timolus on which it sat, it dominated the fertile Hermus plain. The mountainâs summit was fifteen hundred feet above the plain and had sheer walls on three sides, making it an ideal citadel. Even so, it had fallen to enemies twice in its history. The last was to the soldiers of the city of Pergamum. As a result, it became one of that cityâs subjects and later part of the Roman province of Asia.

Its patron goddess was the Asiatic Cybele, renamed by the Greeks as Artemis. The goddess was worshipped because of her reputation as a healer and restorer of life. Due to that influence, the city had a reputation as the seat of life-giving power. Even so, it suffered a severe and deadly earthquake in AD 16. Her citizens were able to rebuild her only with a huge financial investment by the Emperor Tiberius Caesar.2

A main trade of the city was woolen goods. The woven textiles with their beautiful colors were prized throughout the Roman area, and a clothing business thrived.3 When the cloth became soiled, however, it lost its value. If the soiling was too great, it became nearly worthless. The same was true of the garments. As a result, the citizens of Sardis were sensitive to unclean clothes, especially in relation to worship of their gods, insisting the dirty robes not only defiled the devotee but also dishonored the god.4

Sardis had a very large Jewish population. The synagogue excavated there is one of the largest ever found in the Near East.5 It was likely with these Jews that the first Christian missionaries to Sardis made contact. That the text does not mention the Christians having any problem from the Jews suggests there was little problem between house church and synagogue.

The spirituality of the Saints in Sardis reflects the history of the city itself. Once the capitol of the Lydian empire, it boasted riches and life. But even before the coming of Rome, it had declined to the point of political insignificance, living mainly on the reputation of its old glory. Likewise, the Christian Church had also once been alive there and had gained a solid reputation, but now, due primarily to acute complacency, its glory had not only faded but stood in the deadly shadow of apostasy.6 It is of note that Sardis and its sister city Laodicea were the only congregations untroubled by outside pressure and inward error. Yet neither received any praise from the Lord. Instead, both received scathing rebukes. It is likely that in Sardis the lack of opposition and struggle had led to complacency and spiritual weakness.7

Analysis and Summary

The situation of the Saints mirrors that of the city. As Charles stated, âNo city in Asia presented a more deplorable contrast of past splendour and present unresting decline.â20 Further, its goddess was supposed to bring the blessing of life, but instead her cold and lifeless statue gave none. As with the city, so with the church. It, too, was resting on past glory coming from the time when its Christian neighbors saw it as the place where the spirit thrived and the light of the gospel burned warm and radiant. Though the glow of that period remained, it hid an actual pallor of death.

The Church at Sardis received the second most severe rebuke of all the Churches to which the Lord addressed letters. Only Laodicea surpassed it. The likely reason was that, though it was âuntroubled by heresy and free of outward opposition, it had so completely come to terms with its pagan environment that, although it retained its outward appearance of life, it was spiritually dead.â21 One scholar noted that it had become âthe perfect model of inoffensive Christianity.â22 The pagans were in harmony with the Christians primarily because they found nothing to object to. Simply put, the Christians bought peace at the price of standing for nothing. The once-guarded acceptance of some things pagan that had once been the virtue of the branch had opened the doors to full-scale tolerance. Such all-inclusive tolerance is the virtue of people who have nothing to stand for. As happens so often, tolerance had turned into license. The result was that there was little that separated the Christian from the pagan. In other words, the Sardis branch had compromised to the point that it was Christian in name only.

Those who gave themselves over to compromise had symbolically soiled their spiritual clothes. As noted above, the imagery would have meant much to those at Sardis. The textile makers knew well the loss of value when cloth became soiled. Further, religious practice demanded that worshippers appear before their god in clean clothes, for unclean garments defiled the worshipper and dishonored the god. Johnâs imagery, set as it was within a temple context, suggests it could have been priestly garments that the Christians had soiled. As such, faithless Saints doubly dishonored themselves and their God.23

The defiled priestly garments so central in the nightmare that afflicted the soul of the priest Zachariah, noted above, mirrors the sacred garments worn by those who have been endowed as well as the vestments actually worn during the temple service. Symbolically, despoiling these holy items would be breaking the covenants associated with them and thereby forfeiting the shielding and protecting power they offer.24

Remembering what they had left and repenting, however, could bring about a renewal of life.25 Otherwise, even the few living ones who remained would not be able to save the rest. Even so, those who were faithful would not lose their reward. In this case, the reward took the form of three blessings: first, they would be arrayed in white garments, very likely a sign of their triumph over their enemies; second, their names would remain in the divine ledger, thus assuring their citizenship in the eternal world; and third, Jesus would stand by them at the divine court, thus assuring the their full acquittal and access to eternal freedom.

Philadelphia: Like others of the seven cities, the area once occupied by Philadelphia now sits under a modern city, in this case Alasehir. The original city was established between 159 and 138 BC by Attalus II Philadelphus, who gave it his name. It stood on the south side of the Cagamis River and overlooked a broad valley that led gently down to the Aegean Sea over fifty miles away. Part of what made the area attractive was that three major roads connected there, one of which was the important Roman postal road leading to the high plateau country to the east. Its location helped it earn the title âGateway to the Eastâ and contributed to its status of one of the great emporiums of the province of Asia. Its one major drawback was that it lay only a few miles from a major fault line, making it subject to earthquakes. The great earthquake of AD 16 that leveled twelve other cities nearly destroyed Philadelphia forever. It, like its neighbors, was able to rebuild only due to the largess of Tiberius Caesar.

The city was known for its many temples and its enthusiastic celebration of religious festivals. Its devotion to its gods earned it the title of âlittle Athens.â The city sat in a lush grape-growing area, which made it natural for its patron deity to be Dionysus, the god of wine.

As strong as paganism was in the area, it did not seem to interfere much with the Christians. Here, as in Smyrna, the primary opponents of the Church were certain Jews.

Analysis and Summary

The Lord beckoned these people to look to the future when God would establish his kingdom and reverse the earthly conditions so hostile to his people. In that day, all will pay homage not only to the Lord but also to those who had been faithful to him. The promise of dominance over the Jews parallels that of dominance of the Jews over the Gentiles given earlier (see 2:26â27). Those Jews who scoffed at the idea that the Philadelphian Saints were the objects of Godâs love would be forced to see and acknowledge their error in the future.41

In the meantime, because of their faith, the Lord would spare the congregation from the immediate and earthshaking trial soon to come. His words show that he fully intended to preserve these Saints, not by taking them off the earth, but by protecting them from the machinations of Satan, which would hit the world as a flood.42 The light of priesthood would burn bright in this little congregation even as it was dimming and going out in other branches.

The Lordâs promise that he would come to them quickly takes on quite a different meaning than it did to the other branches. The Lord promised Ephesus that he would come quickly, but it would be to take away their lampstand. He promised Pergamum he would come quickly, but to fight against them with the sword of his mouth. Finally, he promised Sardis he would come quickly, but as a thief in the night with the implication that he would take away objects of value. To Philadelphia, conversely, his coming would signal the end of their hour of trial, but just how, he does not say.43

To those who overcame, the Lord gave a threefold promise: First, he would write the name of God upon them by which all would know that they were his special possession. Second, he would write upon them the name of the divine city, thus showing that they were citizens therein. Finally, he would write upon them his own new name, showing that they had not only became one with the Lord but were also like him.

Analysis and Summary

Though blessed as no other congregation, the Laodiceans were guilty of one of the gravest sins: pride and the hubris that grows out of it. The congregation as a whole seem to have been wealthy, secure, and at peace with its neighbors. It does not even seem to have been afflicted with the heresies that threatened to destroy other branches. The problem was that the Saints took credit for their peaceful station and rich condition. By taking credit for their blessings and believing they needed nothing from God, they became gods unto themselves. That act was nothing short of hubris and bordered on blasphemy.

Likely they could have marshaled solid evidence to support their case. Their view, however, was short-sighted and earthbound. The result was a growing spiritual blindness such that they could see neither Godâs blessing in their lives nor the degree to which their arrogance offended him. As a result, their devotion to him and his Church had become but a thin veneer. Thus the membersâ spiritual condition caused them, like their water, to be hard, lukewarm, and nasty.

So great was the sin of pride and the resultant ingratitude that they crowded out all the virtues the branch possessed. As a result, the Lord had nothing good to say about it. Their blindness was becoming so acute that they could not see their real spiritual conditionsâwretched, pitiful, poor, blind, and nakedâfrom which they could be saved only by the greatest effort on their part.

Given the depth of their sin, the Lordâs care of and promise to these lackluster Christians seems out of place unless we remember that his words are not to this branch alone, but to all the churches.66 Even so, he was not prepared to give up on them. At the very moment that they heard the content of his letter, he stood knocking at the door, waiting for them to invite him in. The implications of that imagery should have sounded a clear warning. The Lord was not with them. They had forced him outside. Even so, he was willing to return if they would respond to his knock and open the door. The move was theirs. If they took it, he would again dine with them, perhaps an allusion to the sacramental supper through which they could have his spirit to be with them always (see D&C 20:77). If they refused, they would ever remain spiritually wretched, pitiable, poor, blind, and naked.

Even today, the prophets have warned those who do not fulfill their callings or do so with halfheartedness that they too are in danger of being spewed out, or, as President Joseph F. Conclusion

The writings of the New Testament show that the early leaders had little hope the Church itself or even some of its branches would remain faithful. It was not, therefore, apostasy from the Church that they feared the most but the apostasy of the Church.69 Even so, they fought desperately against it, perhaps more as a means of delaying the inevitable and saving souls than by the belief that their efforts would really make a difference in the end.

From the content of the letters, it is apparent that the Churchâs spiritual life foundered in seven areas. Two were external: willingness to compromise with paganism and failure to stay true to the faith due to Jewish and pagan harassment. Five were internal: the acceptance of unauthorized leaders; approval of false doctrine promulgated by pseudoprophets; halfheartedness and indifference; a loss of love for the Church and its Master; and, finally, pride and self-conceit. Even as John wrote, various branches were succumbing to one or more of these pressures. And in fact, some congregations were already waving the white flag of surrender to the forces of the world.

The false apostles who appeared at Ephesus may have been migrant missionaries perhaps belonging to this same group.71 In short, these people appear to have been spiritual libertines working within the Church in Asia Minor. They set up a prophetic tradition that stood opposite that of John and those associated with him. They refused to recognize his authority or that of authorized representatives of the Lord. One thing is certain, they presented a real threat to the true Church. Many Saints were persuaded by the new seduction and not only left the truth but fostered the evil. Therefore, the Lord warned them through John to ârepent, and do the first works; or else I will come unto thee quickly, and will remove thy candlestick out of his placeâ (2:5).

The letters give but the slightest hint that the churches could or would survive this crisis. Though the Lord offered hope of salvation, this was not to the body of the Church but to its individuals. Indeed, for the most faithful among the branches, the church at Smyrna, the Lord holds no promise of continuance: âFear none of those things which thou shalt suffer: behold, the devil shall cast some of you into prison, that ye may be tried . . . be thou faithful unto death, and I will give thee a crown of lifeâ (2:10).

The Lordâs letters to the seven Churches give full evidence to the Lordâs concerns for both the congregations and the individual Saints. Even though the apostasy was moving forward with increasing success, he was still reaching out to branches and Saints in the hope that they would respond and return to him. There was good reason. The Church was at the point that by rejecting this outreach and refusing to come back to the true doctrine, they would bring upon themselves the promised judgments of God. The dilemma is that there is no way of saving them but by the very truth whose rejection will condemn them utterly. If the Lord, however, held off, refusing to offer assistance or show the way back as judgment, these fallen Saints could claim he had not given them a chance and thereby besmirch his name. By offering repentance and showing the way back, he left them, and them alone, fully responsible for their fate.

It is of note that he appeals to the leader of each branch, showing that he yet recognized their position and was still willing to work with them. Even so, as one scholar noted, the situation depended upon the Saintsâ response to Johnâs message. They had to follow him, for it was he who was in direct touch with God and through whom the Spirit of God controlled and guided the Saints. His words were literally the Lordâs words; he could speak and write with an authority such that he could say âThus saith the Spiritâ and thus make known the will of God.72

The urgent call to repentance grew out of the prospect of an eminent coming of the Lord, not in form of the great king with the blessed parousia but as a thief in the night who would take away the lampstands.73 It is important to note that the Saints would remain. The idea behind the imagery is that the Christians with their heresies were there to stay; it was the true Church that they were in danger of losing.

Johnâs commission was to publish the vision of God to the churches when apostasy and persecution were about to destroy them. While John was tethered to the isle of Patmos, counterfeit authorities made their way through the ranks of the Church. The pattern seems to follow the one portrayed in the Book of Mormon when âthe people did harden their hearts, for they were led by many priests and false prophets to build up many churches, and to do all manner of iniquityâ (4 Ne. 1:34). With all this, a power struggle developed between the false leaders and those leaders whom the Lord appointed known as the Three Nephites. Eventually, though the wicked could not destroy the three nor weaken their testimony, the apostate leaders were finally able to silence them, probably through banishment. The picture in Revelation is about the same. False leaders continually gaining authority and strength were finally able to silence Johnâs influence among the Church members. But they did not silence his testimony. His revelation endured in spite of their machinations.

As disconcerting as the letters to the seven churches were, Revelation brought with it hope, the same hope that allowed it to find place as the last book of the New Testament. To the faithful Saints living in the Roman province of Asia, John bequeathed, through the book of Revelation, a strong testimony that God lived and that Jesus was the Messiah. Excursus on the State of Apostasy at the End of the First Century

Acts chapter 1 through Revelation chapter 3 is the story of a hard-fought struggle that could not stop a heartbreaking defeat. In a word, primitive Christianity, despite all efforts to the contrary, was corrupted. As noted elsewhere in these volumes, the earliest Christians were well aware of the danger (see especially the volumes in commentary series dealing with Acts 20 and 2 Thes. 2). Even during his ministry, the Lord, through a parable, had warned the Saints that an apostasy was coming. His enemy, he explained, would destroy the Church by sowing tares (false beliefs) among the wheat (true doctrine), choking out the latter (see Matt. 13:24â30, 36â41). Modern scripture tells when these seeds of destruction were planted. According to Lord, with the demise of the Apostles, Satanâs earthly assistants sowed their seeds: âwherefore, the tares choke the wheat and [drove] the church into the wildernessâ (D&C 86:2â3). As John wrote, the evil sower was already at work.

Unfortunately, the ground was already fertile and ready for his seeds. The reason was that, after the death of the Lord, Christianity fractured. People began to bring in false doctrines and heresies (see 2 Pet. 2:1). Luke documents the beginning of these problems. He states, âCertain men which came down from Judea [to Antioch of Syria] taught the brethren, and said, Except ye be circumcised after the manner of Moses, ye cannot be savedâ (Acts 15:1). Those who continued to hold this view, in spite of the teachings of the Apostles to the contrary, became one of the strongest factions in the early Church, the Judaizers. These proved to be Paulâs greatest adversaries and greatly contributed to the difficulty of his work. But these were not the only ones to bring in teachings contrary to those of the Apostles. An antiresurrection party arose in Corinth, and over time, incipient Antinomianism, Docetism, and Gnosticism with other heresies made their way into the early Church.

Though the Apostles fought them with all their might, these forces did not weaken. The result was that, by the time of the writing of Revelation, it would be proper to speak not of Christians, but of Christianities. Bart Ehrmanâs studies illustrate the breadth and divergences of these early groups and chronicle the tension and struggle between them during the second and third centuries.74 In the end, those who called themselves the âorthodoxâ won the day and became the foundation for most of the latter-day Christian sects. But in the days of the Apostles, all these factions enriched the ground so that the tares they sowed during the last half of the first century took root and thrived. As a result, the Lordâs Church was driven from the field.

At the very time John was writing, the evil sower was working across the Christian fields. John was therefore not only writing prophecy but also seeing it fulfilled. Indeed, Paulâs prophecy that âa falling awayâ would be sandwiched between the Lordâs first and second comings was proving true (see 2 Thes. 2:1â5). Through chapter 3 of Revelation, the readers and hearers would have seen little or no hope of defeating the rebellion. They could not do that because an important key was not available to them. Sardis: In the sixth century BC, Sardis had been the most powerful city in the ancient Near Eastern world, yet at the time of Christ it had declined to the point that Ramsay could accurately describe it as âa relic of the period of barbaric warfare, which lived rather on its ancient prestige than on its suitability to present conditions.â1 The city was located about fifty miles east of Ephesus and a number of important trade routes passed through it, but more importantly, it was the terminus of the Royal Road. As a result, it was quite wealthy and able to support a standing population in excess of sixty thousand. From the spur of Mt. Timolus on which it sat, it dominated the fertile Hermus plain. The mountainâs summit was fifteen hundred feet above the plain and had sheer walls on three sides, making it an ideal citadel. Even so, it had fallen to enemies twice in its history. The last was to the soldiers of the city of Pergamum. As a result, it became one of that cityâs subjects and later part of the Roman province of Asia.

Its patron goddess was the Asiatic Cybele, renamed by the Greeks as Artemis. The goddess was worshipped because of her reputation as a healer and restorer of life. Due to that influence, the city had a reputation as the seat of life-giving power. Even so, it suffered a severe and deadly earthquake in AD 16. Her citizens were able to rebuild her only with a huge financial investment by the Emperor Tiberius Caesar.2

A main trade of the city was woolen goods. As a result, the citizens of Sardis were sensitive to unclean clothes, especially in relation to worship of their gods, insisting the dirty robes not only defiled the devotee but also dishonored the god.4

Analysis and Summary

The situation of the Saints mirrors that of the city. As Charles stated, âNo city in Asia presented a more deplorable contrast of past splendour and present unresting decline.â20 Further, its goddess was supposed to bring the blessing of life, but instead her cold and lifeless statue gave none. As with the city, so with the church. It, too, was resting on past glory coming from the time when its Christian neighbors saw it as the place where the spirit thrived and the light of the gospel burned warm and radiant. Though the glow of that period remained, it hid an actual pallor of death.

The Church at Sardis received the second most severe rebuke of all the Churches to which the Lord addressed letters. Only Laodicea surpassed it. The likely reason was that, though it was âuntroubled by heresy and free of outward opposition, it had so completely come to terms with its pagan environment that, although it retained its outward appearance of life, it was spiritually dead.â21 One scholar noted that it had become âthe perfect model of inoffensive Christianity.â22 The pagans were in harmony with the Christians primarily because they found nothing to object to. Simply put, the Christians bought peace at the price of standing for nothing. The once-guarded acceptance of some things pagan that had once been the virtue of the branch had opened the doors to full-scale tolerance. Such all-inclusive tolerance is the virtue of people who have nothing to stand for. As happens so often, tolerance had turned into license. The result was that there was little that separated the Christian from the pagan. In other words, the Sardis branch had compromised to the point that it was Christian in name only.

Those who gave themselves over to compromise had symbolically soiled their spiritual clothes. As noted above, the imagery would have meant much to those at Sardis. The textile makers knew well the loss of value when cloth became soiled. Further, religious practice demanded that worshippers appear before their god in clean clothes, for unclean garments defiled the worshipper and dishonored the god. Johnâs imagery, set as it was within a temple context, suggests it could have been priestly garments that the Christians had soiled. As such, faithless Saints doubly dishonored themselves and their God.23

The defiled priestly garments so central in the nightmare that afflicted the soul of the priest Zachariah, noted above, mirrors the sacred garments worn by those who have been endowed as well as the vestments actually worn during the temple service. Symbolically, despoiling these holy items would be breaking the covenants associated with them and thereby forfeiting the shielding and protecting power they offer.24

Remembering what they had left and repenting, however, could bring about a renewal of life.25 Otherwise, even the few living ones who remained would not be able to save the rest. Even so, those who were faithful would not lose their reward. In this case, the reward took the form of three blessings: first, they would be arrayed in white garments, very likely a sign of their triumph over their enemies; second, their names would remain in the divine ledger, thus assuring their citizenship in the eternal world; and third, Jesus would stand by them at the divine court, thus assuring the their full acquittal and access to eternal freedom.

Analysis and Summary

The Lord beckoned these people to look to the future when God would establish his kingdom and reverse the earthly conditions so hostile to his people. In that day, all will pay homage not only to the Lord but also to those who had been faithful to him. The promise of dominance over the Jews parallels that of dominance of the Jews over the Gentiles given earlier (see 2:26â27). Those Jews who scoffed at the idea that the Philadelphian Saints were the objects of Godâs love would be forced to see and acknowledge their error in the future.41

In the meantime, because of their faith, the Lord would spare the congregation from the immediate and earthshaking trial soon to come. His words show that he fully intended to preserve these Saints, not by taking them off the earth, but by protecting them from the machinations of Satan, which would hit the world as a flood.42 The light of priesthood would burn bright in this little congregation even as it was dimming and going out in other branches.

The Lordâs promise that he would come to them quickly takes on quite a different meaning than it did to the other branches. The Lord promised Ephesus that he would come quickly, but it would be to take away their lampstand. He promised Pergamum he would come quickly, but to fight against them with the sword of his mouth. Finally, he promised Sardis he would come quickly, but as a thief in the night with the implication that he would take away objects of value. To Philadelphia, conversely, his coming would signal the end of their hour of trial, but just how, he does not say.43

To those who overcame, the Lord gave a threefold promise: First, he would write the name of God upon them by which all would know that they were his special possession. Second, he would write upon them the name of the divine city, thus showing that they were citizens therein. Finally, he would write upon them his own new name, showing that they had not only became one with the Lord but were also like him.

Analysis and Summary

Though blessed as no other congregation, the Laodiceans were guilty of one of the gravest sins: pride and the hubris that grows out of it. The congregation as a whole seem to have been wealthy, secure, and at peace with its neighbors. It does not even seem to have been afflicted with the heresies that threatened to destroy other branches. The problem was that the Saints took credit for their peaceful station and rich condition. By taking credit for their blessings and believing they needed nothing from God, they became gods unto themselves. That act was nothing short of hubris and bordered on blasphemy.

Likely they could have marshaled solid evidence to support their case. Their view, however, was short-sighted and earthbound. The result was a growing spiritual blindness such that they could see neither Godâs blessing in their lives nor the degree to which their arrogance offended him. As a result, their devotion to him and his Church had become but a thin veneer. Thus the membersâ spiritual condition caused them, like their water, to be hard, lukewarm, and nasty.

So great was the sin of pride and the resultant ingratitude that they crowded out all the virtues the branch possessed. As a result, the Lord had nothing good to say about it. Their blindness was becoming so acute that they could not see their real spiritual conditionsâwretched, pitiful, poor, blind, and nakedâfrom which they could be saved only by the greatest effort on their part.

Given the depth of their sin, the Lordâs care of and promise to these lackluster Christians seems out of place unless we remember that his words are not to this branch alone, but to all the churches.66 Even so, he was not prepared to give up on them. At the very moment that they heard the content of his letter, he stood knocking at the door, waiting for them to invite him in. The implications of that imagery should have sounded a clear warning. The Lord was not with them. They had forced him outside. Even so, he was willing to return if they would respond to his knock and open the door. The move was theirs. If they took it, he would again dine with them, perhaps an allusion to the sacramental supper through which they could have his spirit to be with them always (see D&C 20:77). If they refused, they would ever remain spiritually wretched, pitiable, poor, blind, and naked.

Even today, the prophets have warned those who do not fulfill their callings or do so with halfheartedness that they too are in danger of being spewed out, or, as President Joseph F. Conclusion

The writings of the New Testament show that the early leaders had little hope the Church itself or even some of its branches would remain faithful. It was not, therefore, apostasy from the Church that they feared the most but the apostasy of the Church.69 Even so, they fought desperately against it, perhaps more as a means of delaying the inevitable and saving souls than by the belief that their efforts would really make a difference in the end.

From the content of the letters, it is apparent that the Churchâs spiritual life foundered in seven areas. Two were external: willingness to compromise with paganism and failure to stay true to the faith due to Jewish and pagan harassment. Even as John wrote, various branches were succumbing to one or more of these pressures. And in fact, some congregations were already waving the white flag of surrender to the forces of the world.

Therefore, it would seem that Jezebel and the Nicolaitans were part of the same heretical group working within the Church to pull people into their salacious ways. Their doctrine probably appealed to spiritual prostitution more than physical, but the imagery depicting extreme sensuality gives an accurate feel for their allure. The false apostles who appeared at Ephesus may have been migrant missionaries perhaps belonging to this same group.71 In short, these people appear to have been spiritual libertines working within the Church in Asia Minor. They set up a prophetic tradition that stood opposite that of John and those associated with him. They refused to recognize his authority or that of authorized representatives of the Lord. One thing is certain, they presented a real threat to the true Church. Many Saints were persuaded by the new seduction and not only left the truth but fostered the evil. Therefore, the Lord warned them through John to ârepent, and do the first works; or else I will come unto thee quickly, and will remove thy candlestick out of his placeâ (2:5).

The letters give but the slightest hint that the churches could or would survive this crisis. Though the Lord offered hope of salvation, this was not to the body of the Church but to its individuals. Indeed, for the most faithful among the branches, the church at Smyrna, the Lord holds no promise of continuance: âFear none of those things which thou shalt suffer: behold, the devil shall cast some of you into prison, that ye may be tried . . . be thou faithful unto death, and I will give thee a crown of lifeâ (2:10).

The Lordâs letters to the seven Churches give full evidence to the Lordâs concerns for both the congregations and the individual Saints. Even though the apostasy was moving forward with increasing success, he was still reaching out to branches and Saints in the hope that they would respond and return to him. There was good reason. The Church was at the point that by rejecting this outreach and refusing to come back to the true doctrine, they would bring upon themselves the promised judgments of God. The dilemma is that there is no way of saving them but by the very truth whose rejection will condemn them utterly. If the Lord, however, held off, refusing to offer assistance or show the way back as judgment, these fallen Saints could claim he had not given them a chance and thereby besmirch his name. By offering repentance and showing the way back, he left them, and them alone, fully responsible for their fate.

It is of note that he appeals to the leader of each branch, showing that he yet recognized their position and was still willing to work with them. Even so, as one scholar noted, the situation depended upon the Saintsâ response to Johnâs message. They had to follow him, for it was he who was in direct touch with God and through whom the Spirit of God controlled and guided the Saints. His words were literally the Lordâs words; he could speak and write with an authority such that he could say âThus saith the Spiritâ and thus make known the will of God.72

The urgent call to repentance grew out of the prospect of an eminent coming of the Lord, not in form of the great king with the blessed parousia but as a thief in the night who would take away the lampstands.73 It is important to note that the Saints would remain. The idea behind the imagery is that the Christians with their heresies were there to stay; it was the true Church that they were in danger of losing.

Johnâs commission was to publish the vision of God to the churches when apostasy and persecution were about to destroy them. While John was tethered to the isle of Patmos, counterfeit authorities made their way through the ranks of the Church. The pattern seems to follow the one portrayed in the Book of Mormon when âthe people did harden their hearts, for they were led by many priests and false prophets to build up many churches, and to do all manner of iniquityâ (4 Ne. 1:34). Further, âthey did not dwindle in unbelief, but they did willfully rebel against the gospel of Christâ (4 Ne. 1:38), and âthey did still continue to build up churches unto themselves, and adorn them with all manner of precious thingsâ (4 Ne. 1:41). Soon âthere were many churches in the land; yea, there were many churches which professed to know the Christ, and yet they did deny the more parts of his gospel, insomuch that they did receive all manner of wickedness, and did administer that which was sacred unto him to whom it had been forbidden because of unworthiness. And this church did multiply exceedingly because of iniquity, and because of the power of Satan who did get hold upon their heartsâ (4 Ne. 1:27â28).

With all this, a power struggle developed between the false leaders and those leaders whom the Lord appointed known as the Three Nephites. Death could not touch them, so the false churches had to move against them in other ways (4 Ne. 1:30â33). Eventually, though the wicked could not destroy the three nor weaken their testimony, the apostate leaders were finally able to silence them, probably through banishment. The picture in Revelation is about the same. False leaders continually gaining authority and strength were finally able to silence Johnâs influence among the Church members. But they did not silence his testimony. His revelation endured in spite of their machinations.

As disconcerting as the letters to the seven churches were, Revelation brought with it hope, the same hope that allowed it to find place as the last book of the New Testament. To the faithful Saints living in the Roman province of Asia, John bequeathed, through the book of Revelation, a strong testimony that God lived and that Jesus was the Messiah. His message was a light that pierced the darkness of the gathering apostasy and illuminated the eventual triumph of God. It reassured the Saints that the present distress was not the end of the war but of a single battle. Though God seemed vanquished, he was not and would turn seeming defeat into glorious victory. And Revelation showed how. Thus, it became a beacon of hope to the faithful until, one by one, they passed away. With each passing, the light of truth dimmed and finally went out. The hope they held to, however, never did, and it inspired generations to hang on to what they had and wait for the glorious day when all would be restored again.

Excursus on the State of Apostasy at the End of the First Century

Acts chapter 1 through Revelation chapter 3 is the story of a hard-fought struggle that could not stop a heartbreaking defeat. In a word, primitive Christianity, despite all efforts to the contrary, was corrupted. As noted elsewhere in these volumes, the earliest Christians were well aware of the danger (see especially the volumes in commentary series dealing with Acts 20 and 2 Thes. 2). Even during his ministry, the Lord, through a parable, had warned the Saints that an apostasy was coming. His enemy, he explained, would destroy the Church by sowing tares (false beliefs) among the wheat (true doctrine), choking out the latter (see Matt. 13:24â30, 36â41). Modern scripture tells when these seeds of destruction were planted. According to Lord, with the demise of the Apostles, Satanâs earthly assistants sowed their seeds: âwherefore, the tares choke the wheat and [drove] the church into the wildernessâ (D&C 86:2â3). As John wrote, the evil sower was already at work.

Unfortunately, the ground was already fertile and ready for his seeds. The reason was that, after the death of the Lord, Christianity fractured. People began to bring in false doctrines and heresies (see 2 Pet. 2:1). Luke documents the beginning of these problems. He states, âCertain men which came down from Judea [to Antioch of Syria] taught the brethren, and said, Except ye be circumcised after the manner of Moses, ye cannot be savedâ (Acts 15:1). Those who continued to hold this view, in spite of the teachings of the Apostles to the contrary, became one of the strongest factions in the early Church, the Judaizers. These proved to be Paulâs greatest adversaries and greatly contributed to the difficulty of his work. But these were not the only ones to bring in teachings contrary to those of the Apostles. An antiresurrection party arose in Corinth, and over time, incipient Antinomianism, Docetism, and Gnosticism with other heresies made their way into the early Church.

Though the Apostles fought them with all their might, these forces did not weaken. The result was that, by the time of the writing of Revelation, it would be proper to speak not of Christians, but of Christianities. Bart Ehrmanâs studies illustrate the breadth and divergences of these early groups and chronicle the tension and struggle between them during the second and third centuries.74 In the end, those who called themselves the âorthodoxâ won the day and became the foundation for most of the latter-day Christian sects. But in the days of the Apostles, all these factions enriched the ground so that the tares they sowed during the last half of the first century took root and thrived. As a result, the Lordâs Church was driven from the field.

At the very time John was writing, the evil sower was working across the Christian fields. John was therefore not only writing prophecy but also seeing it fulfilled. Indeed, Paulâs prophecy that âa falling awayâ would be sandwiched between the Lordâs first and second comings was proving true (see 2 Thes. 2:1â5). The word Paul used was á¼ÏÎ¿ÏÏÎ±ÏÎ¯Î± (apostasia). It carried quite a different nuance for him and his audience than it does for us today. It did not carry an explicitly religious nuance but described a very severe civil disturbance. Lexical definitions give âto stand awayâ or âto stand apartâ as well as âapostasy,â but all these fail to catch the main thrust in this context. The word connoted a rebellion in which the authorized leaders with their constitution were replaced by unlawful leaders who brought in a new constitution. But of what did the Apostles understand the mass rebellion would consist? There is no doubt that they saw it as a departing from the living God, but just how?75 Being grounded in the Old Testament and more particularly the LXX, they would have understood the rebellion as expressing itself in the breaking of covenants with God, more particularly âas the loss of a personâs or a peopleâs status as priests serving God in [the] temple.â76 Thereby, they saw that Old Testament apostasy was rebellion against God that expressed itself in the breaking of covenant and the rejection of priesthood service. With the fulfilling of the Mosaic law in Christ, priesthood service devolved upon all worthy males (see 1:6; 5:10; Heb. 7:11â17). To reject God was to reject âa service that was, in early Christianity, best revealed in temple priesthood and ordinances.â77 The apostasy, then, resulted in the loss of the priesthood with its attendant ordinances and rites.78 It is little wonder, in that light, that Revelation, which was designed to fight against this trend, is filled with temple imagery, and that the great sin of incipient Gnosticism was professing to know the mysteries of God when the best they could do was come up with a counterfeit.79

Through chapter 3 of Revelation, the readers and hearers would have seen little or no hope of defeating the rebellion. They could not do that because an important key was not available to them. To understand what this was, one needs but look at our own history. Just seven years after the organization of the latter-day Church, apostasy threatened to destroy it. Rebels were plotting the death of Joseph Smith and how to take over the reins of the Church. The Church was saved by two forces: the English mission and the gathering of the Saints. The latter particularly allowed for control while the doctrine and practices of the Church were being established. The early Church never had that advantage. The situation got out of hand very rapidly as people took what they wanted but left out the hard sayings.

Revelation shows the Lordâs Church was on the brink of self-destruction. Unauthorized leaders and self-appointed prophets were gaining strength and taking over whole Christian congregations. In other words, the rebellion was succeeding. Only an immediate and full repentance would save the few from falling to the powers of the apostates. The reality of the apostasy did not take the authentic Christian leaders by surprise. They had known it was coming for decades. As early as the days of the apostolic ministry, the leaders were well aware that an apostasy would be sandwiched between the Lordâs first and second comings. They did their best to warn the congregations (see, for example, Acts 20:28â31; 2 Thes. 2:3â5; 2 Pet. 2:1â3; 1 Jn. 3:18â19). Both modern Catholics and Protestants are well aware of the apostasy passages found in the gospel record and recognize the event as more than just a nasty thorn in the side of Christianity. A Roman Catholic Bible (see the NAB) translates âfalling awayâ in 2 Thessalonians 2:3 as âmass apostasy,â while Protestant scholars recognize that Paul taught that a âworld-wide rebellionâ would take place.80 However, because of their belief that these prophecies refer to the time just before the coming of the Lord, they do not accept the idea of an apostasy of the early Church.81 As a result, they do not understand that the trial that was soon to overtake the world of the early Church was the loss of priesthood and clarity of the gospel. Instead, they insist that it refers to the work of the anti-Christ found in the other chapters of Revelation. Those trials, however, do not come under the rubric of âthings that must shortly come to passâ (1:1), but rather under those things that âmust be hereafterâ (4:1).
