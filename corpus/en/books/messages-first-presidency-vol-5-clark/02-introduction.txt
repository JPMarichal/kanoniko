James R. Clark, Messages of the First Presidency, vol. 5 ( 1971)

Introduction

This volume contains the Messages of the First Presidency during the period 1916-1934. It covers the last three years of the administration of President Joseph F. Smith and the first sixteen years of the administration of President Heber J. Grant as Presidents of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints and Prophets, Seers, and Revelators to the Church and the world.

If one should seek to characterize the last three years of the mortal life of President Joseph F. Smith, perhaps it could best be done in his own words in the April 1916 General Conference of the Church:

Sometimes the Lord expands our vision from this point of view and this side of the veil, so that we feel and seem to realize that we can look beyond the thin veil which separates us from that other sphere. If we can see, by the enlightening influence of the Spirit of God, beyond the veil that separates us from the spirit world, surely those who have passed beyond, can see more clearly through the veil back here to us than it is possible for us to see them from our sphere of action. I believe we move and have our being in the presence of heavenly messengers and of heavenly beings.

The content of the majority of the Messages of the First Presidency during the last three years of President Smith's life would certainly bear out the fact that for him that veil was thin indeed.

The Messages in this volume from the administration of President Heber J. Grant come in the period between the two great World Wars I and II. The period was one of recovery from World War I and its aftermath the "Great Depression." The "Roaring Twenties" was followed by the limping thirties.

The Lord had a prophet ready for his times, in the person of President Heber J Grant, as He has always had.

Writing of President Heber J. Grant, in 1930, Brigham H. Roberts, "Mormon" historian said:

It is no disparagement to say that President Grant is not a highly imaginative person; intellectually speculative, or a constructive theorist, or largely doctrinaireÂ�.

President Grant would likely say, and reason it out in this fashion; 'What doth it profit, my brethren, though a man may say he hath faith and have not works, can faith save him? If a brother or sister be naked, and destitute of daily food and one of you say unto them, depart in peace, be ye warmed and filled, notwithstanding ye give them not those things which are needful to the body; what doth it profit?'. . .

In a word, President Grant's message to his people and to his times, judged by the temperament of him, and the stern practical nature of him; is emphatically and is likely to continue to be, practical; and will be reflected by his oft repeated admonition:

"Latter-day Saints, keep the commandments of God." (CHC 6:484-489.)

Six years later, in 1936, when President Grant and his counselors in the First Presidency issued a message to the Church on the newly inaugurated Church Security Plan it contained phrases such as these:

The aim of the Church is to help the people to help themselves.

Our primary purpose was to set up, in so far as it might be possible, a system under which the curse of idleness would be done away with, the evils of a dole abolished, and independence, industry, thrift and self respect be once more established amongst our peopleÂ�. (CR October, 1936, p. 2.)

Brigham H. Roberts may have been right in 1930 that President Heber J. Grant was not particularly a "doctrinaire" man, but he was not unacquainted with the history and doctrine of the Church over which he had been called to preside. One doctrinal problem continued to be raised throughout his administration as it had during the administrations of his predecessors since 1890. The problem was how to convince certain people, including John and Lorin Woolley, that the Church neither practiced nor countenanced the practicing of plural marriage! On June 17, 1933, President Heber J. Grant and his counselors, Anthony W. Ivins and J. Reuben Clark, Jr., issued an Official Statement with an accompanying cover letter and instructed each Bishop in each Ward of the Church "to have both these documents carefully and clearly read" at a specially designated sacrament service and then gave instructions that:

Each President of Stake and each Bishop will proceed immediately to correct any situation of the kind described and existing within his jurisdiction.

This volume covers largely the between-wars years. The next volume begins as the groundwork for the Church Welfare Program was being laid. JAMES R. CLARK, ED. D.
