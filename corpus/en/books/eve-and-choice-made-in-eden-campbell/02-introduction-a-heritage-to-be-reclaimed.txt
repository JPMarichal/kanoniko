Introduction: A Heritage to Be Reclaimed

The story of Eden, the Garden, and Adam and Eve is of unusual and equal importance to both women and men, for it significantly affects how we perceive one another and how we behave towards one another. It shapes and colors our expectations along with our actions. If such actions and expectations are based on faulty premises, limitations are imposed, strengths are denied, and relationships are misarticulated daily. We all know that even the smallest error in the foundation of a building can eventually bring it down. Errors in understanding the Eden story are not small, and until they are corrected, ignorance of their presence will continue to be manifest in grossly enlarged consequences to our social fabric.

Because men have different experiences from women, they perhaps cannot fully understand the profound effect an incorrect or incomplete understanding of the subject of Eve continues to have on women, on men, and on society as a whole. The perusal of just a few topical sources gives us a glimpse into the general tenor of society, for much of literature and most histories referring to women have an undercurrent of apology, as though there is something not quite right about being female. In looking for the source of this unease, I came to recognize that it could be traced to accounts of the Creation and to ever-prevalent and negative characterizations of the first woman who dressed the garden in Eden, Mother Eve.

To illustrate, a article begins: "Scientists are calling her Eve, but reluctantly. The name evokes too many wrong imagesthe weak-willed figure in Genesis, the milk-skinned beauty in Renaissance art, the voluptuary gardener in 'Paradise Lost' who was all 'softness' and 'meek surrender.'"1

And a full-page article in the stated unequivocally: "The story of Eve in the book of Genesis has had a more profoundly negative impact on women throughout history than any other."2

While attending a meeting at a well-known university, I saw a campus sign: "Sidewalk Art Contest." The artwork, interesting and varied, included an entry depicting Adam and Eve. Two personable young men were drawing the snake. Its tongue was lapping up towards Eve. Eve was looking ashamed. Adam was looking put upon.

"Tell me the story, " I said.

"You know the story," they replied.

When I asked for their interpretation, they responded, in effect, "You know she shouldn't have done it."

I asked, "Was there another way?"

They answered, "You bet there was. She really blew it."

In a magazine, we find a cartoon depicting Eve and the serpent in bed. The serpent says: "I thought while he's in there brushing his teeth, it would be a good opportunity for us to talk."3

Funny? Not to a woman! Images and ideas following this theme are so much a part of our culture that even in this time of political correctness we accept them and laugh at them, barely noticing their inaccuracies or the consequent harm such images perpetuate.

Effects of the Story

Some would ask, "Does the biblical story of Eve still affect women in the twenty-first century? Can we not see through any error or work around it?" Surprisingly, it seems not. In my research I came across a book written by a woman psychotherapist. Although I knew little of the author or her previous works, I was interested to open her book to a page that related a story of a weekend workshop made up of "competent and influential women." She speaks of the respect accorded them in their chosen fields. The women "appeared confident and self-assured and were obviously intelligent and skillful. As the weekend wore on, each woman began to reveal basic feelings of self-doubt. Each of these women drove themselves to be successfuland in the eyes of the world, they were. Yet, each ... revealed that down deep she there was something wrong with her."

This author explains that as she worked with these women, she suddenly became aware that what she was witnessing were attempts to cope with what she calls "The Original Sin of Being Born Female." She continues: "According to the theological concept of original sin, there is no real justification by works. There is no 'right thing' we can do to atone for it even though we spend much of our time and creative energy trying to."4

Such feelings are surely not part of God's plan for His daughters. They find no support in the correct teachings of the gospel of Jesus Christ. The purpose of the gospel is to teach us to become more godlike. To do this, God has vested each one of us with dignity and majesty. Repeatedly in modern scripture the Lord extends incredible promises to all who will qualify through obedience and worthiness. The Lord is inclusive, not exclusive (see D&C 38:39; 93:1). This dignity and majesty is invalidated, however, as the world accepts skewed concepts of His precious daughter Eve and therefore of all her daughters everywhere.

In this vesting of worth, it is not enough to honor wives, mothers, and daughters. We must value and honor A five-part series entitled "Third World, Second Class" appeared in the Readers recoiled in horror at the injustice, discrimination, and actual murders of women, starting at the time of birth. I will relate just one story from this series:

"When Rani returned home from the hospital cradling her newborn daughter, the men in the family slipped out of her mud hut while she and her mother-in-law mashed poisonous oleander seeds into a dollop of oil and forced it down the infant's throat. [Only male babies were acceptable.]

"As soon as darkness fell, Rani crept into a nearby field and buried her baby girl in a shallow, unmarked grave next to a small stream."5

How could a woman child's life be so little valued, so freely, almost routinely, snuffed out? How do we correct a society whose thinking is so far off course?

In a powerful address, Elder Russell M. Nelson taught that understanding the events of the Creation has a direct bearing on our behavior here and now.6 The key to that understanding is an accurate picture of the events surrounding the actions taken by Mother Eve in Eden. There is no question that we can initiate a course correction by restoring Mother Eve to her rightful role in the grand planand in that process begin to reclaim the value that God has placed on all His daughters, in all ages, at all times.

It is unfortunate that vital truths so carefully articulated by prophets of God are not more widely known or more fully integrated into our society. In secular fashion the story of the Fall as taught today is often passed on with its subtle concepts of guilt, shame, and retribution. It is not surprising, then, that recent informal surveys of believing men and women indicated that they perceive Eve as a sinner, reasoning that God would have found another, less harsh way for mankind to claim mortality had Eve not partaken of the fruit. The inevitable conclusion was that the Fall, a sacramental symbol foreordained of God, was not necessary.

Amazed at the urgency I felt as the research progressed, I pondered, "Why do I feel this growing impression of its importance?" It soon became apparent that the truths taught by the prophets are a necessary part of the restoration of things. Our prophets have spoken with clarity and reverence on this subject, many venturing into seemingly uncharted waters to dispel misconceptions concerning Eve and her role, for reasons essential to the wholeness of our very souls.

A Reality, a Symbol, and a Model

The story of Eden should be viewed at no fewer than four levels. First, this story is based on historical fact. Adam and Eve were real beings. Theirs was a spirit creation followed by a spiritual creation, at which time their spirits were joined with physical bodies, but these bodies were not yet mortal. This period was a time of learning; however, a fall to mortality was necessary if waiting spirits were to claim bodies that were both physical and mortal. This creation and Adam and Eve's actions leading thereto and initiated thereafter are the cornerstone of the grand plan of salvation.

Second, the story is filled with symbols and metaphors that are rich and powerful. As in all grand drama, they have been carefully crafted to indelibly etch on our minds mental pictures that are separate from words. You must seek to see their meaning. The "serpent," the "tree," the "forbidden fruit," the "rib," their "nakedness," are all profoundly symbolic of unchanging moral concepts.

Third, this story must meet each of us where we are in our spiritual life today. It expects that we will begin our own search to find its relevance to our own life, to see wherein there is a model for our own actions.

Most important, this story assures us of the reality of an eternal God, who is literally our Father and who created the world. It brings into clear focus the purpose of this mortal life. From this story we learn that our presence here is part of a vast continuum. We understand that having been part of the grand plan, we bring with us to this earth an entire compendium of experience with attendant strengths and weaknesses, traits and talents. We understand the necessity and desirability of our own tests and trials when we recognize the earth as a vast university. We accept that we are moving forward on our individual tutorial, each one a complex and intense program that will deny us none of the challenges and growth necessary to our eternal advancement.

The promised Savior becomes a living reality. Our awareness of His atoning sacrifice increases the gratitude we feel for His absolute love. The story assures us that we may return to that selfsame Heavenly Father who sent us. On that return we can depend. Through the Savior's atonement and through our repentance and obedience, we will report on our joys and our sorrows, and move on to our own eternal life.

The Prophet Joseph Smith declared Adam to be second only to the Savior in powers and priesthood. Much has been written and spoken of him, but the task at hand is to examine vital insights and clarifying information about his glorious companion, Eve. As myths, cultures and translational bias are pierced by the truth and light of the restored gospel, I hope all will come to know, as I have, that a righteous, brilliant, vital woman was chosen, called, and tutored by God, loved and honored and cherished by Adam, and trusted by the spirits in the Grand Council to act with her magnificent eternal companion in their behalf.

My training is not as a scholar or as a writer. I am simply one who has always believed in the Lord and has claimed the promise that if we search diligently, with a prayerful heart, knowledge will be added, line upon line and precept upon precept. I learned in just that way, finding in our restored scriptures and in the recorded words of prophets, both modern and ancient, a line, a precept, a principle, a paragraph or a chapter, each lending clear insight and careful detail about the true beauty, majesty, mission, character, and righteousness of Mother Eve.

The words of President Boyd K. Packer have been instructional and encouraging: "One must prospect through and dig into the scriptures like a miner searching for precious metal. Scattered here and there are nuggets, some pure, some alloyed with other doctrines. Occasionally one strikes a rich vein which will yield all that one is willing to earn, for the scriptures do not explain nor interpret themselves."7

Armed with a definition of truth as defined in the Doctrine and Covenants"Truth is knowledge of things as they are, and as they were, and as they are to come" (D&C 93:24)I determined to see this work through to its end.

Being a practical thinker, I find answers best through questions. You will therefore find this work laced with questions. In my research I identified twenty key concepts pertaining to our purpose and mission that relate to the profound story of the Garden of Eden. Misunderstood, these concepts can compromise our identity, our relationships, our actions, and, therefore, our mission. Understood properly, they can elevate, unite, illuminate, and bring into clear focus the reasons for our earthly existence.

These questions are listed on the next two pages. Following them is a place to record your current thinking. I encourage you to take the time to do so. When you finish reading this book, you may wish to look back at your answers to these questions to see if your understanding has changed or been enlarged.

I believe that women and men will be better able to articulate their roles, understand their lives, accept their responsibilities, and complete their missions in mortality when they have a clearer understanding of the priesthood and powers of Father Adam, a more exalted view of Mother Eve, and a greater appreciation for the beauty of the partnership of our first parents. Our culture and our consciousness can be transformed, individual by individual, as we come to know truth. Elder B. H. Roberts counseled: "To be known, the truth must be stated, and the clearer and more complete the statement is, the better opportunity will the Holy Spirit have for testifying to the souls of men that the work is true."8
