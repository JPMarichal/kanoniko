Robert L. Millet, Alive in Christ: The Miracle of Spiritual Rebirth ( 1997)

Chapter 9

The Power of the Word

Perhaps the earliest Church memory I have is sitting with my father in a chapel in Baton Rouge, Louisiana, trying to listen to the wire broadcast of the priesthood meeting at general conference time. There was no video coverage, and sessions over the radio were limited in our area to the priesthood meeting. It was often difficult to understand the speakers because of the static. I remember one night in particular when my dad turned to me and whispered, "Listen carefully, son. This is the prophet." The prophet was David O. McKay. I did listen, and I can still recall, decades later, what I felt at the time. My heart beat faster as I felt an inner assurance that this indeed was the Lord's mouthpiece. President McKay's words sank deep into my heart.

When I was about ten years old, I remember very vividly, my Sunday School teacher was Sister Anderson. I don't know much more about her than her name. Because anyone over twenty seems old to young people, it seemed at the time that she was pretty aged. From my present perspective, however, I would guess that she was in her late forties. I don't remember any specific doctrines or precepts she taught during the time I was in her class, but I do remember as though it were yesterday what I felt when she bore her testimony. She knew. And I knew she knew. She said on one occasion: "Someday you will know the gospel is true just as I know, and then you can teach young people, just as I am teaching you now." I felt those words, and something deep down inside of me whispered to me that what she was saying was right and true.

I could relate many similar stories from my past, and each would illustrate key moments in my life where the word—the power of human testimony—was vital in my spiritual growth. The word, either spoken or written, is very often the agent of the new birth, one of the means by which we begin to become new creatures in Christ. Zeezrom the crafty lawyer was transformed from cynical accuser to penitent believer through the power of the words spoken by Alma and Amulek (Alma 11-12). Saul of Tarsus was no doubt touched by the testimony of Stephen. Was he not reflecting on Stephen's transfiguration and on the power of his testimony on the road to Damascus? (Acts 7; 9; McKay, Ancient Apostles, 120-21). There is a tradition to the effect that young Joseph Smith Jr. may have heard a sermon preached on James 1:5-6, a sermon that moved him to read and ponder on the scriptural passage himself (McConkie, New Witness, 5). And so on. Nephi explained that "when a man speaketh by the power of the Holy Ghost the power of the Holy Ghost carrieth it unto the hearts of the children of men" (2 Nephi 33:1).

The Apostle Paul wrote about the importance of human testimony: "For whosoever shall call upon the name of the Lord shall be saved. How then shall they call on him in whom they have not believed? and how shall they believe in him of whom they have not heard? and how shall they hear without a preacher? And how shall they preach, except they be sent? as it is written, How beautiful are the feet of them that preach the gospel of peace, and bring glad tidings of good things! . . . So then faith cometh by hearing, and hearing by the word of God" (Romans 10:13-15, 17). The Prophet Joseph Smith offered this clarification: "Faith comes by hearing the word of God, through the testimony of the servants of God; that testimony is always attended by the Spirit of prophecy and revelation" (Teachings of the Prophet Joseph Smith, 148).

The words of uninspired men may be witty but are seldom wise; they may be clever but are seldom substantive. The word of God, spoken by him or by one of his servants, gets to the heart of matters by going straight to the heart. Mormon taught that the word of God is "quick and powerful" and that it divides asunder "all the cunning and the snares and the wiles of the devil." It leads the man of Christ "in a strait and narrow course across that everlasting gulf of misery which is prepared to engulf the wicked—and land their souls, yea, their immortal souls, at the right hand of God in the kingdom of heaven" (Helaman 3:29-30; compare Hebrews 4:12). Thus Mormon wrote, "And now, as the preaching of the word had a great tendency to lead the people to do that which was just—yea, it had had more powerful effect upon the minds of the people than the sword, or anything else, which had happened unto them—therefore Alma thought it was expedient that they should try the virtue of the word of God" (Alma 31:5).

There is great virtue, great power in the word of God. Many, many times people are turned around in their tracks—converted—through the servants of God bearing down in pure testimony (Alma 4:19). Enos reported in his day, and this would certainly be true of our own time as well, that "there was nothing save it was exceeding harshness, preaching and prophesying of wars, and contentions, and destructions, and continually reminding them of death, and the duration of eternity, and the judgments and the power of God, and all these things—stirring them up continually to keep them in the fear of the Lord. I say there was nothing short of these things, and exceedingly great plainness of speech, would keep them from going down speedily to destruction" (Enos 1:23).

The power of the word of God lies not in its sophistication nor in its appeal to the worldly-wise; rather, the power flows from God through his appointed spokesmen to those who will receive it. "For our gospel [comes] not unto you in word only, but also in power, and in the Holy Ghost, and in much assurance" (1 Thessalonians 1:5). Paul wrote to the Corinthians: "And I, brethren, when I came to you, came not with excellency of speech or of wisdom, declaring unto you the testimony of God. For I determined not to know any thing among you, save Jesus Christ, and him crucified. And I was with you in weakness, and in fear, and in much trembling. And my speech and my preaching was not with enticing words of man's wisdom, but in demonstration of the Spirit and of power: that your faith should not stand in the wisdom of men, but in the power of God" (1 Corinthians 2:1-5).

In that spirit, Brigham Young described the manner in which his heart was stirred and his soul converted through simple testimony: "If all the talent, tact, wisdom, and refinement of the world had been sent to me with the Book of Mormon, and had declared, in the most exalted of earthly eloquence, the truth of it, undertaking to prove it by learning and worldly wisdom, they would have been to me like the smoke which arises only to vanish away. But when I saw a man without eloquence, or talents for public speaking, who could only say, 'I know, by the power of the Holy Ghost, that the Book of Mormon is true, that Joseph Smith is a Prophet of the Lord,' the Holy Ghost proceeding from that individual illuminated my understanding, and light, glory, and immortality were before me. I was encircled by them, filled with them, and I knew for myself that the testimony of the man was true. . . . My own judgment, natural endowments, and education bowed to this simple, but mighty testimony" (in Journal of Discourses, 1:90).

The word of God transcends the things that would offend a person concerned more with style than with substance. Elder Boyd K. Packer stated: "When once I tried to thank a great teacher and patriarch, William E. Berrett, he quickly passed the credit back to one who had taught him. An old convert from Norway was called to teach a group of mischievous Aaronic Priesthood boys. They were greatly amused by his broken English, but somehow the Spirit polished his words and soon the boys responded. I have heard Brother Berrett testify on more than one occasion, 'We could warm our hands by the fire of his faith'" (That All May Be Edified, 146).

The word of God is sufficiently powerful that gospel teachers or preachers do not need to assume the burden of converting their listeners. There is sufficient for the gospel teacher to do by way of reading, studying, preparing, praying, organizing, and presenting that he or she need not feel an obligation to "create" a spiritual experience. In short, there is enough for a teacher to do so that he or she need not usurp the role of the Holy Ghost. Ultimately, he is the teacher. He is the converter. He is the member of the eternal Godhead charged with carrying the word of truth into the hearts and minds of the children of men. He is the agent of the new birth, the one who sanctifies and empowers human beings. President Howard W. Hunter charged religious educators: "Give your students gospel truth powerfully taught; that is the way to give them a spiritual experience. Let it come naturally and as it will, perhaps with the shedding of tears, but perhaps not. If what you say is the truth, and you say it purely and with honest conviction, those students will feel the spirit of the truth being taught them and will recognize that inspiration and revelation has come into their hearts. That is how we build faith. That is how we strengthen testimonies—with the power of the word of God taught in purity and with conviction" (Eternal Investments, 3).

Often the person declaring the word of God does not know the effect of his or her declarations. Each one of us responds differently to the word of truth, and it is not unusual for the spokesman to be unaware of the good that has been done. I have often reflected upon the great prophet Abinadi in the Book of Mormon. Abinadi, like Stephen (Acts 7), submitted to a cruel death at the hands of wicked men. It is not easy to die, even for one's testimony, but the taste of death is much sweeter to those whose lives and words bear fervent witness of him whose servants they are. Frequently the Lord requires certain of his representatives to shed their blood in a martyr's death that their testament might be in full force (Hebrews 9:16-17; D&C 135:5). And so in the case of Abinadi, the testator was put to death. But the testament lived on. Faith had come to one who had heard the word of God preached by a legal administrator. As recorded by Mormon, some time during Abinadi's sermon a young man's heart was touched and a witness planted to the truthfulness of his words; a "young man . . . believed the words which Abinadi had spoken" (Mosiah 17:2). Because of the power of the word, the power of a single testimony, the course of events in the Nephite story was changed.

My friend Robert Matthews shared an experience with me that points up the individual nature of conversion and of how the Holy Spirit customizes lessons for people. At the end of a college course on the Pearl of Great Price, Brother Matthews asked the students to write on their final examination what had touched them the most in their study of this book of scripture. He was stunned when he read one student's response: "The thing that touched me the most was when we learned that Abraham's father was inactive and that Abraham made it anyway." Brother Matthews was certain that he had not mentioned anything about this matter in class and that not one of the students had brought it up. Someone's need to perceive this important verity was met by the individual tutor we know as the Spirit, and the timely lesson was incorporated.

A tender experience of my own also illustrates the power of the word. Many years ago I taught the New Testament in seminary. As we were studying the last week of the Savior's life, I organized a "last supper" somewhat akin to what Jesus and the Twelve enjoyed the night before his crucifixion. It was not an elaborate affair, but students brought prepared lamb, unleavened bread, and bitter herbs. As a class we read and discussed the Master's teachings on that fateful night, and we each partook of the different foods in order to focus more carefully on that sacred occasion. At the end of our discussion I bore witness of the living reality of Jesus the Christ and of how he, the Lamb of God, stands ready to receive us. It was a sweet moment, and I sensed that many were touched by the power of testimony in the room.

A few years later I taught a New Testament course in an institute of religion about two thousand miles away. I thought back on what a nice experience we had had in seminary and decided to duplicate the experience in institute. It went very well. Again, at the close of our discussion I bore my testimony and shared my convictions as to the vital importance of Jesus Christ. I invited each of the students to come unto Christ and yield their hearts unto him. Once more the Spirit of the Lord was poured out in a noticeable manner, and we all rejoiced together in what we had felt. I learned a few weeks later that during the middle of our meal a young man named Michael had happened into the institute building, had noticed what we were doing, and had been offended. He was Jewish, and he supposed at first that we were mocking a sacred ceremony. The longer he stayed and the more he heard, however, the more he was touched. He told me later that when I bore my testimony of Christ the witness came into his heart that Jesus of Nazareth was indeed the promised Messiah. He was baptized into the Church shortly thereafter.

Michael left on a mission. On one occasion, during companion study, he asked his companion how he had come to know of the truthfulness of the gospel. The missionary from Salt Lake City answered that it was while attending a seminary class in which the teacher had recreated the Last Supper and then borne testimony of Christ. Michael responded with astonishment that this was exactly what had happened to him. Within a few moments they discovered they were both talking about the same teacher. I rejoiced with these young men when they told me how the Spirit had conveyed a humble testimony into their hearts in a way that touched and taught and led to conversion.

The word of God is direct and powerful. It is tough on the wicked and hard-hearted (1 Nephi 16:2; 2 Nephi 1:26; 9:40; 33:5) and at the same time soothing and healing to the wounded soul (Jacob 2:8). Samuel the Lamanite testified concerning the power of holy writ to transform human souls: "Ye do know of yourselves, for ye have witnessed it, that as many of them [Lamanites] as are brought to the knowledge of the truth, and to know of the wicked and abominable traditions of their fathers, and are led to believe the holy scriptures, yea, the prophecies of the holy prophets, which are written, which leadeth them to faith on the Lord, and unto repentance, which faith and repentance bringeth a change of heart unto them—therefore, as many as have come to this, ye know of yourselves are firm and steadfast in the faith, and in the thing wherewith they have been made free" (Helaman 15:7-8).

There is consummate power in scripture, whether that scripture be written or spoken (D&C 68:3-4). "Often we spend great effort," President Ezra Taft Benson affirmed, "in trying to increase the activity levels in our stakes. We work diligently to raise the percentages of those attending sacrament meetings. We labor to get a higher percentage of our young men on missions. We strive to improve the numbers of those marrying in the temple. All of these are commendable efforts and important to the growth of the kingdom. But when individual members and families immerse themselves in the scriptures regularly and consistently, these other areas of activity will automatically come. Testimonies will increase. Commitment will be strengthened. Families will be fortified. Personal revelation will flow. . . .

"Success in righteousness, the power to avoid deception and resist temptation, guidance in our daily lives, healing of the soul—these are but a few of the promises the Lord has given to those who will come to His word. Does the Lord promise and not fulfill? Surely if He tells us that these things will come to us if we lay hold upon His word, then the blessings can be ours. And if we do not, then the blessings may be lost. However diligent we may be in other areas, certain blessings are to be found only in the scriptures, only in coming to the word of the Lord and holding fast to it as we make our way through the mists of darkness to the tree of life" ("Power of the Word," Ensign, May 1986, 81-82).
