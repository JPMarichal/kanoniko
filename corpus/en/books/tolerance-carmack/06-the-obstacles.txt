John K. Carmack, Tolerance: Principles, Practices, Obstacles, Limits ( 1993)

Chapter Five

The Obstacles

Fanaticism consists in redoubling your efforts when you have forgotten your aim.

-George Santayana

Tolerance may have improved somewhat in the past century, but we still find frustration, rage, violence, and ethnic unrest near the surface, bubbling out here and there and ready to erupt into a human volcano when faced with some new provocation. Why should this be so? What are the obstacles to more universal tolerance, patience, and kindness?

As we have already discovered, men and women often exercise tolerance in the weaker sense of the word-passively and neutrally. Tolerance of that kind does not mean the active, involved, roll-up-your-sleeves doctrine Christ taught and exemplified. If the universal mind-set were more empathetic and action-oriented, the world would be different. Official intolerance by governments has stifled tolerance in much of the world. Legal and policy barriers to freedoms of religion, speech, and press lock the doors to tolerance.

Most human beings do not understand or accept the principles underlying tolerance. Few know that we have a Heavenly Father with whom we lived before our birth and that our spirits are his children, making us literally brothers and sisters. The widely honored and accepted theory of evolution holds that humans are a product of chemicals and elements of the earth that came together by natural means and evolutionary processes. Many do not acknowledge the function of God in the creation process. Survival of the fittest, rather than mutual regard and cooperation, is the consequence of those ideas.

Even the commandment that people should love God with all their hearts has often been twisted and carried to extremes, thus becoming an instrument of intolerance rather than a guide to tolerant and peaceful living as intended. The resulting intolerant attitude goes something like this: "If you don't worship God the 'right' way, you are not welcome in our community."

A large number of women and men around the world live in conditions of abject poverty. Corruption and unfairness in governments create a barrier to opportunities for the people. Tolerance nearly vanishes under such conditions.

Without knowledge and acceptance of the principles of tolerance, women and men do not develop the attitudes and practices that tolerant people achieve in their lives. Is there no hope, then, for tolerance? My thesis is that hope lies in understanding and applying the doctrine of tolerance. The attitudes and practices need to be spread among the nations and be practiced widely by people. In this way obstacles can gradually be torn down.

The reasons why we do not enjoy more tolerance are many and complex, but the major obstacles we face are identified and discussed in the following paragraphs.

Religious Fanaticism

Sad to say, religious fanaticism deserves its placement at the top of the list of obstacles to tolerance. Much organized intolerance that has turned ugly and dangerous to human life has come as a direct result of religious extremism. Unbalanced views, excessive enthusiasm and zeal, and uncritical devotion often characterize fanatics. Finley Peter Dunne's Irish character Mr. Dooley defined a fanatic as "a man that does what he thinks th' Lord wud do if He knew th' facts iv th' case."R1 [^1]

It's true that much modern official intolerance has been secular rather than religious in nature. Totalitarian regimes still victimize churches and religious individuals. But religious extremists have too often turned communities into bloody factions during world history. Christians carried out many of the pogroms against the Jews. The so-called "final solution" of Adolph Hitler was not a religious pogrom, but Christian crusaders and inquisitors clearly carried out their campaigns of intolerance and violence in the name of religion. Religion has often lost its true meaning and balance in history, descending in the process into fanaticism.

Even now there is religious fanaticism and resulting intolerance at the root of unrest in the Middle East, the former Yugoslavia, Kashmir, Afghanistan, Myanmar (Burma), and Ireland.

Religious fanaticism is a major player in the scene of unrest and war. Governmental policies that favor one religion over others still force millions into silence. The task of labeling religion in the 1990s as an "obstacle to tolerance" is a dreary and melancholy duty, but the label belongs there. Most religions consider tolerance as a cornerstone belief, but major problems persist with those that do not.

Fanaticism existed in biblical times. Jesus fought against excesses and extremes of Jewish religious leaders in his day. He warned against the deadly formalism of Pharisees and scribes operating in Israel. Summing up their approach to religion, Jesus said they "strain at a gnat, and swallow a camel" (Matt. 23:24). Pharisees, for example, took great pride in being strict observers of the law of Moses. Their strictness took the form of stressing petty rules, finding fault with hundreds of innocent, ordinary human acts, and establishing penalties for breaking these rules. Spiritual pride crowded out religion. Self-righteousness, such as praying to be seen by others, replaced true devotion to God. Jesus denounced the Pharisees in terms harsh for the Son of God, because he knew the danger of their fanaticism (see Matt. 23; Mark 7; and Luke 11:37-54).

Among Latter-day Saints, too, there are religious fanatics who are not content to follow the lead of prophets. We must be wary of those who claim to have special, private revelations to guide others, who advocate causes such as a return to polygamy without Church sanction, and individuals who teach and practice income-tax evasion despite explicit warnings by every prophet in recent times. Fanaticism is dangerous to the peace and welfare of all people. We should be on guard against it, knowing its tendency to enflame and lead to violence.

Even simple principles such as virtue, righteousness, and goodness can be carried to such an extreme that others may no longer be able to identify them as such. People still kill innocent human beings to carry out their supposed religious duty.

The conclusion, therefore, is that religious fanaticism is one of the major obstacles to tolerance. It is dangerous when practiced by individuals and it is even more dangerous when practiced by religious groups.

Abuse of Power

Tolerance requires fair, equal, and humane treatment of people without regard to their position or financial status. Another major obstacle to tolerance is the misuse and abuse of power, prestige, and financial status in achieving privilege or immunity from the consequences of action. When widespread misuse and abuse of power prevail, tolerance shrivels and dies. Too often before extending fair treatment, decision makers ask, whether consciously or unconsciously, "Who is the person involved?" They then make decisions on the basis of the person's position and prestige in society. This results in a double abuse of power-by the one wielding power and by the one influencing the exercise of it unrighteously.

While serving in the United States Army in Korea, I received less favorable treatment on an assignment because I was an enlisted man. In 1956, President Paul Andrus, the mission president from Japan, came to Korea to visit the servicemen scattered in various army units. At the time our Church leader was Willice E. Groves, a full colonel. He made arrangements for transporting President Andrus to the outlying areas to meet the servicemen. The chaplain assigned a driver and a comfortable, enclosed army sedan for the trip.

When President Andrus made his next trip to visit servicemen, Brother Groves was gone. We had no officers in our group. It fell on me as group leader to make the arrangements for the trip to the Twenty-fourth Division.

I went to see the chief of chaplains of the Eighth Army. At first he refused to discuss the request. I was, after all, an enlisted man. But finally he provided an open jeep for the long dusty ride. President Andrus wore a navy blue suit. By the time we arrived at the northern unit, his blue suit was brown and his hair covered with dust. To his credit, President Andrus uttered no complaint. He brushed off the dust from his hair and suit, greeted the waiting soldiers, and preached a powerful and moving sermon.

The purposes of the two trips and even the person making them were the same, but the difference lay in who made the arrangements. Perhaps there were wise army regulations governing such things of which we were unaware, but putting the army connection aside, the simple example serves to illustrate the major obstacle to fairness. Uneven treatment based on rank and position is repeated too often in life without any justification and, therefore, is an abuse of power.

There is a debate in the United States about whether blacks can get the same justice as whites in the court systems of the land. In most courts and before many judges the justice meted out is the same for every man and woman who steps before the court, but that is not universally true. Ideally, the background or race of the person should make no difference. The scriptures teach that God will hold those with greater advantages to stricter standards of behavior in matters of judgment (see D&C 82:3; James 4:17; Luke 12:48).

Fair and tolerant treatment of another person ought to result from the operation of the principle that every man, woman, and child is a part of the family of a loving and concerned Heavenly Father. There should be no checking of bank accounts, yielding to influence from friends in high places, notice taken of high standing in the community, or exercise of political power in deciding whether to treat another decently and fairly. Paul taught this doctrine in the twelfth chapter of 1 Corinthians.

Paul likened the Church to a body-the body of Christ. There were many members, but all of the members of the body of Christ were one and a part of the body. The foot, hand, and head were all important. "If the whole body were an eye, where were the hearing?" Paul asked. "If the whole were hearing, where were the smelling? He concludes, "Nay, much more those members of the body, which seem to be more feeble, are necessary: and those members of the body, which we think to be less honourable, upon these we bestow more abundant honour.Â� God hath tempered the body together, having given more abundant honour to that part which lacked." (See 1 Cor. 12:12-24.)

Unfortunately, many of the governments of the world are still full of corruption and intolerance. They do not administer most of their affairs in the manner that Paul suggested the Church be operated. Rather, money under the table, falsification of papers of citizenship status, intervention of the influential to achieve objectives by favor are all common. Called "the back door" in China, such official intolerance will continue until the Son of God reigns on the earth and there are no laws but his laws that apply. We should continue to press for the exercise of more tolerance by people entrusted with power.

Followers of Christ should respect everyone and use power righteously. Priesthood leaders and women leaders need the spirit, attitude, and practice of tolerance. Those having had opportunities to know and experience the Lord's goodness and to be educated in the ways of the Lord should, of course, be held to a higher standard than that to which we hold others.

Joseph Smith, a regular victim of intolerance, wrote, "We have learned by sad experience that it is the nature and disposition of almost all men, as soon as they get a little authority, as they suppose, they will immediately begin to exercise unrighteous dominion" (D&C 121:39). That spirit of unrighteous dominion on the part of others plagued Joseph down to his last day on earth in Carthage Jail. If anyone had the right to say those things, Joseph did.

Latter-day Saints, generally a tolerant and fair people, have not been totally immune from exercising unrighteous dominion, as Joseph implied in the revelation just quoted. As the Saints founded communities in which they formed the majority of citizens of those communities, they also occasionally abused power and position in the treatment of other men and women. We need to both exercise power in tolerant ways and refrain from being a party to seeking and accepting unjust favored treatment from those in power.

Historically it has been necessary to limit government power because of the tendency to abuse it. For example, the premise of the Bill of Rights, the name often given to the first ten amendments to the UnitedStates Constitution, is that government often abuses power and citizens often abuse each other. Thus, the weaker part of the community, meaning those members who haven't sufficient standing to protect themselves from such abuse of power, need the special provisions lodged in the Constitution to protect them. Some of these protective provisions are freedom of speech, the right to a jury trial in certain cases, freedom from unreasonable search and seizure, and the right to have one's home free from military takeover.

The Constitution is based on the further premise that lodging too much power in one branch or department of government is dangerous because of the tendency to abuse power. The solution was to separate these powers between the branches of government. The underlying theory of the separation of powers is that those clothed with power tend to become intolerant and unfair in their treatment of others.

There is a growing recognition around the world that abuse of power resulting in flagrant trampling on human rights is unacceptable. Disclosure, identification, and appropriate sanctions can bring change.

The struggle against intolerance and abuse of power is conceivably the most important social problem the world faces. The hearts and minds of humans hold the key to change. People who have truly made tolerance a keystone of their beliefs and the doctrine by which they live, multiplied to become an association of millions and billions, are the answer to the perplexing and vexing events the daily news is full of. Such men and women, guided by the doctrines of Christ, will create institutions that are fair, safeguarding the rights of everyone, weak or strong. This is the essence of the gospel of Jesus Christ with its goal of peace on earth and "good will toward men" (see Luke 2:14).

The best way I know to bring about such a state of affairs is through active, strong, diligent, and tolerant efforts to spread those doctrines into the homes and lives of everyone who will truly listen and ascribe to them. Jesus said the kingdom of God is like leaven that quietly spreads through the loaf, working its magic (see Matt. 13:33; Luke 13:20-21).

Attitudes of Superiority of Races, Cultures, and Nationalities

Racial, cultural, and nationalist attitudes have been and continue to be an obstacle to human tolerance. Races, cultures, and nationalities are not themselves the obstacles, but attitudes about them continue to create intolerance.

Many people appreciate the diversity of races, cultures, and nationalities. Different nationalities with their systems of governing people add to the variety and interest in life, as long as they do not degrade and enslave men. I am not advocating a world government or the mixture of all the races to obliterate differences. Racial diversity is generally a natural phenomenon. And there is safety in having power divided between different nations.

On the subject of mixed-race marriages, Church Presidents, when they have spoken on the subject, have counseled that members marry within their own races and cultures.R2 [^2]

In our present ward, several ward members have mixed-race marriages that appear to be successful, with righteous families resulting. Most of us could give testimony to similar observations. We should not condemn those members or feel that they are unequal to those who have married within their race and culture. I bring up the subject to make my position clear at the threshold of discussing one of the serious obstacles to tolerance.

This is a book about the doctrines of Christ, not a book on anthropology. The doctrine is clear. There is no super race or inferior race. Many in history, including Hitler, have espoused the idea of superior races destined to rule people, and some individuals and groups accept that extremist concept today.

These ideas are wrong and offensive to the Lord. President Spencer W. Kimball stated in his direct manner:

We do wish that there would be no racial prejudice among people, but the fact that a person is a member of the Church is no indication that he is going to accept a Christlike view until he becomes Christlike. Often our own people snub and belittle our Mexican members. We do not uphold them in this and would that they would treat them as brothers should be treated.R3 [^3]

President Kimball also said on another occasion: "Now then, who are you and who am I that will distinguish between an Indian child and a white child? Who are we that we are so preferred in the kingdom of heaven? What have we done that we are entitled to so many blessings? What did you individually do that made you superior to your other darker brothers and sisters? Was it something you did?"R4 [^4]

A discussion of this difficult obstacle to tolerance gets near the heart of the problems of intolerance in the world. It is important to get straight that there is neither black nor white, Jew nor Gentile, male nor female in terms of worthiness in the eyes of God (see 2 Ne. 26:33).

Despite these plain doctrinal truths, there are still widely held beliefs and attitudes that certain races, cultures, and nationalities are superior to others. We can enjoy our own race and nationality without buying into the superiority notion. That those attitudes are still so pervasive in the world is a major stumbling block to tolerance.

Some rely on the scriptures to establish the idea that some races are superior and others inferior. We cannot explain the timetables of God as he dispenses the gospel and priesthood keys and ordinances to his daughters and sons. We can only seek understanding from the scriptures through prayerful study. To find answers to questions not directly answered by searching the scriptures, we can petition our Heavenly Father for inspiration. New revelation on general questions, however, will most likely come through the prophet of God to guide His church. Some answers related to the subject at hand came through President Spencer W. Kimball in June of 1978 (see Official Declaration-2 in the Doctrine and Covenants).

A superficial look at our limited record in the New Testament might lead readers to conclude that Jesus discriminated in favor of the Jews and Israel and against the Gentiles. I firmly believe, however, that when the entire record is available to us we shall see that Jesus was totally just, merciful, and fair to all, irrespective of race, culture, or nationality.

Nevertheless, we read that when the woman of Canaan (clearly a Gentile) came to Jesus in behalf of her daughter who was "grievously vexed with a devil," Jesus said: "I am not sent but unto the lost sheep of the house of Israel." The statement seemed hard and uncaring, but rather than take offense the woman pleaded with Jesus, "Lord, help me." His next answer is even more perplexing. "It is not meet to take the children's bread, and to cast it to dogs." Still pressing her point, the woman of Canaan said, "Truth, Lord: yet the dogs eat of the crumbs which fall from their masters' table." Jesus' great and perfect tolerance emerges after this amazing discussion in his next words: "O woman, great is thy faith: be it unto thee even as thou wilt." The record then states, "And her daughter was made whole from that very hour." (Matt. 15:22-28.)

In a seeming about-face, after confining his formal ministry to the Jews and Israel, when Jesus returned to earth after his resurrection he commanded his disciples, "Go ye therefore, and teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost" (Matt. 28:19).

Later, Peter, who must have heard the command to go and teach all nations, required a special revelation to let him know that the gospel was now for the Gentiles as well as for Israel (see Acts 10).

We cannot understand all that is involved in these and other difficult passages of scripture and early Church practices, but it seems clear enough that, for good and just but unexplained reasons, Israel received the priesthood, the keys, and the gospel of Jesus Christ first. Then it went to all other nations; and after that, the people of Israel will have a second chance to receive the gospel.

In the same way, we cannot fully comprehend why all worthy males did not receive the priesthood until 1978, but we trust in the Lord and remember his teaching that we are all alike in his eyes. God has his timetables for when things occur. He is perfectly just, but there are many additional things for us to learn. In the meantime we do not believe that any nation, race, or culture is a lesser breed or inferior in God's eyes. Those who believe or teach such doctrine have no authority from either the Lord or his authorized servants.

It seems clear that active and concerned tolerance, as defined earlier, if fully accepted by nations, communities, and religions, would solve the kinds of problems we still face in the United States and most other nations. Many among the races who live together feel prejudice, intolerance, bitterness, and hopelessness. Governments can do some things to help, and thoughtful public policies can assist; but wide acceptance of the doctrines of Christ and of the attitudes and practices of tolerant people is the ultimate answer.

Now let's turn our attention to attitudes about nationality, patriotism and pride in our own nation. These are natural emotions and can be exciting and thrilling. We feel those emotions during and after each round of the Olympic Games, for example. People may, however, carry these feelings, as with religious zeal, to extremes and become intolerant of other nations, thus opening the door to all the dangers inherent in extreme nationalism suggested by history and current events. We all belong to the family of a wise and loving Heavenly Father. That fact creates ties beyond any we should feel for race, culture, or nationality. I am advocating balance, not obliteration of nations or nationalities. Let us enjoy our uniqueness but keep in mind our greater brotherhood and act accordingly.

This is the Lord's earth. He created it and "saw that it was good" (Genesis l:25). He allowed the conditions we have created to go forward unimpeded by his forceful intervention. His granting of agency to us was itself a great act of confidence in man and is in a class with the Atonement itself as among the greatest acts of tolerance we are aware of. Much of what we have done with the choices granted to us by our agency has been evil and harmful, but much has also been good.

We apparently grow by making moral choices, and we stagnate when choices are made for us. The choices humans have made created, in part, diversity of governments, nationalities, races, and cultures on the earth. True, there are some recorded interventions of God in the process, but mostly we have created conditions that exist here on the earth. The Lord has tolerated these choices for the most part. This suggests that we should also honor them. We have not been appointed the judges of our brothers and sisters. Apparently it is one of the eternal laws of growth governing the universe that we have moral agency and are accountable to God for our choices.

This third obstacle to tolerance, then, consists of attitudes we hold about race, culture, and nationality. Tensions created by these attitudes of superiority and inferiority continue to erupt in intolerant ways in our human relationships. Answers to the tensions caused by attitudes toward race, culture, and nationalities lie not in social engineering by those who set themselves up in power to force their ideas on us, but in spreading tolerance among all people and having a greater degree of acceptance and practice of it.

Evil and Corrupt Men

The fourth obstacle to tolerance was difficult to identify and label, but had to be here. There are men and women who have become, for whatever reason, so evil and corrupt that showing them kindness and tolerance is both futile and meaningless. Acts of tolerance are a sign of weakness to such people. They will use those acts to their advantage and never respond in kind. Such evil men and women are dangerous, and we are well advised to avoid dealing with them.

Fortunately, we seldom find people like that. Humans do evil things, but most are not beyond the point that some factor of rational and righteous thinking can touch them.

The scriptures establish the same point made here. The reality of evil is a major theme in all of the scriptures. The book of Revelation in the Bible states that "there was war in heaven: Michael and his angels fought against the dragon; and the dragon fought and his angels, and prevailed not; neither was their place found any more in heaven. And the great dragon was cast out, that old serpent, called the Devil, and Satan, which deceiveth the whole world: he was cast out into the earth, and his angels were cast out with him." (Rev. 12:7-9.)

Jesus and his Apostles fought with these evil angels who had been cast down to earth. They cast evil spirits from the bodies of human beings they had possessed. The influence of the devil is felt in the earth and may explain the existence of people who have become evil through and through.

In the 1930s, British prime minister Neville Chamberlain, a pacifist by temperament, could never give up the idea that if he was fair and tolerant to Adolph Hitler and the Nazi government in Germany, he could win peace for his nation and for the world. The idea was idealistic and good, and ordinarily the logic behind it would appeal to and convince many. A majority of his countrymen agreed with him for a number of years. He was, however, dealing with evil men with whom rational negotiation was impossible. Many ignored and scoffed at Winston Churchill's warnings about Hilter's intentions, but eventually Chamberlain became the victim of his failure to come to grips with the nature of his treaty partner. Hitler looked at Chamberlain's efforts as weaknesses to be exploited. Hitler had no honorable intentions in his negotiations. He was evil, devious, and dishonorable.

This example serves to illustrate the point that there are people from whom we must withhold the hand of fellowship and tolerance. Only strength and vigilance count in dealing with such people. Hitler, Goebbels, and Goering laughed and jeered at every act of tolerance. Expecting truly evil people to respond to principles of tolerance is dangerous and naive.

It is sad to think that humans can descend to that level and buy into a totally evil and depraved philosophy, but such is sometimes the case. Author and psychiatrist M. Scott Peck, who wrote the best-seller The Road Less Traveled, also wrote a book called People of the LieR5 [^5]

The compiler of the Book of Mormon, General Mormon, a charitable and tolerant man, reached the point that he could serve no longer as a leader of his own people because of their depravity. Mormon recorded that even when the Nephites won battles they did "boast in their own strength, and began to swear before the heavens that they would avenge themselves of the blood of their brethren who had been slain by their enemies.Â� And it came to pass that I, Mormon, did utterly refuse from this time forth to be a commander and a leader of this people, because of their wickedness and abomination." (Morm. 3:9, 11.) For a time, Mormon became a witness to their destruction from the sidelines. His refusal to lead them was the direct result of their wickedness and abominations.

Another way of describing this obstacle to tolerance is to say that intolerance has become so completely ingrained in the character of some humans that it is no longer possible to work with them on a rational basis. From such people we must retreat and leave them in the hands of God, who will judge them with the principles of justice and mercy.

Perhaps this is what Paul meant when he told Timothy that "evil men and seducers shall wax worse and worse, deceiving, and being deceived" (2 Tim. 3:13). These words state a prophecy about the existence of evil humans and their fate. They lose the ability to deal with others in a truthful, straightforward way, but in addition to using deception, they themselves are deceived. We cannot afford to be involved with such people.

We can discern that we are dealing with totally evil and corrupt people when the truth is so completely corrupted that we must discount everything they say. When we discover through experience that someone will twist and use every word we say against us, it is further evidence we are dealing with such persons. They may masquerade as good Church members or as angels of light. We should be wary of Church members who use their membership as the reason why we should do business with them or invest in the venture they are promoting. Shakespeare's Hamlet observed that "one may smile, and smile, and be a villain."R6 [^6]
