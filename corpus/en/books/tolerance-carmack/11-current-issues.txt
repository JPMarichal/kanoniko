John K. Carmack, Tolerance: Principles, Practices, Obstacles, Limits ( 1993)

Chapter Ten

Current Issues

And a man's foes shall be they of his own household.

-Matthew 10:36

This chapter discusses some questions about Church policies and procedures that some people have questioned in regard to tolerance. The discussion is limited to two issues. There are many more, of course, but these two are important current issues.

First of all, with the steady growth of our missionary force, some question whether the missionary program goes beyond the bounds of tolerance to infringe on the rights of individuals, other churches, and communities.

Second, are Church practices dealing with Church critics tolerant? This issue concerns both those critics who are members of the Church and those who are not.

Let's discuss the missionary issue first.

Is the Church Missionary Program Tolerant?

The Issue Stated

Others know and mostly admire Latter-day Saints for their missionary zeal. At any given time the Church has forty thousand or more missionaries serving throughout the world, wherever local laws and policies allow activities of the kind missionaries engage in.

The issue is whether proselytizing by one Christian denomination among members of other Christian denominations and non-Christian religions is tolerant.

Some countries consider proselytizing undesirable enough to be against public policy. These countries often have laws against doing the kind of missionary work Latter-day Saint missionaries traditionally do-open contacting, teaching, and converting people to their faith. Some countries even have laws, directed at their own citizens, that make it a criminal offense to convert from the state-sanctioned or traditional religion predominant in that country to any other religion.

Latter-day Saint Beliefs

Latter-day Saints have proselytized regularly since before the Church was organized in 1830. This missionary service takes many forms, such as handing out tracts, going door-to-door, holding street preaching meetings, teaching friends in members' homes, and holding public meetings in churches or rented rooms.

The missionaries' message is that the gospel of Jesus Christ with all of the keys and powers of the priesthood has been restored to the earth through Joseph Smith the Prophet, and that the Book of Mormon is an ancient scripture, a second witness that Jesus Christ lived and broke the bands of death.

The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints is not the only religion with a strong belief in its divine mission and duty to share it. Other churches hold similar beliefs and share them through proselytizing. The United States Constitution protects what one believes about God and religion. Many similar provisions in other countries exist. As previously discussed in this book, it is in poor taste to ridicule what another person sincerely believes. What a church does in the name of religion that violates decency and public policy is and should be subject to criticism, but not what a church believes.

The assumption of Church leaders from the beginning has been that proselytizing was perfectly legal, was guaranteed in the United States by the Bill of Rights, and was a duty and service owed to mankind. Church members doing missionary work often received persecution, but few called their work illegal. In Europe and other areas where the traditions of freedom had not become specific, there were many early contests of the right to proselytize. But freedom of religion has generally carried the day.

When proselytizing in a country that has no law as specific as the Bill of Rights of the United States Constitution guaranteeing freedom of religion, Latter-day Saints still feel that something akin to natural law or the law of God grants them and others the right and duty to share their religion. The Church respects local laws but believes prohibitions against missionary work are an infringement of human rights. Most thoughtful observers agree.

D&C 134 of the Doctrine and Covenants contains a statement of beliefs about the right to do missionary work. The Saints adopted this section by unanimous vote as a declaration of belief regarding governments and laws in general at Kirtland, Ohio, 17 August 1835. One partial verse reads, "We believe it just to preach the gospel to the nations of the earth, and warn the righteous to save themselves from the corruption of the world" (D&C 134:12). That succinct statement summarizes what Latter-day Saints believe about their God-given right and duty to proselytize.

Some Christian sects believe that for one Christian church to share the gospel with Christians who already belong to a church is somehow morally wrong and an act of intolerance. Their reasoning seems to be that such activity interferes with the freedom of choice of a Christian because she or he has already made a Christian choice. These sects seem to believe proselytizing is akin to sheep stealing.

Such reasoning is self-serving and designed to guard against membership changes. Guaranteed freedoms hammered out in early United States history and in the constitutions of many countries are against this kind of reasoning. There has always been tension between those whose activities upset the status quo of people's religious affiliation and those who desire to maintain the status quo.

What Was Jesus' View?

Jesus, of course, was a teacher, a missionary, and a proselytizer. He went about preaching and healing, making converts who signified their acceptance of his teachings by baptism, and he also organized a church with twelve Apostles whom he sent forth to teach and baptize. As the Lord's work grew, he called seventy more missionary leaders and sent them to villages and cities two by two to teach, baptize, convert, and heal. (See Luke 6:12-16; 9:1; 10:1, 17.) Jesus was constantly in trouble with the established religion and civil authorities because of his activities. He eventually gave up his life but never stopped his proselytizing activities.

When the Savior was resurrected, he commanded his disciples to go into all the world and continue their missionary work, baptizing and converting. Those instructions to his disciples were universal and contained no cautions against "sheep stealing." (See Matt. 28:19-20; Mark 16:15-20.) The rest of the New Testament is largely a record of the missionary work of the Christians, including letters written by one of the most zealous of the missionaries-Paul. Authorities sometimes tried to stop their work, arresting Christian missionaries for proselytizing activities. But they would not stop until they were jailed and martyred, as many of them were.

The foregoing is what is well known about the actions and commandments of Jesus and his disciples. They established a missionary organization with a message for people of all religions. As Paul said in his letter to the Roman members, "How then shall they call on him in whom they have not believed? and how shall they believe in him of whom they have not heard? and how shall they hear without a preacher? and how shall they preach, except they be sent?" (Rom. 10:14-15.)

Paul's own intense missionary spirit and commitment shines through in all of his writings. He said, "I have nothing to glory of: for necessity is laid upon me; yea, woe is unto me, if I preach not the gospel!" (1 Cor. 9:16.)

To be true to the charge of having a dispensation of the gospel in their hands, Latter-day Saints also have little spiritual choice but to be missionaries, and they are. Like Paul, woe to them if they don't preach the gospel!

That the missionaries today are so numerous and diligent in their missionary efforts makes some church leaders and public officials nervous. The belief of Latter-day Saints, however, is that their Lord showed them the way to proselytize by example and commanded them to do it, subject to some limitations based on tolerance and respect discussed next.

Church Critics and Tolerance

Criticism against The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints seems to be constant. A leading sports magazine claimed that the football team of Brigham Young University was the most-hated football team in America. This article would be too trivial to discuss except for its underlying basis. What concerned those interested in fairness and tolerance was the author's statement of reasons for his bizarre conclusion. He identified Brigham Young University's affiliation with The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints as the principal reason for the hatred. He then discussed the high standards of behavior espoused by the players; the teachings of the Church about its divine origin and mission; allegations of historical intolerance by the Church; a sweeping, generalized allegation of self-righteousness on the part of the players; and, perhaps more to the point, Brigham Young University's tendency to have a winning football program.

How strange to find a football program judged by the doctrine of the church that operates the university! Who is being intolerant?

A more serious recent wave of criticism breaking over the Church was a flurry of news articles that featured interviews with writers and scholars-many of whom claimed to be believing Latter-day Saints in good standing-in which symposium speeches were discussed that criticized Church procedures in handling dissent.

Are there any principles to guide Church leaders in dealing with critics outside the Church who charge the Church with intolerance? How should the Church handle such criticism? What has our experience taught us during the long history of enduring criticism in books and articles? Should methods of handling criticism from members of the Church differ from methods of dealing with criticism from nonmembers?

These are thorny questions. The nature and style of criticism varies greatly from case to case and from situation to situation, making general principles difficult to identify. Church leaders must use the principles and practices of tolerant people together with wisdom and inspiration. The discussion breaks naturally into two obvious categories-dealing with critics from outside the Church, and dealing with those from inside the Church.

Outside Critics

The most common method used in handling outside critics is to simply leave them alone. Something has motivated them to publish their criticisms, ranging from trying to be helpful to trying to harm the Church. Leaving them alone, while not ignoring what they say, is the principle that we should most often invoke.

The tendency to publicly criticize the Church is not new. As an early example, E. D. Howe published the anti-Mormon book Mormonism Unvailed in 1834 in Ohio. Such efforts continue. By quietly ignoring criticism, the Church often saves itself from second and third waves of criticism that usually carry the additional assertion, "You are too sensitive and paranoid."

Tolerant people exercise patience and usually ignore even unfairness and hate as Jesus did when he was brought before King Herod. In most instances the effect of the criticism fades away and few remember the criticism. Time and levelheadedness are curative tools.

Most if not all of us have experienced unfair criticism sometime during our lives. There may be a short-term fallout from it, but in time, truth and patience win the day with people of goodwill.

Jesus advised his leaders to turn their cheeks and carry on. In his Sermon on the Mount, anticipating that there would arise women and men of ill will, he said that the meek, merciful, and peacemakers would be blessed. Then, directly on point, he said, "Blessed are they which are persecuted for righteousness' sake: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven." Continuing, he added, "Blessed are ye, when men shall revile you, and persecute you, and shall say all manner of evil against you falsely, for my sake. Rejoice, and be exceeding glad: for great is your reward in heaven: for so persecuted they the prophets which were before you." (Matt. 5:10-12.) Jesus also advised, "Agree with thine adversary quickly, whiles thou art in the way with him" (Matt. 5:25).

Getting more specific, and perhaps preparing his disciples for the terrible trials that he knew they would face, the Savior said, "If the world hate you, ye know that it hated me before it hated you. If ye were of the world, the world would love his own: but because ye are not of the world, but I have chosen you out of the world, therefore the world hateth you." (John 15:18-19.)

A true disciple of Christ is in for misunderstanding, hate, and criticism. Christ received it and his critics finally crucified him. Critics pummeled Joseph Smith with criticism and finally murdered him.

But is remaining silent in face of criticism the only course we should follow? Christ spoke out against the Pharisees, scribes, and hypocrites, and once even called Herod "that fox" (see Luke 13:32). He also answered some of the hate-filled charges and criticisms leveled against him (see Luke 20:1-8).

Sometimes Church leaders are unable to answer charges and criticisms because by answering they would reveal confidential communications.

To summarize this suggestion, following Jesus' lead, the best answer to most criticism from outside sources is to leave it alone, not dignifying it with an answer. The Lord said, "Be still and know that I am God" (D&C 101:16).

The danger with more venal criticism is that it can lead to serious harm, even genocide, justified by lies and accusations. One of the questions many Jews asked after the Holocaust was what if the Jews and all other right-thinking people of influence had immediately and firmly countered and challenged the Nazi program of anti-Semitism? Perhaps it would not have gotten out of hand. Unfortunately, the world's moral commitment to tolerance was insufficient, and in fact, many besides the Nazis were guilty of their own intolerance of the Jews.

The question of when to take a stand is a judgment call each individual must make. Silence under circumstances calling for an answer or denial can sometimes be tantamount to an admission that the criticism is true. Whether and when the Church or other institutions should take a public stand is also a judgment call, lodged with those assigned and authorized to make the judgment.

For example, books and articles written about the forged Church historical documents and the associated murders in the 1980s in Utah contain many unfounded, unverified, and malicious assertions. The truth about what happened in that whole episode is in danger of permanent distortion. The reputations of innocent people were injured. In such a matter it is important to dig out the facts and make them available to avoid permanent distortion of the events and to straighten out the record for the sake of the reputations of people injured by charges and scuttlebutt.

Active countermeasures to present the truth, rather than silence and acquiescence, are sometimes necessary to counter criticism that may distort the truth in permanently harmful ways.

All of the approaches described above qualify as tolerant responses to criticism from sources emanating from outside the Church.

Now let's turn our attention to criticism from within the ranks of the Church, most often found in scholarly journals, magazines, books, and public symposia.

Inside Critics

It has been more than a decade since the emotionally charged fight for a United States Constitutional amendment called the Equal Rights Amendment caught The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints in the eye of a media hurricane. One Church member, favorable to the amendment, chose to go public with her anger and displeasure when she found herself the subject of a disciplinary proceeding held by the stake presidency and high council of her stake. Charges of unfairness, bias, and intolerance reverberated across the United States. Some thought they heard echoes of the Inquisition.

It is not my purpose to examine that old issue, but to discuss the issue of tolerance and fairness when responding to critics of the Church and its leaders from inside the Church.

For a number of years, for example, symposia have been held featuring a variety of themes related to the Church, some of which have taken the form of strong criticism of the Church and its leaders. What are the principles involved in examining and assessing the actions of members involved in public criticism of their own church and its prophets? Should the Church make distinctions in dealing with members as compared with nonmembers who level strong criticism against the Church, and if so, what is the basis for those distinctions?

During the Equal Rights Amendment campaign in southern California, I sometimes answered questions posed by the media in my calling as public communications chairman. I found that a number of misunderstandings existed about what was happening within the Church and how the Church operated. When we discussed matters away from the emotion and glare of instant media, even if the media did not agree with the stand the Church took, they understood that the Church was not involved in an intolerant inquisition. This section will share some of the reasons why the Church handles things the way it does.

Public criticisms by members, sometimes harsh and blunt, have travelled the airwaves and appeared in writing regularly for many years. To my knowledge, few of the participants have been called to answer before formal Church disciplinary councils. According to recent reports by some critics, priesthood leaders have called them in for counsel when leaders felt their actions and rhetoric had been disloyal to the Church and its leaders. Such interviews have been confidential, but some of those involved have chosen to go public, either directly or by sharing it with another who has gone public, stating to the media the substance of the interviews.

This places a Church leader-whose motives have been characterized and whose words have been quoted publicly-in an awkward position. He may have been misquoted or misunderstood, but the duty of confidentiality imposed by the priest-penitent privilege requires that he remain silent and leave the matter as his critic characterized it.

Let's consider some of the principles involved.

A Church disciplinary council is not a civil or criminal court. It administers the internal principles, doctrines, and procedures of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. The council does not charge individuals with criminal conduct or civil wrongs. The decisions of such a council only deal with Church standing.

The primary purpose of the council is to assist a Church member to repent and determine the member's standing before the Church and before the Lord. Other purposes include protecting innocent victims and safeguarding the integrity and good name of the Church. In the Doctrine and Covenants we read a succinct explanation of this function:

We believe that all religious societies have a right to deal with their members for disorderly conduct, according to the rules and regulations of such societies; provided that such dealings be for fellowship and good standing; but we do not believe that any religious society has authority to try men on the right of property or life, to take from them this world's goods, or to put them in jeopardy of either life or limb, or to inflict any physical punishment upon them. They can only excommunicate them from their society, and withdraw from them their fellowship. (D&C 134:10.)

When the general public reads or hears that a member has grievances against Church procedures, the general reader or listener can easily become confused about what is going on and misunderstand the role of the Church. The public may think the Church is conducting a trial with legal penalties as the potential end result. A disciplinary council, however, is a private affair dealing with morality, sin, private apostasy, disloyalty, and conduct that is inconsistent with membership in the Church. Most churches have similar procedures.

The Church has taken great care to ensure fairness in its council proceedings, such as appointing members of the council to prevent insult or injustice, providing opportunities for the individual to speak, and similar fair processes. The member can also appeal the decision reached by the council in case he or she feels wronged for any reason. These safeguards are not the same as found in a court of law, because Church councils are not courts of law; but the procedures protect members from injustice and unfairness.

Moreover, the best protection is the quality of those sitting as members of the councils. Almost without exception they are prayerful, humble men serving without pay who accept their role as a call from the Lord. Mistakes can be and are made, but the system allows for reviews and appeals, and takes them seriously.

These councils deal with a variety of charges, including apostasy and failure to obey important commandments. For example, it may not be illegal under the laws of a country to commit fornication and adultery, but it is a serious sin in the eyes of the Lord and the Church and could subject a member to discipline, the purpose being to assist the individual to repent and return to practices compatible with membership in the Church. The charge of engaging in conduct unbecoming a member of the Church covers a variety of offenses against the Church, including speaking against the Lord's anointed prophets and other Church leaders, leading members to reject gospel principles, and teaching doctrine other than that accepted by the Church.

The laws, doctrines, and procedures of the Church are applicable to all members, but every member is not held equally responsible when transgressions occur.

A well-known principle establishes the doctrine that one who enjoys many privileges and possesses special knowledge has correlative responsibilities. "For of him unto whom much is given much is required; and he who sins against the greater light shall receive the greater condemnation" (D&C 82:3). James said it this way: "Therefore to him that knoweth to do good, and doeth it not, to him it is sin" (James 4:17). Jesus taught: "But he that knew not, and did commit things worthy of stripes, shall be beaten with few stripes. For unto whomsoever much is given, of him shall be much required: and to whom men have committed much, of him they will ask the more." (Luke 12:48.)

When deciding whether one needs to be contacted, interviewed, or brought before a formal disciplinary council to answer for a transgression, the Church officer will consider not only whether an offense has been committed against the commandments of the Lord and the law of the Church but also what the background is of the person involved. Is the person a new member without many opportunities to know and be grounded in doctrine and procedure? Has the person been ordained to the higher priesthood and made special commitments in connection with that ordination? Has she or he been to the temple and made special covenants? Another relevant question is whether the person is a trusted faculty member of a Church school. A special commitment to the Church and its schools is a condition of employment for faculty members. All of these questions are relevant and pertinent to any inquiry into the propriety of words and actions called into question.

We believe "that every idle word that men shall speak, they shall give account thereof in the day of judgment" (Matt. 12:36). In preparing men for that day, the disciplinary councils are to guide offending members into the paths of repentance. Alma taught a similar doctrine in the Book of Mormon: "For our words will condemn us, yea, all our works will condemn us; we shall not be found spotless; and our thoughts will also condemn us; and in this awful state we shall not dare to look up to our God; and we would fain be glad if we could command the rocks and the mountains to fall upon us to hide us from his presence" (Alma 12:14).

Disciplinary councils look most of all for sincerity, humility, and meekness in members appearing before them. These are the sacrifices required of disciples of Christ since his crucifixion and resurrection that ended the sacrifice of shedding the blood of animals and burnt offerings. "Ye shall offer for a sacrifice unto me a broken heart and a contrite spirit." (See 3 Ne. 9:19-20.) "All among them who know their hearts are honest, and are broken, and their spirits contrite, and are willing to observe their covenants by sacrifice-yea, every sacrifice which I, the Lord, shall command-they are accepted of me" (D&C 97:8).

It is hard to assist someone who exhibits pride, haughtiness, and disrespect, and who publicly complains of actions taken by prophets of God. The Church can and does extend patience and long-suffering, but when dissension and schisms arise because of the words, criticisms, and activities of members, the Church will likely call into question their standing within the ecclesiastical organization.

The scriptures warn against disloyalty to the Lord's anointed. "And if any man lift his voice against you [Joseph Smith the Prophet] he shall be confounded in mine own due time" (D&C 71:10). In D&C 121 we read, "Cursed are all those that shall lift up the heel against mine anointed, saith the Lord, and cry they have sinned when they have not sinned before me, saith the Lord, but have done that which was meet in mine eyes, and which I commanded them.Â� Wo unto them; because they have offended my little ones they shall be severed from the ordinances of mine house." (D&C 121:16, 19.)

The Lord urges his disciples to be peacemakers, meek, lowly, and humble. He loves all of his children, but eventually every knee must humbly bow and confess in humility before him. His anointed, in the eyes of the Church, is the prophet of God, but the term includes others whom God has called to minister as judges holding keys of responsibility for the welfare of the people. To speak against the Lord's anointed, particularly in public, is an act of pride and apostasy.

Some may justify their actions by stating their humility and sincerity while appearing to others to speak in rebellion. In the Church they are not their own judges. If called in question they will be able to state their feelings and bring others to assist in the process, but those called of God to judge must prayerfully and spiritually make those judgment calls.

In conclusion, we recall that Alma was troubled when unbelievers deceived and led astray some members of the Church in his day. The king assigned Alma the responsibility of dealing with it. Alma poured out his whole soul to the Lord for guidance. The voice of the Lord came to him and both comforted and instructed him. (See Mosiah 26:1-22.) This was the instruction:

Therefore I say unto you, Go; and whosoever transgresseth against me, him shall ye judge according to the sins which he has committed; and if he confess his sins before thee and me, and repenteth in the sincerity of his heart, him shall ye forgive, and I will forgive him also.Â�

Now I say unto you, Go; and whosoever will not repent of his sins the same shall not be numbered among my people; and this shall be observed from this time forward. (Mosiah 26:29, 32.)

Alma went forward and regulated the affairs of the Church by handling the critics and dissenters in the way the Lord instructed. "And whosoever repented of their sins and did confess them, them he did number among the people of the church; and those that would not confess their sins and repent of their iniquity, the same were not numbered among the people of the church, and their names were blotted out" (Mosiah 26:35-36).

Peace and unity returned to the Church with the application of those regulations the Lord taught to Alma. These principles apply to handling Church critics today who operate in pride and rebellion, and who lift up their voices against the Lord's anointed.

Usually in cases of apostasy, disciplinary actions result for apostasy only after long and patient attempts to work with those involved and after Church leaders give notice of the offenses they perceive have been committed.

The key to good standing in the Church is repentance, sincerity, confession, and humbly doing all that is required to become unified with the Church.

After examining and understanding all of these principles, it is clear that private churches have the right to worship God according to the dictates of their own conscience (see A of F 1:11) and establish their own principles of Church government; when care is taken to safeguard the rights of members to be heard, including the right to appeal unjust decisions, then we are in a better position to know whether the Church is acting in a tolerant and responsible way in a given case.

This is a difficult subject, but one the Church cannot ignore. We have already seen that the Missouri era required that the Church institute and perfect firm and fair procedures to meet the internal dissensions faced then, and the Church must have the same firmness and fairness today to carry out its purposes.
