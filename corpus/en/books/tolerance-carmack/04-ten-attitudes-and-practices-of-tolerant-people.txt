John K. Carmack, Tolerance: Principles, Practices, Obstacles, Limits ( 1993)

Chapter Three

Ten Attitudes and Practices of Tolerant People

He had been called "fifty percent American and one hundred percent British."

-William Manchester, about Winston Churchill

Most of us would like to incorporate in our lives the attitudes and practices of tolerant people, for they are people of breadth and skill who seem to move easily, naturally, and effectively among people of every nationality, race, and religion. They are almost universally respected.

Are tolerant people just born that way? Is it something learned in their unique homes from the example of excellent and tolerant parents? Does tolerance come as a gift earned and learned in premortal life? We do not know all the answers to these questions, although it seems reasonable that at least a partially positive answer could be given to all of them.

We can increase tolerance. Identifying the attitudes and practices of tolerant people is a good starting point.

This is a practical chapter that can serve as a guide to increase tolerance. We will discover that none of these attitudes and practices are particularly difficult to acquire and incorporate into our own set of beliefs and practices. We simply need to identify them and gain some mastery of them in our lives. The mastery we need is of ourselves-our hearts and our minds- combined with a determination to become more tolerant. This requires, as is true with any act of repentance, a desire to change.

Recognition of intolerance in our character and habits precedes the desire to change; but without the desire to change and replace narrowness and intolerance, we are condemned to repeat negative cycles in our relationships with others. Intolerance becomes an ingrained character trait that can be replaced with the broader and spiritually pleasing trait of tolerance. If we justify the status quo by saying, "That is the way I am; take me or leave me," we are unlikely to change and grow.

Following are ten attitudes and practices observed in tolerant people.

Tolerant people are not "me" oriented but look outwardly to serve the needs of others. Their versatility helps them be more mature and effective in working and living with people. Their versatility comes from experience, rooted in a sincere understanding of who they are and the value of other people.

Versatile people are not, however, chameleons who, in order to please other people as they move from one location or situation to another, change their color to become something other than who they are. Rather, they seek to retain their uniqueness as a person of a particular race, religion, nationality, culture, or community. They replace attitudes and practices only when convinced they are improving themselves as a part of the growth process.

People can increase their versatility. Although increased versatility often comes naturally with experience and maturity, most of us can benefit by study and practice.

Mission presidents and their wives constantly observe the progression of their missionaries from young men and women who think mostly of themselves and their own needs to powerful servants of the Lord who enjoy the gift and ability to adapt instantly to the needs of those with whom they interact. Outstanding and experienced missionaries are versatile. Paul understood this, sharing his manner of doing missionary work with the Corinthian Saints in the following words:

For though I be free from all men, yet have I made myself servant unto all, that I might gain the more.

And unto the Jews I became as a Jew, that I might gain the Jews; to them that are under the law, as under the law, that I might gain them that are under the law;

To them that are without law, as without law, (being not without law to God, but under the law to Christ,) that I might gain them that are without law.

To the weak became I as weak, that I might gain the weak: I am made all things to all men, that I might by all means save some. (1 Cor. 9:19-22.)

Paul's points are clear and sharp, giving brilliant insight into the versatility he developed.

First, Paul declared that he was a free man-a unique individual who as one of God's children was different from every other person. He valued himself and did not seek to become an imitation or copy.

Second, however, he adapted himself to every circumstance that he found himself in:

Â When working with the Jews, he adapted himself to that outstanding nation of people with its unique practices, customs, and characteristics.

Â When teaching those Jews who practiced the law of Moses, he worked with them in sympathy and understanding, knowing how they felt about the importance of the law.

Â In his work with the Gentiles he sought to become as one who was not encumbered with the law of Moses that had been fulfilled and supplanted by the gospel of Christ. He adopted a posture that did not threaten them or put them down because they did not have the law.

Â To the weak, he became as if he were weak, showing empathy and love to meet them on their level.

Finally, he explained that this versatility had allowed him to become all things to all people, that is, to adapt himself to every situation and meet the needs of all people. He concluded by explaining that he did all of this for the sake of the gospel and for the purpose of bringing Christ and salvation to people.

Paul did not change who he was and what he believed, but held fast to his firm testimony of Christ. By this time in his ministry he had progressed from being a highly intolerant Pharisee to being an effective and versatile missionary for the Church of Jesus Christ. The gospel had added tolerance to his character.

These verses provide an excellent scriptural example of what versatility means and how Paul applied it in his ministry. He stood before great magistrates, preached to all of the known nations of the earth, and became a versatile missionary.

If narrow, intolerant Saul of Tarsus could grow into versatile Paul, there is hope for the rest of us. We can learn versatility and tolerance.

In essence, versatility is the ability to forget ourselves and our needs and to adjust our approach to meet the needs of others. That skill is related closely to patience, kindness, and love, and leads to a discussion of the second characteristic of tolerant people.

President Ezra Taft Benson taught that "patience is another form of self-control. It is the ability to postpone gratification and to bridle one's passions. In his relationships with loved ones, a patient man does not engage in impetuous behavior that he will later regret. Patience is composure under stress. A patient man is understanding of others' faults."R1 [^1]

People are often impatient with one another. They push and shove, fight to be first in line, make ugly and obscene gestures when another has done something they perceive as stupid or improper, and yell at each other. It is commonplace in movies and television shows for characters depicted on the screen to shout and scream at each other. Perhaps this is having an effect on the behavior of people in real life. Moviemakers justify including these noisy scenes by claiming that they give vent to pain or honest expression. In real-life situations, however, the yelling and screaming often harm the victims in ways difficult to repair.

A Latter-day Saint sister in Salt Lake City was conversing with a social services worker she greatly admired. The sister mentioned Joseph Smith and the Book of Mormon in the course of the conversation. The social worker replied that the Book of Mormon was trash and Joseph Smith a horse thief. Ordinarily, the sister confessed, she would have exploded and stamped out of the room. But this time she calmly stated her belief that the Book of Mormon was true and Joseph Smith a prophet. The social services worker promptly apologized for her intemperate remarks. Patience is a powerful tool. Charity, closely related to patience, "suffereth long, and is kind" (1 Cor. 13:4).

Patience seems rarer and rarer. Many people now consider patience to be weak and without real virtue. Experience suggests, however, that it takes strength to be patient when provoked. Part of the blame for the devaluing of patience lies with the example of athletes, many of whom are revered and idolized as heroes. Some prominent athletes and even their coaches and managers vent their anger at will when a call goes against them. This behavior can influence us in our treatment of others when we become displeased with a judgment call in life.

Consider the effect of such behavior by travelers in foreign countries at ticket counters or on buses, trains, and airplanes. Impatience is especially harmful in relationships between husbands and wives, and between parents and children. "Can't you do anything right?" "There you go again!" "Why can't you be like your sister?" There must be correction and discipline in families, but impatience, failure to listen and consider, intolerance with things we don't like, yelling, shouting, and withering criticism hurt and destroy love.

We are all tender and vulnerable to harm in the winds of harshness, anger, and raised voices. Patience, lowered voices, and taking time to hear, understand, and communicate are desperately needed attitudes and practices. We should apply correction tenderly, patiently, and quietly at a time when the recipient is ready to receive it and when we have our composure.

Some letters should be set aside to be reread the next morning. We need to learn to count to ten or otherwise avoid immediate reaction to disappointment. It helps often if a person will "bite his tongue." As the scripture says, "A soft answer turneth away wrath" (Prov. 15:1). Patience is an ingrained character trait that tolerant people exhibit.

In doing these things, tolerant people carefully guard their commitments to enjoy quality time with families and friends but do not create an exclusive club that shuts other people out.

A few years ago the Los Angeles Times printed an article by its religion reporter about the Latter-day Saint people. While the tenor of the article was overall a positive one, it contained the opinions of some who said that our people were often found to be narrow, exclusive in their relationships, and strict. Whether it was justified or not, that was the impression we had given some people who had observed our life-style.

Peter described Church members in his day as being "a peculiar people" (1 Pet. 2:9), meaning that they were set apart for the special purposes of the Lord and purchased by his blood. Indeed, in the positive sense of that word, Latter-day Saints should be a "peculiar" people. Perhaps our habits of abstaining from alcohol, coffee, tea, and tobacco make it harder to expand our horizons and mingle comfortably with others who do not follow such a strict regimen. Some carry heavy responsibility in Church assignments that reduces their time to expand activities and broaden their lives. We use these and other reasons as excuses for not getting involved in good causes that open our lives to those around us.

Often it is the busiest family that seems to stretch and expand to include others about them. For example, a California stake president was invited to serve as president of his large county dental association. He was already serving on the California State Ethics Committee and had other civic responsibilities in addition to his duties as stake president. He wanted my opinion about whether he should accept the assignment. His term on the ethics committee was soon ending. He had two excellent counselors serving him in the stake presidency. I asked him if he felt he was in a position to make a contribution of importance in the position offered him. His answer was affirmative.

I then shared my own philosophy about responding to these opportunities: always find ways to be involved in bettering your profession, community, state, nation, and world; serve only for the right reason-to give unselfish service and not for personal gain or reward; only accept what you can reasonably make time to do well; and whatever you accept, do the job in a timely, efficient, and excellent manner. If you will spend a few minutes each day in thinking, planning, writing a letter, or making a call, you will probably perform the service better than someone with a great amount of time.

Floyd Packard of Carlsbad, California, shared with me some advice that President Ezra Taft Benson gave him when President Benson called him to serve as stake president. The advice was that about half of his service hours should be spent in bettering the community, outside of strictly Church pursuits.

A few years ago President Gordon B. Hinckley invited me as an Area Presidency member to assist him in setting apart several new mission presidents. One had served a mission in Nauvoo, Illinois, and the other had been the chief executive officer of one of Utah's major public utilities. President Hinckley discussed their roles in detail before setting them apart. I was amazed at his grasp of community affairs in Salt Lake City and Utah, but I was equally impressed with his intimate knowledge of Nauvoo. President Hinckley's breadth of knowledge and tolerance has grown by enlarging the circle of his influence and interests.

Tolerant people find time to get out of their narrow niche in life and serve in broader ways, making many friends and acquaintances along the way. There is time if we make time to do these things, yet we can also maintain close family ties and serve the Church in callings extended to us. When the basics of our lives are in place, we have a wonderful launching pad to use for broader service.

As a young man, one of our close friends worked in the mission office of the Norway Oslo Mission. It was his assignment to drive Elder Spencer W. Kimball of the Council of the Twelve Apostles throughout Norway as he toured the mission. Elder Kimball was vigorous and industrious, using every hour and seizing opportunities to train and teach. This left him little time, it seemed, to learn about Norway-how her people lived and what kinds of buildings and cities they had built. My friend discovered, to his surprise and amazement, that Elder Kimball was finding time to discover and see Norway. After the work was over, late at night or early in the morning, Elder Kimball learned about Norway and her people by walking the streets and seeing the people and cities.

In this dispensation the Lord admonished us to "teach one another the doctrine of the kingdom." The list of subjects includes theory, principle, doctrine, the law of the gospel, and things of the kingdom of God, but also includes "things which have been, things which are, things which must shortly come to pass; things which are at home, things which are abroad; the wars and the perplexities of the nations, and the judgments which are on the land; and a knowledge also of countries and of kingdoms." By learning these things, the Lord explained, the Saints will be prepared in their callings and missions. (D&C 88:77-80.)

Following that counsel would add to our breadth of knowledge and reduce narrowness and satisfaction with the status quo. It is an exciting way to live. No experience is irrelevant or useless. Constant growth is the result.

On one occasion I was sitting in my first Church meeting on the island of Sri Lanka in the capital city of Colombo. The branch Relief Society president gave the opening prayer. It was an ordinary situation that occurs over and over in the Church, so I was not prepared for the feeling of electricity that came over me.

As the sister began to pray I knew that an extraordinary and powerful human being was pleading with the Lord on behalf of the small congregation. She knew the Lord, was spiritually alive and alert, and had a sound understanding of the gospel. There in Colombo the Lord was operating through one of his spiritual giants. It was a simple but edifying moment of the kind one does not easily have without the opportunity to be involved outside his or her own society.

Tolerant people become that way partly because they continually enlarge the storehouse of experience to include people of new countries and cultures, gaining new insights as they seize opportunities and keep their eyes, ears, and hearts open.

Elder Merlin R. Lybbert of the Seventy shared with me some counsel that Elder Boyd K. Packer gave him before the Lybberts left Salt Lake City for a three-year assignment in Asia. "Take time to get a feeling for your assigned places," Elder Packer had said. "Stay an extra day and find out what is going on. Don't rush from place to place in a frenzy." Elder Lybbert shared that insight with me as I joined him in Asia.

While Elder Lybbert and I visited Vietnam, we found ourselves with an afternoon free of responsibility. We could have rested in our quarters arranged by our Vietnamese hosts. We had already enjoyed a tour of some of the sights of Hanoi that were of great interest to us. We had also captured a feeling for the long and continuous history of the Vietnamese people at their historical museum.

Elder Lybbert suggested we spend the afternoon strolling through downtown Hanoi. On that hot and humid day we saw how the Vietnamese people live, what their commerce looks like firsthand, and how helpful and friendly they are. And we sensed everywhere the intense motivation they have to speak and understand English as a second (or third or fourth) language.

We also visited children involved in their after-school programs, discussed the creation of Vietnamese cartoons with a company newly privatized by one of the government agencies, and did some window shopping. As we strolled around the lake in the center of Hanoi, we watched couples enjoying the scenery and the lawns. The Vietnamese people impressed us with their vitality and industry.

Elder Packer was right. Tolerant people get out of their hotel rooms and off the beaten track. They often take an extra day to use in the high adventure of getting to know people. Taking the extra day adds to their storehouse of tolerance. They cannot have too much of it.

During a trip I took to India on Church business with two of the Apostles and their wives, General and Mrs. Rodrigues invited us to dinner at their home. I sat next to Jean Rodrigues, the general's wife. She was interested in learning more about the Church we represent because she spends much of her time with various charitable organizations created to meet some of the vast needs of the people in her land. Our conversation ranged through many subjects. The general, whom we had met at a reception two nights earlier, amazed us with his grasp of United States history and politics. His conversation was sprinkled with anecdotes and incidents from his extensive reading.

General Rodrigues's knowledge of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints was not extensive, but again, because of his habit of wide reading, he had read his history and knew much more about us than the average person would know. He wanted to learn more and had the benefit of two Apostles of the Church in his home for the evening. We made new friends that evening, including a broad-minded and tolerant world military leader who had earned his tolerance in part by wide reading combined with a deep interest in people. The kind of reading the general had done is the price he had paid to acquire a heightened interest in a religion that is still remote from the common experience of his country. This example illustrates the point that tolerant people read about other people and their countries.

When can we find time to do the reading suggested here? It is hard to tuck in a little reading during a busy day, but if we want to read we will find an early hour, or a late half hour, and snatch a moment here and there to do it. Over years of time the minutes add up, and we enjoy the pleasure of learning about other people in the process. Reading books, articles, and pamphlets-when added to conversations and interviews in which we can ask questions and listen to those with experience and knowledge-all heighten interest in others. That interest inevitably adds to our knowledge and nudges us toward greater tolerance.

One of my university professors at UCLA was Arvo Van Alstyne. Few had more desire to learn and know than he. He was constantly reading, asking questions, writing, mastering the details of his assignments, and showing that same curiosity and interest in his Church assignments and in people. His tolerance and goodwill toward people were obvious to observers wherever he lived and worked. His habit of reading widely was a cornerstone of his breadth and tolerance.

It is a great blessing to live in a day when books and articles are so readily available at little cost to those knowing how to use public libraries. Reading about countries, their histories, and their people is the habit and practice of tolerant and wise people.

Tolerant people avoid these habits, speaking positively of those who differ from them because they enjoy positive feelings about them.

In one of his discourses, Joseph Smith quoted the thirteenth chapter of 1 Corinthians and then commented: "Don't be limited in your views with regard to your neighbor's virtue, but beware of self-righteousness, and be limited in the estimate of your own virtues, and not think yourselves more righteous than others; you must enlarge your souls towards each other, if you would do like Jesus, and carry your fellow-creatures to Abraham's bosom."R2 [^2]

These broad views advised by Joseph Smith are a good summary of this particular principle and practice followed by tolerant people.

Tolerant people strive to avoid stereotyping others. Stereotyping is a reprehensible habit and practice.

At times stereotyping has escalated into a policy so destructive as to defy all reason. The destruction of Jews by Hitler's policy, the murder of kulaks by Stalin, and the massacre of Hindus by Muslims and Muslims by Hindus in India following independence from England are examples. Newspaper reports of today describe programs of "ethnic cleansing" involving Muslims, Serbs, and Croats in Bosnia-Herzegovina. There is no letup in this evil activity.

People use labeling and stereotyping to justify evil ideas, the consequences of which are intolerant acts. Pin an impure label on another and the act of intolerance that follows becomes a religious or patriotic duty. Intolerance then overcomes love and fairness and sets aside normal, decent impulses of humanity.

When we stereotype, we generalize about a person, placing the person in a category instead of looking at the person as he or she really is. We draw mental pictures of people, giving them characteristics we have decided are common to the group or category in which we place them. We need to be wary of such generalizations, especially if the picture that emerges is negative. Mark Twain's fictional character Huckleberry Finn was prejudiced against black people until he became acquainted with Jim, the runaway slave, and grew to love him as an individual quite apart from his race.

One of the realities of life on this planet is the complexity of the human race that inhabits it. As I write this book our home is Hong Kong. I often hear Americans make generalizations about Chinese people. It is convenient to think of the Chinese as a single race, but that nation and its races are incredibly diverse. It is a mixture of many different ethnic groups. For example, among those we call Chinese are the Hans, the Mengus, the Huis, the Zangs, and the Mans, just to name a few of the more prominent groupings. In Hong Kong one hears Cantonese, Shanghai, Hui, Hakka, Mandarin, and Wu dialects spoken. The Chinese are an amalgam of diverse groups whose languages, cultures, and nationalities defy accurate stereotyping.

If stereotyping in negative ways were only inaccurate and misleading, that would arguably be merely fuzzy, unhelpful, and imprecise thinking, lacking in reality. We know, however, that stereotyping is more dangerous than that and needs to be confronted when it becomes a growing and general phenomenon in a society. The massacres of Armenians, Cambodians, Hindus, Muslims, Jews, and the Russian middle class all have roots in intolerant ideas, stereotyping, and unchallenged rhetoric.

In the 1980s a referendum removed from the Idaho State Constitution a law forbidding Mormons to hold office. The law obviously had been both unenforceable and unenforced for many decades, but it was still a part of the Idaho Constitution until removed. A sad footnote to the referendum that successfully removed the offending anti-Mormon provision from the constitution of Idaho was that more than one hundred thousand citizens voted against removing it. In California similar unenforced and unenforceable laws discriminating against Native Americans and Japanese Americans have only recently been repealed as well.

We are not, therefore, discussing a nice little benign principle of human behavior but are attacking a central problem, unsolved to this date. Intolerance is a powder keg ready to explode into racial, religious, and national wars, several of which are raging right now. I express concern, alarm, and dismay about negative laws, policies, and official intolerance. This official intolerance starts with negative comments about another race, religion, or nationality. It feeds on jokes that make a class, group, profession, or race the butt of the joke. The offending fare served daily includes Polish jokes, doctor and lawyer stories, and any number of other common and ordinary ways of stereotyping and negatively comparing "them" with "us."

As these negative practices gather momentum and begin to take on a life of their own, they become not only difficult to stop but also dangerous. Jewish leaders have established organizations to challenge anti-Semitism quickly and bluntly, remembering a long and bloody history in which the Jews have been victimized by others.

Saying negative things about "them" and drawing odious comparisons are human tendencies that tolerant people fight.

In 1981 the Church asked our family to leave Los Angeles, where our roots were deep, and to live for three years in Idaho to serve the people and missionaries living and working there. We placed our home on the market for lease.

Bill Farnsworth's employer, a major international bank, transferred Bill and his wife, Ella, to southern California. Hearing that our home was available for lease, they made an appointment to see if they should consider it for a temporary family home. We lived in one of the West Los Angeles wards blessed with good leadership and talent. The Farnsworths decided against leasing our home after they conferred with local Church leaders.

We learned that their most important goal in choosing a home was to move into an area where they could serve. Their choice had little to do with prestige or comfort, although a comfortable home was important for a person in Bill's professional position. They found a home in the Wilshire Ward in the inner city, where they both gave great and unselfish service in the community at large and in the Church. They immediately rolled up their sleeves and created a place for themselves. These were habits they had picked up during their years of moving from place to place in their company.

We miss out in life when we wait until conditions are just right and all of our needs are met before we become serving and caring neighbors and a part of our communities.

Travelling abroad, my wife and I typically meet expatriates whose assignments require them to live for several years in a foreign country. Invariably they are desperately needed in both their employment and in the Church. Many have felt a calling to be there. In their homes, always temporary places of abode, they have created a haven of peace and beauty for themselves, their families, and their friends.

Often their homes become a church on Sunday and a school during the week. Pictures from home, new objects found in their temporary place of residence, and mementos of other assignments in other locations brighten and cheer their temporary homes. Usually they learn to speak the language of their adopted land, at least in part, and always they increase the circle of their friends as they learn to love the people among whom they reside. Ammon's statement to Lamoni exemplifies their attitude: "I desire to dwell among this people for a time; yea, and perhaps until the day I die" (Alma 17:23).

Americans often show little interest in the languages and cultures of others. We are somewhat spoiled because the English language has become the language of commerce. People everywhere are studying English. We could, nevertheless, show more interest in studying and mastering other languages, both in school and as we travel and live abroad. And we often look down on others who struggle with English, even though we are aware of how difficult it is for us to speak in another language.

Those whose employment does not provide opportunities to live abroad can develop similar practices and attitudes wherever they live. Much of the battle to achieve tolerance lies in attitudes of the mind and heart. The world is becoming smaller, in that races, cultures, and nationalities now live together in virtually all of the cities of the world. When we are alert we find ways of serving, associating with, and knowing those of other races, cultures, and nationalities, while studying their language and their ways.

The point of this attitude and practice is that tolerant people are not so caught up in their own lives and comforts-although they must and do pay attention to these necessary parts of life-that they fail to seize opportunities every day, wherever they live and work, to learn and know and serve. Opportunities seem to crop up easily and naturally in their lives and they seize them, not waiting for perfect conditions to arise in the future. They live in the present tense.

In our travels as Church officers we are sometimes entertained in the homes of those not of our faith. Business, professional, and educational leaders enjoy these opportunities regularly. The majority of those who entertain us know enough about the Latter-day Saints and their habits to be aware of the Word of Wisdom. Usually in a spirit of tolerance and goodwill our hosts offer a drink of ice water or a soft drink during cocktail time and avoid off-color or suggestive jokes and stories. In other words, our hosts consider our needs, implying that our differences are acceptable. We should emulate this tolerant practice as opportunity comes to us. Remember also that our versatility can be measured by the extent to which we make adjustments to meet the needs of others.

People criticized Jesus because he often ate with publicans and sinners. He did not apologize, explaining that it was his work and his duty to live among and teach those who did not know and understand his gospel. His followers sometimes exhibited less goodwill.

President Heber J. Grant loved to play golf, and some of his golfing companions were not Church members. Knowing of their habits and the respect they held for his differing habits, he would wander away occasionally to give them a chance to indulge their usual habits.

Tolerant families find ways to include people from other faiths in their lives. When members of other faiths come into their neighborhood, they include them whenever possible in their activities. How is it likely to make members of other faiths feel if we exclude their children from all activities and parties because they are not Latter-day Saints? How tolerant would such a practice be? As a purely hypothetical example, how would we feel if we knew we were going to live in a predominantly Catholic neighborhood and that our children would have no playmates, would not be welcome at any parties, and would have no friends, because our neighbors desired to keep their families from being contaminated by Latter-day Saints? Are we sometimes guilty of this kind of intolerance and discrimination?

Many times others' fears and intolerance lead them to accuse Latter-day Saints of being offensive and intolerant. Even if the Latter-day Saints do not warrant many of the allegations of narrowness and intolerance, we can improve our tolerance and let down our barriers. We can be less reluctant to join others in social and community experiences and invite others into our lives. We can emphasize similarities rather than underline differences in life-style. We can be more aware of how our actions affect others and, with empathy, minimize offensiveness. We can be as inclusive as possible and sincerely interested in others. We shall probably never overcome fully the misunderstandings and the allegations of intolerance, but every improvement will be beneficial, both to Latter-day Saints and their friends and neighbors.

One of the important and little-heeded teachings of the Savior is his injunction to "judge not, that ye be not judged" (Matt. 7:1). We should not judge our neighbor, and he should not judge us.

We need to be less judgmental of other Church members as well. We should separate the sinner from the sin and the life-style from the doctrine, do less gossiping, and inflict less harm. Only the bishopric and stake presidency must judge the members, and then only to bring them under Church discipline.

Yes, we have much to learn from the tolerance and graciousness of people from all faiths. We can hold fast to everything we believe without making others feel unnecessarily uncomfortable and unwelcome in our lives. The Lord showed us the way and expects no less of us.

We need to be especially careful in communities where a majority of the neighbors are Latter-day Saints. We probably cannot avoid all misunderstandings, but feelings of acceptance and inclusion are a goal toward which we should move. We can continually improve our empathy, understanding, and ability to work effectively with people of all faiths.

This is not to say that tolerant people are predominantly of a particular personality type or that they adopt a special or singular social style. Tolerant people are themselves. They are tall and short, light and dark, extroverts and introverts. All of them, however, have developed the kinds of attitudes and practices that allow them to reach out effectively to touch people of all faiths, people in their neighborhoods, and even strangers they meet.

Tolerant people are interesting to others because they are interested in others. They are joyful people because they cause joy in the lives of others. It was said of President Joseph F. Smith that "he loved a good story and a good joke. There was a good laugh in him always. He had no patience with vile stories, but there was a fine vein of humor in him, and could relate incidents of his early life and entertain the crowd about him as few men ever could."R4 [^4]

I recall attending a Regional Representatives seminar years ago and having a vantage point from which I could watch President Gordon B. Hinckley, then Elder Hinckley of the Council of the Twelve, walk into the crowd of Regional Representatives during a break. He called most of them by name and seemed to recall experiences and incidents of their lives.

President Hinckley is not a bombastic man, but his tolerance, goodwill, and remarkable interest in people have touched many lives. All who have worked closely with President Hinckley can remember some tense situation when he has defused the tension with a quiet remark or a joke and a spontaneous smile. His interest in people in and out of the Church is sincere and his acquaintances legion. He is stimulating.

Kathleen Hill, who worked for Winston Churchill in his home at Chartwell, recalled, "I had never been in a house like that. It was alive, restless. When he [Churchill] went away it was still as a mouse. When he was there it was vibrating."R5 [^5]

People have asked me how President Thomas S. Monson recalls so many experiences, replete with details and names. While he is gifted with a remarkable memory, I believe much of his ability to remember comes from genuine interest in people and from the involvement of his whole soul in whatever he is doing. He is a stimulating, tolerant, and broadly based man with a common touch. This involvement, interest, and joy are part of what it is to be tolerant. It brings excitement and life to otherwise ordinary and drab situations.

More often than not, the excitement and joy of tolerance is created by the habit of asking questions and listening to people. If we show sincere interest in others, they feel it and enjoy being with us. Who can resist someone who genuinely cares about you and is interested in your life, your ideas, and your future?

Great mission presidents take an interest in their missionaries' lives and affect them immeasurably. Why? Because they take time to listen to them, and they serve alongside them. They also interview them, which means they ask questions and listen. No problem is too small or insignificant for mission presidents to show their care and concern. The same thing is true of mission presidents' wives. They become almost family members to each missionary. The aches and pains, real or imagined, the health problems and dreams of the missionaries become the subject of frequent discussions between mission presidents and their companion wives.

When the three-year term is over, mission presidents and wives have a few things they can share with others who have not served in such callings. For the most part, however, it is impossible to relate the true meaning of their service to missionaries because the glory of it lies in small improvements in the lives of missionaries, in a change of heart, in incremental (and for others insignificant) growth in a missionary, and above all, in loving relationships. None of this is easy to share or particularly interesting to others, but tolerance and joy are the inevitable outcome of such experiences.

In summary, tolerant people become vitally interested in others. Their experiences, attitudes, and practices lend joy and warmth to others. They become stimulating because they are genuinely interested in others and show that interest. Their circle of friends constantly widens. They likely do a lot of listening and remember much because of their sincerity.

The scriptures give a firm basis for this practice followed by tolerant women and men. "It must needs be, that there is an opposition in all things. If not so Â� righteousness could not be brought to pass.Â� It must needs be that there was an opposition; even the forbidden fruit in opposition to the tree of life; the one being sweet and the other bitter. Wherefore, the Lord God gave unto man that he should act for himself. Wherefore, man could not act for himself save it should be that he was enticed by the one or the other." (2 Ne. 2:11, 15-16.)

From this scripture we learn that a part of the law of the universe is that there are and should be points of view and various sides to questions. Unfairness, intolerance, and error almost invariably creep into decisions when we consider only one side of an issue. The reason for this is that people's own biased viewpoints skew what they see and decide. Further, if those holding only a particular viewpoint are presented with a complex matter, the result is an incomplete basis for judgment. When we consider only part of the facts and principles relevant to a judgment or decision, it is highly likely that the judgment or decision will be erroneous, biased, or simply poorly conceived.

When a stake president initiates a formal high council for the purpose of "settling important difficulties which might arise in the Church" (D&C 102:2), at least two of the members of the council must speak about the matter-one on each side of the issue-when simpler matters are involved. In more difficult matters, four or, at most, six members of the council must speak, again with equal numbers of advocates on each side of the issue. The further purpose is "to prevent insult or injustice." The procedure allows the council to examine the evidence presented in the matter "in its true light Â� according to equity and justice." (D&C 102:13-16.)

Here is a recognition by the Lord that fairness and justice require a full review of the sides of an issue. Wise decisions on important matters likewise require that opposing voices be heard. People often become heated and biased in their viewpoints, particularly in controversies involving money, reputation, and family matters. Friends take sides in the controversies. Wise people often refuse to take sides or opt to stay out of the controversy. If a decision or judgment must be made, however, thoughtful and tolerant people carefully weigh both or all sides of the matter before they draw conclusions, make decisions, or take action.

Tolerant people develop the habit of withholding judgment until they have procedurally taken these steps. They are able in that way to avoid being tossed to and fro by heated arguments and persuasive advocacy.

This patience and forbearance are easily misunderstood in real-life situations. People in heated controversy do not want or appreciate tolerance. Rather they want agreement and joinder in righteous indignation against the other side. But we usually don't fully understand a matter until we know all of the facts and understand all of the arguments.

Alfred P. Sloan, the president and chief architect of General Motors Company in the 1920s, postponed important decisions if all who spoke on the matters agreed with the proposed decisions. An important matter was not decided until it was understood and it could not be understood, without opposition or testing.R6 [^6]

Parents who jump to conclusions without hearing their children's version of a story, or who always believe their children no matter what evidence is available to the contrary, are not wise. They can become intolerant and in the process harm their children, even in the cases in which they always agree with their children no matter what the evidence to the contrary might be.

It is inspiring to observe a wise mother, father, or leader at work, balancing and weighing facts and realities, treating others fairly and justly, and making decisions that bless others and stand the test of time. Such habits are as rare as they are universally needed. Unfair and thoughtless decisions harm people. Arbitrary, cruel, and capricious actions of men and governments are inevitably intolerant.

One of God's great attributes is that he is both just and merciful. His judgment will be just because, as the writer of Ecclesiastes observed, "God shall bring every work into judgment, with every secret thing, whether it be good, or whether it be evil" (Eccl. 12:14). As described in John's Revelation, the final judgment will thoroughly analyze the facts from the books (see Rev. 20:12).
