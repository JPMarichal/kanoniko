John K. Carmack, Tolerance: Principles, Practices, Obstacles, Limits ( 1993)

Chapter Seven

Tolerant Friends

We talk of choosing our friends, but friends are self-elected.

—Ralph Waldo Emerson

The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints has enjoyed many tolerant friends who were not members of the Church but who came to the Church's aid in times of challenge and distress.

We have read much about the intolerance of enemies of the Church who seemed obsessed with harming the Church. There have been many of these, and they continue to surface, going public with activities and writings designed to hurt and belittle the Church. This spirit of opposition began when young Joseph Smith, who lived on a farm near Palmyra, New York, experienced persecution for saying he had seen a vision. Detailing those activities is not the purpose of this book.

In this chapter emphasis will be on the positive contributions made by many friends who, for one reason or another, chose to come to the aid of the Church during times of need. Perhaps we hear less about these friends because acts of tolerance and kindness do not generally make exciting copy in newspapers and on radio and television broadcasts. If we counted the number of these friends, as well as the vast number of men and women of goodwill who do not make themselves known, we might find that "they that be with us are more than they that be with them" (2 Kgs. 6:16). Many such friends are quietly there, ready to come forward when a pressing need arises.

Before the Church was organized on 6 April 1830, friends came to Joseph Smith's aid. When he desperately needed a little money, Martin Harris gave him gifts and loans. When he needed a secretary or scribe to write for him, Oliver Cowdery appeared on his doorstep in Harmony, Pennsylvania. When he needed peace in which to translate and complete the Book of Mormon for publication, Peter Whitmer made his busy farmhouse available for Joseph, Emma, and Oliver. Even Joseph's father-in-law, critical of his activities, provided a home for Joseph and Emma in Harmony, Pennsylvania, for a time. Friends came as they were needed.

Our friends also come that way. In a sense they come by grace. As Emerson said, we do not select them generally. They come as we need them, tolerant and empathetic men and women of goodwill. It is ironic that the more the Church sought refuge from disfavor and persecution, the more the Saints needed the sunshine of tolerance and goodwill. As they sought to get away from the world, the Saints found they needed tolerant friends who were in the world. These tolerant friends saved Church members time and again from peril, taught them how to deal with dangerous situations, and opened doors of opportunity to them.

A review of Church history reveals a number of these men and women armed with tolerance, a desire to serve, and love for the Saints as their only motivation. The Church needed them then and it needs them now. In a sense, this chapter will be a case study in tolerance, the cases being the good works of actual friends who sacrificed time, energy, talent, and sometimes personal health for the Saints. The clear implication is that Church members today should not only be grateful for them but also return the favor by showing that same friendship and tolerance for others who need our help. We should be especially interested in helping those who, just as the Saints often were in the past, are almost friendless.

An example of someone unexpectedly befriending the Church arose simply and naturally in the context of a meeting in Los Angeles a few years ago. It was during the height of the campaign for and against the Equal Rights Amendment when news stories bashed the Church almost nightly. The person giving the invocation to this totally nondenominational gathering was a layman from a community church. Among other things he prayed for was a plea for the "Mormon church that seems to be having so much difficulty right now." It startled me, but helps make the point that there are more women and men of tolerance out there than we realize.

If ever the Church needed a friend, it was during the trying Missouri period of Church history. Because it was a time of great intolerance, there are lessons to learn from it that will be the subject of chapter 8.

Looming as a major figure in the tapestry of our history is a tall, distinguished attorney of Liberty, Clay County, Missouri, named Alexander William Doniphan. A respected attorney for thirty years in Liberty, under the firm name of Atchison and Doniphan, and prominent enough to merit a public statue placed in Richmond, Missouri, in 1918, Doniphan befriended Joseph Smith. Representing Joseph as his lawyer, Doniphan developed not only tolerance for him and the Latter-day Saints but also loyalty and respect.

The governor of Missouri ordered the citizens to arm and join together in a militia to meet a perceived threat to public order caused by the presence of the Church there. Doniphan felt he should do his civic duty by serving in that militia. He accepted an appointment as brigadier general. He must have been torn between that civic duty and his love for Joseph and the Latter-day Saints, since the primary purpose of the militia was to restore order and peace to the state by driving his friends, the Latter-day Saints, completely out of Missouri.

During negotiations designed to avert further bloodshed, Doniphan persuaded Joseph and other leaders to surrender to the militia. He also persuaded the Saints to give up their arms and to prepare to leave the state forever. The negotiations and resultant agreements probably avoided even worse bloodshed than the Saints had already experienced. Though he was a leader in the militia, Doniphan did not commit the acts of intolerance by the militia against the Latter-day Saints.

Doniphan performed his best-known service to Joseph Smith by disobeying a military order. His superior, General Lucas, ordered Doniphan as commander of the First Brigade to shoot Joseph Smith and several other Church leaders, all without a hearing or following other legal procedures. Doniphan's reply memo to Lucas's astonishing and lawless order is classic, revealing the principles and courage of the man. It is short and pointed:

It is cold-blooded murder. I will not obey your order. My brigade shall march for Liberty tomorrow morning, at 8 o'clock; and if you execute these men, I will hold you responsible before an earthly tribunal, so help me God.

A. W. Doniphan,

Brigadier-GeneralR1 [^1]

This bold action saved Joseph's life. General Lucas blinked in the face of Doniphan's boldness, doubtless knowing of his reputation for integrity and influence in Missouri affairs. The militia placed the prisoners in jail, where they awaited trial for nearly five months before they escaped during a change of venue under circumstances that have led most students of this period of history to conclude that their jailers allowed them to escape.

Few friends have exceeded Alexander Doniphan for fairness, tolerance, and service. The history of the Church would have been much different without him.

During that same Missouri era, a woman befriended Joseph and the Latter-day Saints. She was the wife of General Moses Wilson, who had charge of Joseph's transportation to jail. He brought Joseph into his home on the way to prison. Speaking of that night, General Wilson said, "He was a very remarkable man. I carried him into my house, a prisoner in chains and in less than two hours my wife loved him better than she did me." Mrs. Wilson was ever after tolerant and helpful to the Church, once riding thirty miles to warn the Latter-day Saint missionaries of a plot to punish them that she had overheard.R2 [^2]

The Saints next moved to Illinois, homeless and hungry. The citizens of Quincy, Illinois, befriended the Saints and housed them while they decided on their future course and awaited the fate of their prophet. After Joseph escaped and joined them, the Saints continued to need help and tolerance. They found it in the person of Hiram Kimball.

Kimball had purchased hundreds of acres of land in and near the little town of Commerce, Illinois, situated on the Mississippi River. He also owned and operated a general mercantile store that served as a gathering place for the Saints. Joseph purchased from Kimball on favorable terms a large portion of his land holdings in Commerce. His store became a place of refuge for the Saints where they could acquire the things they needed on generous terms. The Saints built their homes on that property. Sincerely touched by the plight of the Latter-day Saints, Kimball freely gave them the hand of fellowship and tolerance. Although not then a member of the Church, he later gave a lot in Nauvoo to each member of the Council of the Twelve at an 1841 Christmas party.

Hiram Kimball's greatest service may have been in lending his name and prestige to the Saints' community-building enterprise. He and Daniel H. Wells, another friend unaffiliated with the Church, gave standing to the Saints and their community by serving on the Nauvoo City Council with Latter-day Saints and on a number of other community council positions of prominence. In 1843, Hiram Kimball was converted and joined the Church. He married a prominent Latter-day Saint, Sarah Granger. When the mobs drove the Saints out of Nauvoo, Kimball cast his lot with the Saints and joined them on their trek to the Salt Lake Valley.

Wells provided an aura of fairness and justice to the watchful outside world in Illinois with his ability, interest, and service in civic and municipal affairs. He became attached to Joseph and even served as brigadier general of the Nauvoo Legion, a military unit that the city of Nauvoo organized for defensive purposes. Wells refused to join in the clamorous intolerance of the growing mobs emerging around Nauvoo who were motivated by hate and prejudice.

When a mob murdered Joseph and his brother Hyrum, Wells experienced the deep sorrow and indignation other friends of the martyrs felt, and then decided to cast his lot with the Saints. On 9 August 1846, he joined the Church and remained faithful and active until his death. During the three-day siege of Nauvoo by the mobs, Wells was a tower of strength. He was among the last to leave Nauvoo when the Saints made the decision to evacuate. He obtained one of the cannon balls the mobs fired against them and sent it to the governor of Illinois as a specimen of the state's failure to keep the promise that its governor made to ensure the safety of the Saints. In Utah he served as Second Counselor to President Brigham Young for twenty years.

Without acts of tolerance by many friends, the Saints and their cause would have been in even more serious jeopardy in Palmyra, Harmony, Kirtland, Missouri, and Illinois. When we think about the intolerance and prejudice that the Latter-day Saints faced, we should also remember the tolerance and service so many others have freely given.

Diminutive in stature but a giant in character, Thomas L. Kane next comes on the scene of Church history. Kane, who lived in Pennsylvania, possessed a fine sense of fairness, was well educated, and-through his own character and service, building on that of his parents-had developed a place of honor in Pennsylvania with a reputation that reached to the highest levels of the United States. He became for the Latter-day Saints a quintessential friend for all seasons.

Kane heard about the Saints during a meeting in Philadelphia that he had attended when he was twenty-four years old. Elder Jesse Little had addressed a group on the subject of Mormonism. Kane invited Little to his home after the meeting for further discussions. The story of the Latter-day Saints engaged Kane's sympathy and interest immediately. He learned firsthand about the problems of the Saints and their intended exodus to the West. Fascinated, he decided to get involved. Kane gave Little an introduction to the vice president of the United States and then decided to join Little on his visit to the capital. There he assisted in negotiating the terms of the enlistment of five-hundred young Church members who were to be formed into a battalion to participate in the campaign to win the West from Mexico. Kane had become a friend of the Saints.

The federal government dispatched a team to deliver the official orders to the Latter-day Saints and to form the Mormon Battalion. Kane decided to join the team so that he could get acquainted with Brigham Young and the Saints. He laid eyes on them for the first time on the Iowa side of the Missouri River at a spot the Saints later named Kanesville out of respect for his friendship and service to the Church. There he became seriously ill. The Saints nurtured him back to health. His love and admiration for them as a people blossomed.

From that point until his death, through many days of trial and difficulty for the Saints, Kane's tolerance and love for them never faltered. It seemed that he was there for them during their darkest hours of distress. Though he never joined the Church, he felt deeply the Saints' fellowship and said he had learned "to know from the heart, that I love you, and you love me in turn."R3 [^3]

Years later, after the Saints had established a new community in the valleys of Utah, United States president James Buchanan accepted the allegations pressed on him by the Church's enemies to the effect that the Mormons out in the Utah Territory were in rebellion against the federal government. He reacted by sending about 2,500 troops with an equal number of support-team members to quell the rebellion. The commander was Colonel Albert Sidney Johnston. The army commenced west in the summer of 1857.

To Brigham Young and the Saints the pending arrival of the troops was nearly the last straw after their experiences in Missouri and Nauvoo. They vowed that intolerance and mob action would never again be allowed to go unchallenged by strong counteraction. They prepared to meet the army in unique defensive ways, looking at the army's coming to Utah as a major challenge to the Saints' peace and independence. War and disaster appeared to be brewing. In fact, this period is known as the Utah War.

Hearing of the potentially tragic events unfolding and involving his friends, Colonel Kane, though not physically well, sprang into action. His tolerance was the roll-up-your-sleeves kind described in chapter 2. He wrote to President Buchanan, offering his services as a mediator. Buchanan, who by then was having second thoughts about the wisdom of the martial events he had set in motion, gratefully accepted Kane's offer. Kane, at his own expense, started immediately for Utah under the assumed name of Dr. Osborne, botanist. He caught a ship to Panama, crossed the Isthmus of Panama, and reached the California coast. He first caught up with the Mormons at their settlement in San Bernardino, then hurried on to Salt Lake City for a reunion with his old friends, including President Brigham Young.

After conferring with his friends, he rushed quickly to intercept Colonel Johnston and his army in Wyoming. The newly appointed Utah territorial governor was in the party. Kane, brushing aside Johnston's animosity, convinced Governor Cumming that the Latter-day Saints were not in rebellion. Governor Cumming agreed to a compromise that allowed the army to enter the valley peacefully. Had the compromise failed, the Saints would have carried out a scorched-earth policy in Utah communities, leaving only a wasteland to greet the army upon their arrival. With Kane's intervention, the Utah War blew over without ever erupting into a shooting war.

Kane is buried in front of a Presbyterian church that he built in the town of Kane, Pennsylvania, named for him. The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints has purchased the church, and it is now a visitors' center and chapel. Known as a man of integrity, goodwill, and tolerance, Thomas L. Kane looms large in the history of the Church.

Because of his friendship and tolerance, the Latter-day Saints also revere the name of United States president Millard Fillmore. One travelling through Utah will see signs identifying Millard County and Fillmore City, which are named for him. He was instrumental in assisting Utah to become an official territorial government of the United States. The town of Fillmore formerly served as Utah's state capital.

United States president Zachary Taylor, who was not a friend of the Church, opposed Senator Henry Clay's effort at achieving a great compromise to overcome a slavery-induced deadlock that was impeding the expansion of territories and states. Clay's omnibus bill admitted New Mexico and Utah without reference to slavery as a part of a whole package of proposals. President Taylor, calling the Mormons a pack of outlaws, was not favorable toward the proposal and efforts to achieve territorial status by John M. Bernhisel and Almon W. Babbitt, who represented the Church in this matter. The prospects that the bills would pass did not look good.R4 [^4]

Still, Fillmore was the vice president under Taylor, and as presiding officer of the Senate he held the deciding vote in case there was a deadlock in voting on the Clay bill. He was tolerant and fair by nature, and Bernhisel found him to be friendly.

President Taylor became ill, and died on 9 July 1850, with a vote on the omnibus bill imminent. Congress broke the bill into several parts and enacted them one at a time. Fillmore, who succeeded Taylor as president, was an indispensable part of this process.

A problem caused by the intolerance of the Church's enemies remained. The Church, comprising most of the population of the territory of Utah, wanted strong representation on the team of men appointed to govern the territory. Enemies of the Church opposed any Mormon appointments. Unfriendly and intolerant officials had been the bane of the Latter-day Saints and would continue to be a great problem, but Fillmore told Bernhisel "that officers, by all means, should be appointed among your members."R5r

President Fillmore appointed Brigham Young as governor, to be part of a slate of territorial officers comprised of members and nonmembers. Bernhisel appraised Fillmore as "a noble, high minded, accomplished gentleman, and the more intimately I become acquainted with him, the more he excites my respect and admiration."R6 [^5]

Incidentally, Fillmore turned to Kane for evaluation of Brigham Young in assessing the opposition to his appointment as governor. A Unitarian in faith, Fillmore was tolerant of other religions and could see no reason to deny an office to a person because of his religious affiliation.

On President Fillmore's death in 1874, the Deseret News editorialized that he was "more thoroughly imbued with the fundamental principles of American Government than Â� any President since his time."R7 [^6]

Abraham Lincoln, considered by many to be the greatest president of the United States, was a state legislator in Illinois during the era of Church presence in his state. He knew about the Latter-day Saints and their trials. As far as the record reveals, he was, as his reputation on other matters would suggest, fair, impartial, and tolerant of the Church.

One of Lincoln's best-known statements about the Church illustrates his tolerant approach. T. B. H. Stenhouse, a Church member, interviewed Lincoln, who is reported to have said:

Stenhouse, when I was a boy on the farm in Illinois there was a great deal of timber on the land which we had to clear away. Occasionally we would come to a log which had fallen down. It was too hard to split, too wet to burn and too heavy to move, so we plowed around it. That's what I intend to do with the Mormons. You go back and tell Brigham Young that if he will let me alone, I will let him alone.R8 [^7]

That statement sounds typical of Abraham Lincoln's wisdom and sense of humor. Even if his expression of tolerance is not the active and involved kind described earlier in this book, the statement is still tolerant and fair, and it came from a busy United States president at a time when the question of polygamy aroused the nation.

Lincoln's example of tolerance calls to mind a recorded incident in the New Testament concerning the trials experienced by the Church of Christ then. When the Jewish council met to consider what to do about the Christian activities in and around Jerusalem, the prevailing mood was angry and intolerant. The great Jewish teacher, Gamaliel, suggested to his brethren, "Refrain from these men, and let them alone: for if this counsel or this work be of men, it will come to nought: but if it be of God, ye cannot overthrow it; lest haply ye be found even to fight against God" (Acts 5:38-39).

Tolerance and honesty are closely related. What has been written about the Latter-day Saints has sometimes been not only slanted and dishonest, but also vicious. Charles Dickens was a writer whose work was an exception to that tendency. His reputation as one of the greatest writers in the English language only seems to grow with the passage of time.

Among a collection of Charles Dickens's writings published with the title The Uncommercial Traveller is a piece in which he gives a firsthand account of his visit to the departure docks of London. There he spent some time aboard the emigrant ship Amazon, which was carrying about eight hundred Mormon converts from Great Britain to the United States. Dickens's essay contains a detailed and positive reaction to what he observed. He found that "nobody is in an ill-temper, nobody is the worse for drink, nobody swears an oath or uses a coarse word, nobody appears depressed, nobody is weeping." He further wrote: "These people are so strikingly different from all other people in like circumstances whom I have ever seen, that I wonder aloud, 'What would a stranger suppose these emigrants to be!' "R9 [^8]

As he concluded his essay, Dickens summarized his experience in these words:

What is in store for the poor people on the shores of the Great Salt Lake, what happy delusions they are labouring under now, on what miserable blindness their eyes may be opened then, I do not pretend to say. But I went on board their ship to bear testimony against them if they deserved it, as I fully believed they would; to my great astonishment they did not deserve it; and my predispositions and tendencies must not affect me as an honest witness. I went over the Amazon's side, feeling it impossible to deny that, so far, some remarkable influence had produced a remarkable result, which better known influences have often missed.R10 [^9]

It is refreshing to find an eyewitness's honest description of early Latter-day Saints and to sense the tolerance of this great and famous man.

One of the most colorful and best-known United States presidents was Theodore Roosevelt. He became acquainted with the Latter-day Saints during a speaking trip to Rexburg, Idaho, as a candidate for vice president of the United States. While there, Latter-day Saint leader Ben E. Rich introduced him to the audience with such eloquence that Roosevelt invited Rich to join him in his private car on the railway trip from Idaho Falls to Utah. They spent the better part of the night talking about the Church and its teachings.R11r

Several acts of tolerance endeared Roosevelt to Church members, but his fairness, tolerance, and friendship during the fierce battle to seat Elder Reed Smoot of the Council of the Twelve as a Utah senator is probably the best-known incident. During the fight to seat Senator Smoot, Ben E. Rich and John Henry Smith were sent to Washington, D.C., to assist in the campaign. Utah senator Thomas Kearns, no friend to the Church, introduced them to President Roosevelt in his office. Roosevelt responded, "I know both of these men better than I know you. They are my personal friends." He asked if Reed Smoot was a polygamist, or if he had ever been a polygamist. Their negative reply prompted Roosevelt to say, "By all that's holy, I say to you that Reed Smoot is entitled to his seat in the Senate under the Constitution, and the fact that he is a high church officer makes no difference. I shall do all in my power to help him retain his seat."R12 [^10]

Again, a young restored Church, plagued by prejudice and intolerance, won an important battle for fairness and equality with the help of a tolerant, fair-minded statesman.

The Church probably has more friends today than ever before. We need them all. There can never be too much honest tolerance in the world. Joseph Rosenblatt, a Utah business leader of the Jewish faith, has had a friendly and tolerant relationship with the Church. On one occasion he made these observations:

Brotherhood in this community (Salt Lake City) is that which is practiced by the Latter-day Saints, not simply in words or hospitality Â� , but in a way of life. My father built the first synagogue here in 1900 and finished it in 1903. The only sizable contribution he got came from the LDS Church.

In those years, anything as big as they gave made the difference between success and failure. Such action is there all the time. If you're in trouble Â� the first place you want to go is to see some Church official who will direct you and help.R13 [^11]

We have many friends, but not too many. As Mohammed's son-in-law said, "He who has a thousand friends has not a friend to spare."R14 [^12]

In the 1980s, a case of blatant intolerance brought yet more tolerant action and friendship to our rescue. Some strident enemies of the Church, including former members, pummeled the Church with a movie called The God Makers that surprisingly captured a sizable audience, chiefly among some Protestant church groups. One wonders where their tolerance doctrine was hiding. The movie held up the most sacred ordinances of the Latter-day Saint religion to ridicule. It featured secret tapings taken inside a Latter-day Saint temple, attempting to discredit ordinances that Latter-day Saints hold sacred.

In 1984, the Arizona Chapter of the National Conference of Christians and Jews under NCCJ regional director Donald A. Eagle sponsored a year-long study of the film. The report from this study, distributed nationally, concluded that the film "is essentially unfair, that it does not accurately portray the Mormon faith, Mormon Church history or anything else about the spirit of the Mormon faith."R15r

Eagle emphasized that their report was not so much a defense of the Mormon faith as "a call for respect, understanding and tolerance among religious groups."R16 [^13]

The Church knew that what the NCCJ study said was true, but the statement had great weight coming from an organization with an outstanding reputation for tolerance. When that organization put its reputation on the line for the Church, the effect was devastating to the standing of the film among responsible groups. Such courage and active tolerance are rare, deeply moving, and appreciated.

Many other friends emerged from the NCCJ report. Mesa mayor Al Brooks, a Jew, explained that "what NCCJ has done was not just for any one faith or any one person. It was done because it was the right thing to do."R17 [^14]

That comment is refreshing and commendable. It is what I am trying to convey in this book about tolerance. There is hope for everyone when tolerance goes public without any thought of personal gain.

One more case study illustrating the principles of tolerance and the attitudes and practices of tolerant people will serve as a conclusion to this chapter.

Mayor Theodore Kolleck of Jerusalem, known as Teddy, has battled intolerance in his beloved city that is a religious center for Jews, Christians, Moslems, and many other religions. His powerful, practical brand of tolerance has so far kept Jerusalem from becoming a Beirut or Belfast.

It is not so much that he is a friend of Latter-day Saints as that he has exercised the principle of tolerance as he presides over a deeply divided community-Jerusalem.

Hebrew poet Yehuda Amichai observed:

In a time of war, and continuing wars, Teddy has created a coexistence of a real kind. He doesn't brush it over with sappy sentimentalism about how "the world is one." He is not sentimental at all. He is a romantic of realism. He believes that you can stick to reality, and not paint things over, and you can fall in love with reality as it is. He has made Jerusalem work, not by making it something different, but by making it work as it is."R18 [^15]

Without Teddy Kolleck, students would probably not have the opportunity to enjoy the BYU Jerusalem Center on Mount Scopus. His fair, courageous, and steadfast support during the planning and building of the center, enduring vitriolic opposition by a few powerful Jewish factions in Jerusalem, has made a difference.

Kolleck lives in a three-room apartment, drives his own little automobile, travels through Jerusalem without a bodyguard accompanying him, and has a listed telephone number. He is tough, realistic, and authentic. He is capable of saying to Jerusalem's warring factions, "Look, I'll fix your sewers if you knock off the sermons!"R19 [^16]

The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints has friends throughout the world. They contribute much to the operation of the Church. And they teach us to be more thoughtful, kind, and tolerant friends ourselves.

In conclusion, consider this suggestion in your own life: Choose some of your friends from the friendless. Look for the less honorable and even those who are persecuted, and serve them.

Attitudes and practices of tolerant people unleash energy. Because they are less "me" centered they take a great interest in others. They are not afraid to share their feelings and time with people. All of this makes it stimulating and pleasing to be with tolerant people. Their circle of friends is always expanding. Being Joyful and Stimulating. What is the effect of my presence with others? Do I bring joy, warmth, and stimulation to others? Am I a good listener?

They are not impetuous on important matters. They refrain from jumping to conclusions, knowing that prejudgment is a first cousin to prejudice. Hearing and Understanding All Sides of an Issue. On important matters, do I withhold judgment until I have heard the other side, or sides, of the issue?
