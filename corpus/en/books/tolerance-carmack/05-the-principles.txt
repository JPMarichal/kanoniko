The Principles

Why Understanding the Principles of Tolerance Matters

What are the underlying ideas or principles that form the foundation for tolerance? Knowing the fundamental principles and doctrines that support tolerance is vital. If solid principles guide our conduct and if we understand why these principles are valid, in times of trial and testing these principles will be our anchor. Even daily choices reflect what we understand and believe in our lives. These choices affect not only our lives but also the lives of others and our relations with them.

To illustrate the power of ideas, suppose the principle involved is the doctrine that wives and husbands should love each other and have no intimate relations with any other person (see D&C 42:22-24). Suppose a husband and wife love each other and naturally prefer each other to any others but don't agree with the doctrine previously summarized. They may, nevertheless, be loyal and faithful to each other without having accepted or even carefully considered the issue of chastity. Such a couple may consider the doctrine of chastity irrelevant. Is it?

If other willing participants tempt one marriage partner to engage in extramarital activities, the natural goodness and loyalty of the tempted partner may be sufficient to safeguard against disloyalty and adultery. The probability of remaining chaste and true to one's marriage partner is much stronger, however, when the individual understands and accepts Christ's doctrine of marital fidelity and loyalty, has made a covenant to live that way with his partner, and has made these marriage commitments in solemnity before God.

Likewise, understanding and accepting principles of tolerance generate the underlying power to be tolerant. This is one of the purposes of my writing this book: to enable family, friends, and members of the Church who are motivated to live the gospel of Christ to understand more fully the righteous principles underlying tolerance and thereby deepen their commitment to be tolerant. Following are eight underlying principles of tolerance.

All Men and Women Are Literally Brothers and Sisters

The first principle underlying tolerance is that God is the literal Father of the spirits of men and women (see Acts 17:29; Rom. 8:16; Heb. 12:9). That being true, all humans who live, will live, or who have ever lived are brothers and sisters; they are the offspring of God. In addition, we are all descended from Adam and Eve, our common ancestors. As Paul taught the Greeks in Athens as he stood on Mars Hill, God "hath made of one blood all nations of men for to dwell on all the face of the earth" (Acts 17:26). Joseph Smith and Sidney Rigdon further discovered during their vision concerning life after death that the people of all worlds in the entire universe, not just those people on this earth, "are begotten sons and daughters unto God" (D&C 76:24). Every human being we shall ever see, therefore, is a son or daughter of God and a brother or sister of ours. They are also potential heirs of God and joint-heirs with Jesus Christ (see Rom. 8:16-17; Rev. 3:21).

This is a powerful principle, one that demands we be tolerant of others. When we ignore, belittle, or put someone down, when we treat another as unequal to us or infringe on his or her rights, we are mistreating a member of our own family, a descendant of our Father in Heaven, and a potential joint-heir with Jesus Christ.

"Thou Shalt Love the Lord Thy God. . . ."

The second principle is the commandment to "love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy mind" (Matt. 22:37). Jesus taught this Jewish law in answer to the question, "Master, which is the great commandment in the law?" (Matt. 22:36.)

Tolerance is the inevitable result of living a life filled with love for the Lord. King Benjamin taught that we all need to love the Lord and become reconciled to him. By so doing, a person becomes "as a child, submissive, meek, humble, patient, full of love, willing to submit to all things which the Lord seeth fit to inflict upon him, even as a child doth submit to his father" (Mosiah 3:19). Unfairness and intolerance sadden a person who truly loves the Lord. Tolerance is a natural by-product of living the first great commandment.

"Thou Shalt Love Thy Neighbour as Thyself"

Associated with the love of God is the commandment to love our neighbor as much as we love ourselves (see Matt. 22:39). Jesus answered the question "Who is my neighbor?" with the great parable of tolerance known as the parable of the Good Samaritan. Choosing his cast of characters carefully, the Savior introduced a citizen of Jerusalem desperately needing a neighbor with love and tolerance. Thieves stripped and wounded this citizen, leaving him half-dead by the side of the road. Shunned and ignored by a Jewish priest and then by a Levite, he was left to die. A neighbor, in the broad sense of the word, came by and took care of his wounds, transporting him to an inn and giving care, love, and sufficient funds to pay the bills. The irony and power of the parable lies in the simple identification of the benefactor as a Samaritan, a man from a nation intolerantly labeled by the Jews as half-breeds and infidels. (See Luke 10:30-37.) The despised Samaritan proved to be the neighbor.

This third principle, love of neighbor, is closely related to the first concept, the brotherhood of man, and flows naturally from it. If we are all children of a common Heavenly Father, including Samaritans (representing the despised and less-honored nations of the world), then there is no valid reason for rejecting another person on the basis of nationality and blood.

In light of these principles underlying tolerance, we can more fully understand the basis for what Jesus called "a new commandment" that he gave to his disciples to "love one another; as I have loved you." He further explained that in this way all men would "know that ye are my disciples, if ye have love one to another." (John 13:34-35.)

God Made Men and Women Free and Accountable

The Book of Mormon prophet Samuel the Lamanite told the Nephites in his warning sermon from the wall of Zarahemla, "Behold, ye are free; ye are permitted to act for yourselves; for behold, God hath given unto you a knowledge and he hath made you free" (Hel. 14:30).

In the Doctrine and Covenants, the Lord calls this the "moral agency which I have given unto him [humankind], that every man may be accountable for his own sins in the day of judgment." The Lord goes on to explain that "it is not right that any man should be in bondage one to another. And for this purpose have I established the Constitution of this land [the United States Constitution, with its guarantees of justice and freedom]." (D&C 101:78-79.)

God intended all human beings to have freedom and to be treated justly before the law. His gospel is not forced upon anyone. The Lord's approach is well expressed in the words of an insightful hymn:

He'll call, persuade, direct aright, And bless with wisdom, love, and light, In nameless ways be good and kind, But never force the human mind.R1 [^1]

Tolerance is diminished when governments or tyrants wrest freedom from men and women. If God made people free, all should respect that freedom by treating others with tolerance.

From the premortal existence until now, Satan has sought to destroy the freedom and agency that God gave us and replace them with slavery and tyranny. Satan has influenced the despotism, corruption, injustice, and intolerance we see in people and governments. To impose an untouchable status or any other caste system on men and women in society is wrong. Systems that include such principles are intolerant. No sound doctrine justifies labeling men and women in ways that create an inferior status for some of them.

The acclaimed author and observer of India, M. M. Kaye, made the observation in her autobiography that "young children do not have any caste. Caste is something that they acquire later on-as do we all in our own fashion; together with prejudice and intolerance Â� 'Isn't it sad that our children must grow into people?' "R2 [^2]

One of the great principles of tolerance, therefore, is the doctrine of moral agency and the fact that God made us free. Moral agency, understood and used righteously, results in mutual respect and tolerance.

Mankind Has Right to Religious Freedom

Another principle of tolerance, related to the fourth one and often missing in states placing little or no premium on freedom and human rights, is the fundamental right we have to believe in whatever religion we choose, or to believe in no religion if that is our choice. We also have the right to practice our religion freely and share it with others, as long as that practice and sharing do not harm others or infringe on the freedom God has given everyone.

A statement directly on this point is found in D&C 134 of the Doctrine and Covenants. It is a declaration of beliefs regarding governments and laws in general and was adopted by a conference of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints in 1835:

We believe that religion is instituted of God; and that men are amenable to him, and to him only, for the exercise of it, unless their religious opinions prompt them to infringe upon the rights and liberties of others; but we do not believe that human law has a right to interfere in prescribing rules of worship to bind the consciences of men, nor dictate forms for public or private devotion; that the civil magistrate should restrain crime, but never control conscience; should punish guilt, but never suppress the freedom of the soul. (D&C 134:4.)

Government needs power, but that power should be carefully limited so that people are left free to believe what they want to believe, to speak about those beliefs, and above all to practice their religion and share it with others in peaceful ways. Any society that does not safeguard this principle is intolerant on its face. Infringements of religious freedom still exist, and some "enlightened" governments are eroding this basic concept. Without constant vigilance, prayer, and persuasion this principle will fade and die.

People Should Forgive Other People

Pride, real and imagined wrongs, and bitterness sometimes divide men and women, communities, and nations. Tolerant people forgive. Bringing oneself to forgive another, even when one is clearly a victim, creates an atmosphere in which tolerance flourishes. Friendships and empathy appear in the sunlight of goodwill. "It is only one step from toleration to forgiveness."R3 [^3]

The doctrine of forgiveness is pervasive in the New Testament. It is a foundational doctrine of Christ (see Matt. 6:12, 14-15; 18:15, 21-22). We also find it forcefully and clearly expressed in modern revelation. The essence of the teaching in the Doctrine and Covenants is that only the Lord has enough knowledge and authority to judge who is eligible for divine forgiveness. His judgment will be just because he will know all the facts and laws that govern. We, however, having imperfect knowledge, must forgive all others.The Lord explains that the failure to forgive leaves the greater sin with the embittered one who refuses to forgive. (See D&C 64:7-10.) The Lord also suggests a proper attitude of mind. When we are victimized, we should say in our hearts, "Let God judge between me and thee, and reward thee according to thy deeds." (D&C 64:11.)

Misunderstandings and contention often require someone to commence the process of forgiveness. On one occasion a dear friend requested that I loan him a few dollars and keep the matter between him and me alone, not sharing the transaction with his wife. The request came at an awkward time for me to respond favorably, and in addition I was serving as my friend's priesthood leader. I not only refused to make the loan but also added advice about the improperness of his plan to borrow money without informing his wife.

In the months following I learned that my friend complained about me to mutual friends in the Church. This left me in an interesting situation. I felt that my decision was right and that my advice to him about including his wife in solving his financial problems was also right. On the other hand, he was clearly bitter over the incident. I felt I bore some responsibility for his feelings. I apologized for offending him, asking his forgiveness. I told him that the timing had been personally awkward for me to respond positively to his request for a loan, but that I knew that the way I had turned him down was insensitive. He explained that he had felt humiliated as he left my office. He asked that I forgive him and we expressed our mutual love. Our friendship has endured.

Forgiveness, including forgetting wrongs against us when we are innocent victims, is a healthy reaction to problems. We cannot keep score and await our turn to strike back if we are to develop a truly Christlike character. After we have taken all proper steps to protect ourselves and others, if we find that nothing more can reasonably be done, we should cut our losses, move forward, and treat the person who offended us as a friend and brother. We can say, as Joseph Smith did in answer to Brother W. W. Phelps' plea for forgiveness,

Come on dear Brother since the war is past, For friends at first are friends again at last.R4r

Elder David O. McKay quoted this practical advice given by a discerning writer: "In the very depths of your soul dig a grave; let it be as some forgotten spot to which no path leads; and there, in the eternal silence bury the wrongs which you have suffered. Your heart will feel as if a load had fallen from it and a divine peace come to abide with you."R5r

An unhealthy sign of our times is that people often angrily file a lawsuit, initiating and prosecuting long and protracted litigation over matters that should have been settled or forgiven.R6 [^4]

Tolerant humans are slow to anger and quick to forgive. Whether or not their antagonist forgives them in turn, they freely forgive, leaving judgment to God. They know that not all problems can be solved and are willing to walk away from situations without bitterness or recrimination. This does not mean that they lack intelligence, judgment, or backbone, or that they handle their affairs loosely and without regard to their families' well-being, but rather that their action results from a Christ-like character trait.

Tolerance Embraces Fairness, Justice, Equity, and Mercy

People have a fine sense of what is fair and unfair, and often experience hurt, suffering, and anger when they are treated unfairly or arbitrarily, judged harshly, or ignored as if they have little or no worth.

A corrupt and arbitrary society stifles tolerance. To some it may seem banal to say that governments should operate by laws, not according to the whims of humans, but in that phrase we find an important concept without which tolerance in the larger sense is difficult to achieve. Laws governing us should bear a close relationship to the needs of citizens, especially those laws governing criminal acts. Laws should be fair and processes impartial.

Even the chief executive of the state should be subject to those laws, despite his position of power and prestige. People should be protected against arbitrary action by government. An impartial judiciary is a treasure, often appreciated only when lost.

Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn's excellent little novel One Day in the Life of Ivan Denisovich,7 illustrates the point. Ivan was sent to prison camp without fair procedures. He spent his entire energy finding ways to beat the corruption and survive. Tolerance became almost irrelevant. The name of the game was survival. Close observers of the Soviet Union in its last stages testify that nearly all of the people became corrupt out of the necessity to survive. Almost everything became corrupt, unfair, and lawless.

A recent example of official intolerance was the era known as the Cultural Revolution that extended throughout all of China. Authorities imprisoned men, women, and youths for years, and murdered, exiled, tortured, and starved others because the Red Guard found them guilty, without trial, of any number of trivial offenses.

The point is that in their relationships with others and with the state, people have a desperate need for fairness, justice, equity, and mercy. Without these principles operating somewhat effectively between people and their government, the winds of intolerance sweep through a nation and chill and penetrate to the bone. Pervasive intolerance replaces tolerance.

The Lord Is the Rock of Tolerance

The eighth underlying principle of effective tolerance is the most important one. The Atonement was the greatest act of tolerance in the history of the world. The death of Christ completed a universally applicable redemption for mankind. On condition of repentance, the Atonement ransomed every person who ever has lived or will live on the earth (and was even greater in scope than that). Christ accepted the burdens and sins of those who plotted against him, cursed and spat on him, crowned him with thorns, nailed him to a cross, and mocked him while he hung there. He died for all of them and for us, thus opening the doors of resurrection and salvation to all. We, of course, must do our part to enjoy the full benefits of Christ's atonement.

His acts of tolerance in those last hours on earth are still the acts by which we can measure our personal tolerance. Jesus used his miraculous healing power once again to restore the ear of one of his captors whom Peter had slashed with a sword. Jesus told Peter to put up his sword and not resist the arrestors, that He could have called legions of angels to His rescue had He wished to do so, but that He must fulfill His mission. The Savior walked voluntarily and courageously to his death, a duty assigned to him as our Redeemer. (See Matt. 26:51-57; Luke 22: 49-51; John 18:10-11.)

Again, on the cross, Christ's reaction to those hurling taunting words at him was to plead, "Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do" (Luke 23:34). When one of the criminals hanging on a cross near Jesus aimed a vicious remark at him, Jesus uttered no condemnation (see Luke 23:39-43). Jesus was also gentle with the various judges who sat on his case (see Luke 23:3; John 18:23, 33-37), except that he refused to honor King Herod with a reply to a direct question (see Luke 23:8-9).

The Savior showed all of us the way to tolerance. His life, and particularly the events leading to his crucifixion, include the greatest acts of tolerance known in history. With that perfect tolerance, can we think of even one instance during which he compromised his own beliefs? No, it is not necessary to compromise in order to be tolerant. Jesus is rightly called the rock. Tolerance anchors on that rock.
