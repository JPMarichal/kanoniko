John K. Carmack, Tolerance: Principles, Practices, Obstacles, Limits ( 1993)

Chapter Eight

Lessons of Adversity

Adversity is not without comforts and hopes.

-Francis Bacon

Chapter 7 presented a historical overview of the positive contributions made to the Church by kind and tolerant friends who elected themselves to the tasks of rescue, aid, and succor. These cases helped establish the positive role tolerance has played in the rise and growth of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, even its survival. Thus departing from a general tendency of Church historians to chronicle the trials and establish the facts of intolerance directed against the Church, that chapter cast Church history in a manner that allows us to say, "We are grateful for survival, for friends, for tolerance, and for the lessons of adversity. Thank the Lord for tolerance!"

The principle that there must be opposition in all things has been quoted in a previous chapter. Adversity tests points of view and tempers theories and principles so that the fine steel of practical truth emerges. This chapter contains an analysis of one of the difficult eras of Church history. The purpose of this chapter is not to recount the intolerant facts so that we can say yet again, "Look at the intolerance we endured," but rather to draw lessons from adversity.

From jail in Liberty, Missouri, during March of 1839, Joseph Smith dictated a letter designed as a general epistle to insecure and homeless Church members who had been driven from Missouri. The portion of the letter in the following quote, written under inspiration, has been designated as revelation and accepted as a part of the Doctrine and Covenants. The Lord said to Joseph: "If thou art called to pass through tribulation; if thou art in perils among false brethren; if thou art in perils among robbers; if thou art in perils by land or by sea; if thou art accused with all manner of false accusations; if thine enemies fall upon thee; Â� know thou, my son, that all these things shall give thee experience, and shall be for thy good. The Son of Man hath descended below them all. Art thou greater than he?" (D&C 122:5-8.)

What good came from the Missouri years? What lessons can we mine from it? What have we learned about tolerance from the Missouri era? In the following paragraphs, seven ideas are presented that attempt to answer these questions.

Chastening and Intolerance Can Teach Important Lessons

Speaking to the Church as an institution, the Lord spoke through the Prophet at Fishing River, Missouri, on 22 June 1834. He told them:

Were it not for the transgressions of my people, speaking concerning the church and not individuals, they might have been redeemed even now.

But behold, they have not learned to be obedient to the things which I required at their hands, but are full of all manner of evil, and do not impart of their substance, as becometh saints, to the poor and afflicted among them;

And are not united according to the union required by the law of the celestial kingdom.Â�

And my people must needs be chastened until they learn obedience, if it must needs be, by the things which they suffer. (D&C 105:2-4, 6.)

Acts of intolerance buffeted Latter-day Saints in Missouri, giving them experience and eventually redounding to their benefit. The Lord does not always intervene when intolerance and hate raise their ugly heads. And that good may come from such adversities does not excuse the intolerant and cruel humans who perpetrate them. (See Alma 14:10-11.)

But in the Missouri experience, there was also unrighteousness in the camps of the Saints. The Lord pointed to transgressions, disobedience of the Lord's commandments, failure to care for the poor among them, and lack of unity as contributing to their troubles in Missouri. He called the Saints to repent, but from the events that transpired over the next five years, they obviously had more lessons and chastening to experience, all to their long-range, collective benefit.

Even acts of intolerance teach and sanctify us.

Unity and Order Out of Disunity and Confusion

The Lord pointed to disunity as a problem in the revelation quoted above. The dark days of disunity, punctuated by competition for control and position, that started in Kirtland continued in Missouri. This disunity led to disfellowshipment and excommunication of some of the leading lights of the Church. With some of the leading brethren unwilling to support Joseph Smith's policies and leadership, the Church turned to its disciplinary system for relief, only to find its organizational structure confused, with unclear lines of authority and untested procedures for handling dissent, apostasy, conspiracy, and disloyalty.

The necessity of survival when under assault by Missouri mobs led the Church to act decisively, beginning a process of clarification that led to finding a way to cleanse itself from wrenching internal quarrels and save the growing body of the Saints from extermination, starvation, and the other horrors brought on by mob rule and state militia attacks. Of necessity the Church learned it could survive as an institution if it took strong measures to cleanse and unify the inner vessel. Every society based on established doctrines and principles, and valuing unity and loyalty, needs clear, tolerant, and predictable disciplinary procedures that teach, correct, and unify the organization and its members.

As a result of the grave trials and dangers of Missouri, Church leaders firmed up their procedures, finding the will to excommunicate even such luminaries as Oliver Cowdery, David Whitmer, and William W. Phelps who, though arguments could be stated for the logic of their viewpoints, had become disloyal to the Church and its prophet. These lessons are not far from the institutional memory of the Church today, and the benefits of the Missouri era continue.

The Church continues to have dissent, both of the loyal and disloyal variety. A tolerant church has room for differences, but we should confront differences that are carried too far. Dissent that is based on anger, hate, or excessive pride, or that is designed to disrupt and paralyze the Church is dangerous. Human nature has not changed much since the Missouri period in Church history. Conflict is always lurking nearby. The opposition experienced during the Missouri era strengthened the Church by forcing it to recognize the danger of disunity and take steps to purify the Church.

Danger of Fanaticism and Extremism

One of the sad and destructive footnotes of the Missouri melee was the rise of a small element of fanatical, radical, and unbalanced members of the Church, which will be discussed later in this chapter. It is clear that on the whole, however, the Church's participation in the violent scenes of Missouri was defensive in response to mob action by Missouri citizens and officially sanctioned militia action against them that rose only slightly above mob rule.

Many Missouri citizens during this period were guilty of a radical and angry response to their fears. Basically the Missouri people feared that Latter-day Saints might outvote them at the ballot box; might squeeze them out by their closely knit economic practices; might overwhelm them in sheer numbers, especially dangerous because the Saints were also considered to be Northerners; and might dilute them by what Missourians perceived as anti-slavery sentiment among the Saints. Finally, the Missourians feared that the Saints were Indian sympathizers.

Many Missouri citizens practiced the types of intolerance commonly observed at that time in Missouri. We can understand the attitudes and fears of Missouri citizens, but even the rest of the United States, in looking at what occurred, condemned in great measure the rise of lawlessness, prejudice, intolerance, and radicalism in Missouri. There is a general recognition, moreover, that simple, unabashed prejudice against and hatred of Mormons prevailed. Once again there arose the ancient concept of might makes right.

Religious fanaticism has already been identified in a previous chapter as an obstacle to tolerance. Missourians were guilty of a fanatic response to their fears, which motivated them to take the law into their own hands to retain Missouri as a slave state, perpetuate racial prejudice, and act on economic fears. These feelings, fanned into flames, resulted in lawlessness, forceful ejection of all Latter-day Saints, and mob rule. Missouri citizens who became enraged and violent needed more patience, more reliance on fairness and due process, more use of persuasion and long-suffering, more understanding of both sides of the issues, as well as all of the attitudes, practices, and principles previously discussed in this book.

Missourians were not the only ones guilty of fanaticism. Let's now take a look at the Latter-day Saints' response to the Missouri mob rule.

We need to remember the activities of a small number of the Saints and draw lessons from their unique brand of fanaticism. Some Church members turned to secret acts of plunder and terror in defense of their families and friends. Their motives may have been sincere and good, but, as history warns us, it is possible to take virtue to excess, as did Sampson Avard and those who joined him and who were sworn to secrecy by their leader.

Writers attached the name Danites to this group of men who defended the Saints by lawlessness, secrecy, and violence. In the early history of the Latter-day Saints, the term Danites had a much different meaning than that associated with Avard. Its original meaning seems to have been tied to Old Testament prophecies in the book of Daniel, drawing its symbolism from prophecies about the Lord establishing the kingdom of God on the earth in the last days. Because of activities of the group that Avard led in Missouri, the term Danites has taken on a sinister, extremist connotation.

Certainly those associated with Avard carried their loyalty and defensive activities to an extreme in Missouri. Joseph Smith and other Church leaders denied association with Avard in his extremist actions, and no reliable evidence has corroborated attempts by some to implicate Joseph as a leader and advocate of reckless, extreme, and secret actions. The term Danites would hardly be remembered, and then only in a healthy way, had it not been for the activities of Avard and his followers in Missouri.

The point to be made here, however, is that enemies of the Saints in Missouri did not commit all of the acts of disloyalty, extremism, and foolishness. Unbalanced and fanatical Latter-day Saints in Missouri were also active.

And there are fanatical and unbalanced members in the Church today, advocating and doing extreme and strange things. In identifying fanaticism as one of the obstacles to religious tolerance, we have discussed that danger to the Church and society. The Church should constantly be on guard against extremism and fanaticism. Fanatics usually start with the best of motives but then move into dangerous pathways, often taking others with them. Many are sincere and teachable, while others are unteachable and dangerous.

The Missouri period taught us many lessons, and one is to guard against and control fanaticism and extremism.

Separating Church and Politics Without Abdicating Moral Duty

Citizens of Missouri were afraid that their desire to retain the kind of community they had been accustomed to enjoy-with slavery, an outlook on politics and a life-style unique to southern states, and customary commercial approaches-was in grave danger as the population of Latter-day Saints continued to rise steeply. European nations, France and Germany prominent among them, face this same problem today. Africans and Moslems seeking a better life continue to pour into these two and other countries, bringing their own religion and way of life.

Missourians were particularly afraid of the tendency of Latter-day Saints to vote as a block. This same problem still plagues the Latter-day Saints to some extent. In most places the Saints are just a fraction of the population and are not looked upon with fear, greed, or suspicion at election time. Occasionally, however, and constantly in Utah and a few surrounding states, there is a tendency to fear the Mormon vote or to blame the Saints for the political climate, the passage of specific legislation, and nearly every public and private problem.

Joseph Smith understood well the fears of citizens in those communities used for gathering by the Saints. He explained that block voting was not necessarily the result of Church policy but rather the natural phenomenon caused by voters who share common beliefs, allegiance to one candidate, and support for or against particular legislative issues.

What can be done about the problem? What does the Missouri scenario teach?

Scrupulous attention to separating the Church from both politics and activities influencing voting and the affairs of government is essential. The Church must also guard against the misuse of the Church organization by the ambitious. Abuse of the official Church machinery for private, especially political, purposes must be avoided. The Church must take care to distinguish between actions and rights exercised by members as citizens and actions dictated or even suggested by the Church as an institution.

Even with constant care on these issues, observers often accuse the Church of dictating policy in Utah. There is little foundation for such accusations, although Latter-day Saints assert the right to have influence on moral issues and, as citizens, on all issues. After all, in Utah they live in a state with over 70 percent of the population affiliated with the Church.

Some would strip the Church of all influence on moral questions and remove the right of the Church to let its position be known on questions involving the moral climate of the communities where the Saints reside. While the Church should carefully communicate its support of separation of church and state, it cannot allow itself to be stripped of its power and duty to be a righteous force in the community. This duty to influence moral questions should be exercised thoughtfully, wisely, and tolerantly, with restraint and full recognition of the rights of others. All sides of the issues should be considered carefully before taking a moral stand on an issue. But it would be folly and totally unfair to silence one segment of a community by restricting its members from speaking and making their preferences known on moral issues. In addition, all citizens have a right to become politically involved as candidates or otherwise.

The Church is still accused of block voting. Rumors and accusations that the Church violates the principles of separation of church and state abound, but the Church learned during Missouri, Illinois, and early Utah history to separate politics and government from the Church without abdication of moral duty and community involvement.

Economic Tolerance

In Missouri, the growth of the Church raised fears, some legitimate, that the Latter-day Saints would do business only with other Church members. The Church tended to be an economic block as well as a voting block. In its infancy the Church was growing and consolidating by gathering together in large groups that tended to be closely knit economically as well as spiritually. In Jackson, Clay, Ray, and Daviess counties, the Saints lived among other people. They had not had significant experience with how commercial matters should work to be fair to their neighbors. Their policies and practices caused genuine fears in their neighbors.

The problem was not limited to the Missouri period. My great-grandparents assisted in establishing Joseph City, Arizona. As a boy I spent summers with my grandparents working with them on the farm. Joseph City, unlike Winslow where I lived, was a Latter-day Saint community. At one time the United Order of Joseph City owned everything; the community operated as an economic and commercial unit. This practice harmed no one outside their community, because all who lived and worked there, except Indians who occasionally sought employment with the Saints, constituted the united order. After that experience of economic unity, however, dissolution of the united order followed, as well as gradual integration with those not of the faith who moved into the town.

The Church has now gained experience with living together economically and culturally in a diverse community. Today, even where Latter-day Saints form the majority of citizens of a community, a peaceful and harmonious commercial integration usually exists. The Missouri period taught Latter-day Saints lessons in economic tolerance, but Church members need to be on guard even today against economic boycotting of neighbors whose religion differs from theirs.

Tolerance Requires Patience with Legal and Political Processes

Without being critical of the noble and embattled predecessors of the Church in Missouri, who often found that even the judge was their enemy, patience and persistence in following legal and constitutional procedures, even when conflicts became severe, would have been commendable, and is a virtue and lesson we can glean from the Missouri era.

The prejudice, intolerance, and aggression against the Latter-day Saints in Missouri were almost more than anyone could expect them to bear, yet at times the Saints reacted instead of always finding ways of obtaining due process through legal procedures. As has been mentioned, it was primarily the more fanatic and extreme elements of the Church that reacted and counterattacked when more patience and tolerance may have helped.

We must always obey the law, except when emergencies require immediate self-defense (as they often did in Missouri). A good test of the fairness of an action is whether lawful and tolerant people, such as Doniphan in Missouri, would consider the Saints' response to be fair-minded and tolerant. The verdict was not always positive, even in Doniphan's eyes. Even though the Saints have been provoked many times since the Missouri days-almost beyond their ability to endure, especially during certain periods of Utah history-cooler heads have prevailed on them to be careful, perhaps because they remembered their experiences in Missouri.

Refraining from Boasting

Tolerant people do not boast that God is on their side or that he will destroy the nonbelievers. All ought to leave up to God what he will or will not do in our affairs, at least in rhetoric and boasting. The caravan of the Church will move on, but it will be more tolerant and gentle if we do not boast of revelations, spiritual gifts, or revenge.

In Missouri, some of the Saints boasted that God would give them the properties of Jackson County no matter what those residents did. God's ways and thoughts are always higher than ours. In one way or another, he will honor those who honor him.
