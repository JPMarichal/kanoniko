John K. Carmack, Tolerance: Principles, Practices, Obstacles, Limits ( 1993)

Chapter Two

Tolerance in Action

Justice is truth in action.

-Benjamin Disraeli

A s with many important principles and doctrines, we learn what tolerance means best through personal experience rather than logic. The examples of fair and tolerant people are our best teachers. Nevertheless prophets, dictionaries, and recounted experiences can help us define and describe this elusive but dynamic concept.

Understanding tolerance requires us to observe and identify the attitudes and practices of tolerant people and uncover the principles that form its foundation. We also need to ask about its limits. This pick-and-shovel work will be done after this overview chapter. But we must start digging someplace, so let's do it.

What Is Tolerance?

If we take only a superficial look, tolerance might appear to be a tepid principle, hardly a robust Christian doctrine. Indeed, one definition of the word is that it means merely to endure or adapt to beliefs and practices differing from or conflicting with our own. Using that definition we are tolerant when we passively endure the ideas of others with whom we disagree. No strong conviction is required.

As used in this book, tolerance means more than that. Tolerance emerges as active, vital, and alive.

President Spencer W. Kimball said that tolerance is "the most lovable quality any human being can possess.Â� It is the vision that enables one to see things from another's viewpoint. It is the generosity that concedes to others the right to their own opinions and peculiarities. It is the bigness that enables us to let people be happy in their own way instead of our way."R1 [^1]

Note the strong words President Kimball used to describe tolerance-"lovable quality," "vision," "generosity," and "bigness."

Looked at in this light, tolerance is clearly something we all need in our lives. Friendships and relationships that we desperately need result from its practice. Tolerance is the right way to posture ourselves and The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints in a richly diverse world.

Tolerance a Doctrine of Christ

Christ, whose character exemplified tolerance and whose actions taught us how to apply it in our lives, taught his disciples early in his ministry to approach seemingly conflicting and threatening activities with confidence and tolerance. For instance, John once came to Jesus in alarm, explaining that they had found a person casting out devils in the name of Christ. Expecting Christ's approval of their action in the matter, John said, "We forbad him, because he followeth not with us." Jesus gently replied, "Forbid him not: for he that is not against us is for us." (Luke 9:49-50.)

Jesus' reaction may have surprised the Apostles, but it immediately established tolerance as a doctrine Jesus expected his disciples to adopt. Thus, we learn that Christians are to respect the actions and beliefs of those not belonging to their society. Others also perform good deeds and act out of sincere motivations. This was true in the days of the early Church and is true today.

Intolerance Teaches About Tolerance

Contrasting intolerance with tolerance clarifies the meaning of both principles. The practice of belittling the beliefs of others is an act of intolerance we frequently see. We may disagree with others about principles or doctrines, but it is unfair and in poor taste to belittle or scorn others for what they believe. Tolerance demands more of us. At one time or another most of us have been intolerant of others in that way. This point needs to be clear-belittling others is wrong and we should fight such tendencies.

Elder John Taylor shared his healthy attitude about others' beliefs in these words:

If any man under the heavens can show me one principle of error that I have entertained, I will lay it aside forthwith, and be thankful for the information. On the other hand if any man has got any principle of truth, whether moral, religious, philosophical, or of any other kind, that is calculated to benefit mankind, I promise him I will embrace it, but I will not partake of his errors along with it.R2 [^2]

That kind of tolerance is virile and confident: it builds others. Jesus and his disciples had enough confidence in the doctrines of Christ that they did not exhibit the weakness and timidity inherent in intolerance. Neither should we.

By the doctrinal principle of moral agency and the widely accepted human right of religious freedom, we are all entitled to our own beliefs, ideas, and behavior. Our priorities grow out of those. Latter-day Saints have strong beliefs about right and wrong, truth and error, good and evil.

But our beliefs do not require or permit Latter-day Saints to be intolerant of others whose beliefs differ from our own. Can we not hold fast to everything we believe, yet have sympathy and understanding for those whose beliefs differ from ours? We do not need to endorse or adopt anything we do not believe. To be open to new ideas increases our understanding and adds to our reservoirs of truth. This open approach requires confidence but is a powerful and enlarging doctrine. Let's not get caught up in pinning labels on others, no matter how many times we are victims of such intolerance. We call ourselves Christians, so let's be Christians.

Stated positively, we learn from examples of intolerance that tolerance includes respecting the religious views and doctrines of others. Showing such respect is one of the highest forms of tolerance.

Tolerance an Active, Not Passive or Neutral, Principle

Now we identify a major point in this chapter. Tolerance is not a morally neutral principle or doctrine. Many people are simply neutral in beliefs, having little conviction about right and wrong, fairness and unfairness, good and evil.

This book is meaningless to such persons. What is written here is primarily for Latter-day Saints who believe and actively practice their religion and for our friends of other faiths who care enough to find out what we believe about tolerance and deepen their own feelings for tolerance. Latter-day Saints do not live in a vacuum. They live in a world with many different ideas and beliefs and among people with widely variant life-styles. People of other faiths and people without any profession of faith also have beliefs and standards they live by. Often these standards differ from those of their neighbors.

Since people live together in communities with so many varying beliefs and strongly held opinions, tolerance is as essential to the proper functioning of these communities as lubricating oil is to the internal combustion engine. The powerful concept spoken of here is more than a mere shrug of the shoulders when faced with the clash of ideas.

President Kimball increased our understanding of tolerance as an active and compelling doctrine when he advocated the cause of American Indians, pleading with us to become more involved in lifting and assisting them. You can almost hear the urgency in his voice as he said:

I ask not for your tolerance [tolerance in the neutral, amoral sense described above]-your cold, calculating tolerance; your haughty, contemptible tolerance; your scornful, arrogant tolerance; your pitying, coin-tossing tolerance. I ask you to give them what they want and need and deserve: opportunity and your fraternal brotherliness and your understanding; your warm and glowing fellowship; your unstinted and beautiful love; your enthusiastic and affectionate brotherhood.R3 [^3]

This is vibrant, living doctrine. President Kimball called Church members to action.

This is not to suggest that tolerance always or even usually requires intervention in overcoming prejudice and human suffering, although sometimes it does. Nor does all of this mean that we need to right every wrong, get involved in every cause, and solve every problem like Don Quixote. No one has the strength to take up every cause presented in the course of life, but we do need an active kind of tolerance, and a mind-set of being willing to roll up our sleeves and get involved when it is right and the Spirit dictates.

Too often our relationships with members of other churches are a bit cold and standoffish. Sometimes we design a carefully constructed response that, when we are presented with opportunities for association and service, allows us to be correct in our response without becoming enmeshed and involved.

The general rule the doctrine of tolerance requires is that we must care about people and we should act on righteous impulses. The Apostle James asked what profit there was in lofty statements to the hungry, such as, "Be ye warmed and filled; notwithstanding ye give them not those things which are needful to the body" (James 2:16).

When word came to President Brigham Young in the fall of 1856 that there were companies of Saints headed west for Salt Lake who were undoubtedly in need of assistance, he announced the "text" for those who would be addressing Church members assembled for conference: "The text will be-to get them here!"R4 [^4]

In this dispensation the Lord explained that we should not have to be "compelled in all things." People are "agents unto themselves," meaning that they have freedom and power to make choices so that they can actively do what is right and needed in situations requiring their service. "The power is in them," the Lord explained. (See D&C 58:26-28.)

Wisdom Tempers Active Tolerance

In applying the general rule requiring action in helping others, we should remember that the book of Ecclesiastes emphasizes there is a time and a season for all things. In exerting ourselves in the cause of fairness and tolerance, we must develop and carry out thoughtful procedures designed to help and not harm.

Sometimes the Spirit will whisper to us that we should withhold help and not interfere in a scene of tragedy or injustice. The Spirit whispered to Alma that he and Amulek should not intervene as they witnessed the wicked people of Ammonihah throwing the believers into the fire. Alma explained that their forbearance would allow events to continue unabated, standing as a witness against the offending parties. (See Alma 14:11.)

As was the case with Alma and Amulek, however, our hearts must always reach out in love, understanding, empathy, and tolerance; there should be a desire to assist fellow human beings in need, especially those suffering the results of prejudice and discrimination. The Spirit will often whisper to us that we should intervene when that is the right course of action to follow.

Tolerance an Influence Rather Than a Central Goal

For a few people, achieving fairness and tolerance for a particular group of humans is their major goal in life-almost a calling. To most of us, however, tolerance can simply be an attitude and practice that influences how we react to situations in the normal course of pursuing other worthwhile activities, goals, and priorities. A personal sketch will illustrate this point.

Upon graduation from UCLA law school, I accepted an offer to serve the California legislature as a legislative assistant for a season. My wife, Shirley, and I discovered that housing was tight in Sacramento, so we drove to Sacramento ahead of our appointed starting date, nailing down a small duplex to serve as our home while we lived there. As we moved into our apartment we noticed one vacancy in the small compound where we lived.

Jim (not his actual name) was one of the fifteen legislative assistants chosen for the class of 1960-61. He became my closest friend among the fifteen because we officed together in the state capitol where we spent many hours discussing family, politics, our work, and religion. Jim was black, devoted to his wife and children, religious, and sensible in his views on politics and life.

Jim and his wife had trouble finding a place to live. We suggested they apply for the vacant apartment in our complex. When they applied they were told the apartment was rented. It wasn't. In answer to our inquiry the manager asked, "Did you know they are black?" We said of course we did and explained that they were clean, family oriented, and committed to their religion. Their habits were conservative, and we were positive they would cause no problems. Another tenant was found soon afterwards.

Eventually Jim and his family found a place to live in a predominantly black neighborhood. He chuckled with understanding as he recounted his conversations with the manager and thanked us for our interest. It hurt, but he had experienced the same treatment many times. We also felt hurt. Our sense of fairness was outraged, but in the moral climate of the times the landowner was following a common policy.

Although I believed in the blacks' struggle for decent housing and fairness, my own priorities did not lead to spending spare moments with the NAACP, CORE, or other civil rights organizations of the day. I devoted much of my spare time in Church and other kinds of community service. Jim understood that and respected it, but advocating fairness and tolerance for blacks was central to his life activities. In this he was not wrong, but neither was I in my direction. We both needed tolerance in the activities we pursued.

Our faith in the living prophets and our conviction concerning the reality of inspiration and revelation from above are the deepest feelings my wife and I have. This faith helped us establish the priority of seeking the kingdom of God first. Our priorities have led us to make a full commitment to the goals and purposes of the Church. Most discretionary time has been spent furthering the work of the Church. We never felt that callings in the Church interfered with our desire to treat all people justly and fairly. We have lived among Jews, blacks, Asians, and other races. Our children's friends have been of all races. We feel neither superior nor inferior to those of other races, cultures, and nationalities. Power, force, and wealth do not make one race superior to another.

Races, cultures, and nationalities differ, but God is the Father of all. I know of no suggestion in the scriptures that races are to be obliterated, boundaries between nations erased, or cultural differences eliminated. The Lord does, however, insist on tolerance and respect for all. The gospel of Jesus Christ should teach us to love and respect all races and nationalities.

Although we all need tolerance in our lives, no matter what role we carve for ourselves, not everyone must make it the primary goal pursued. Tolerance should be included as an underlying attitude and practice in every role.

For example, advocacy of fairness for and inclusion of the Gentiles was a major part of Paul's ministry. Peter did not show quite the same zeal Paul did for bringing Gentiles into the Christian family. It required a special revelation to get him going in that direction. The Church needed Peter's inspiring leadership and his own set of priorities in Church service. It also needed Paul's versatility and central commitment to the Gentiles. Paul needed no special revelation to send him on missions to the Gentiles. Nothing could stop him.

Ironically, however, it is Peter's phrase that has rung the bell of tolerance through the centuries that followed his taking the gospel to the Gentile Cornelius. Peter's experience taught us that a tolerant God harbors no prejudice for or against any nation or race; he "is no respecter of persons." He accepts all who love him and live righteous lives. (See Acts 10:34-35.)

Notas:
[^1] Tolerant people are versatile. Although all ten attitudes and practices are important and interrelated, this one deserves its placement at the top of the list. Tolerant people are versatile. They move easily from one situation to another, adapting quickly to the needs of others. They are able to converse effectively with people about a variety of subjects.They are flexible and caring. They make sincere efforts to meet the needs of other people. If they do not know those needs, they find them out immediately and make adjustments. Versatility. How versatile am I? Without giving up a single important thing I believe, how much effort am I willing to expend in order to adapt to and meet the needs of others? Can I get beyond my own needs to see others' viewpoints?
[^2] Tolerant people are patient. Patience is a much-neglected Christian virtue, though it is mentioned often in the New Testament (see Luke 21:19; Rom. 5:3; James 1:3; 2 Tim. 2:24). It is also listed as an attitude and practice recommended for those of the ministry in this dispensation (see D&C 4:6). Tolerant people are gentle and patient with others, willing to take time with them and to enjoy experiences they enjoy. Tolerant people give explanations and facts to those who need and want them, allow choices to others, and are careful not to give offense to those who are shy, wary, or uncomfortable in situations involving human interaction. Patience. How patient am I when things do not go my way? Am I composed under the stress of driving in heavy traffic, working through bureaucratic red tape at the airport, and interfacing with my children?
[^3] Tolerant people enlarge their circle of friends beyond their family and close-knit group. Having close family ties is one of the great blessings of life. A circle of valued friends with whom we enjoy social, educational, spiritual, and cultural events is also a source of enjoyment and stability. Tolerant people, however, do not limit themselves in their relationships to a tight circle of family and friends. Because of their attitude of tolerance and openness, they find their circle constantly widening. They are interested in the people at work, those with whom they do business, and neighbors who live near them. As they participate in community projects, recreational pursuits, and cultural events, and as they continue their education throughout their lives, they also expand that circle of friends and acquaintances. Circle of Friends. Do I search out new faces in the Church congregation, at school, or in other organizations and make them my friends? Are my prayers and goodwill limited only to my family and close-knit group?
[^4] Tolerant people are fascinated with other people and societies. While related to the last attitude and practice discussed, this one is on a broader scale. Tolerant people tend to be interested in people and society, learning about the way they live, think, and feel. From their interest and newly acquired knowledge they develop empathy and friendships. Intolerant people, on the other hand, tend to be closed, narrow, and satisfied with the status quo. They are not interested in gaining new experiences or finding out why others act and feel differently about things. They tend to approach new challenges with the attitude, "Well, that is not the way we do things around here." Their interest in the world doesn't stray from Main Street in their own town. Interest in Other People and Societies. Am I interested in learning about people who differ from my culture? Do I take a little extra time to see and experience new places when travelling?
