Sidney B. Sperry, Voice of Israel's Prophets ( 1953)

Chapter Fourteen

EZEKIEL, THE PROPHET OF JUDAH'S CAPTIVITY

The Prophet's Name and the Place of His Book in the Canon.—;The name of Ezekiel means "God is strong" or "God strengthens." We know from certain historical sources that the Book of Ezekiel was part of the canon of scripture, 200 B. C. or before. [^1] However, in the centuries following, its authority was questioned at various times by Jewish teachers. One of their principal objections to the book was that it contradicted the books of Moses. Another objection was that the visions in Chapter 1 and chapters 40 to 48 were too obscure. For a time Jews under 30 years of age were forbidden to read these obscure portions. In fact, the Book of Ezekiel came dangerously near being excluded from public reading altogether. But it was finally saved for the Jewish church by Hananiah, the grandson of Garon, who was a contemporary of Christ, and of the school of Hillel. All objections were overcome, and the position of the Book of Ezekiel was firmly established in the Jewish canon of sacred scripture.

Some Facts About Ezekiel.—;It should be said at the outset that, quite contrary to the position of much recent criticism, I hold to the essential authenticity of the Book of Ezekiel and also to the view that the prophet was no imaginary character. There can be no question but that the book has suffered considerably in the transmission of its text; nevertheless, I can not bring myself to the extreme critical views that now seem to be on the verge of dominating Old Testament scholarship in respect to Ezekiel. As Latter-day Saints we graciously give credit to scholars for all of the solid work that they have done in times past toward illuminating this and other books of the Old Testament, but we refuse to share the spiritual apostasy of those who reject it along with the prophet who wrote it.

In the opening chapter of the Book of Ezekiel we are told that the prophet was the son of Buzi. He is also spoken of as "the priest." This is probably a general title and has no special reference to Ezekiel's office in the priesthood in the sense that Latter-day Saints usually think of it. He may indeed once have held the office of a priest, but it is more than likely that he was given the Melchizedek Priesthood, particularly in view of the events recorded in chapters 2 and 3. It will be remembered that the Prophet Joseph Smith once said, "All the prophets had the Melchizedek Priesthood and were ordained by God Himself." [^2] Unfortunately, nothing is known about Ezekiel's father, Buzi, or his immediate family. The family must have been considered prominent and influential, for, according to the account in 2 Kings 24:14-16, mostly the "chief men of the land" were taken captive to Babylon by Nebuchadrezzar when Jehoiachin was deposed as king of Judah. Most scholars assume that this event took place in 597 B. C., but the fact that Zedekiah succeeded Jehoiachin leads us to assign it a little earlier, to 601 B. C., following the lead of certain chronological data in the Book of Mormon. Thus Ezekiel was "among the captives by the river of Chebar" when the Lord opened to him the visions of heaven. The opening lines of Ezekiel are puzzling to us:

Now it came to pass in the thirtieth year, in the fourth month, in the fifth day of the month, as I was among the captives by the river Chebar, . . .

We ask the question, "Thirtieth year of what?" Is it the thirtieth year of some Jewish king's reign? The answer is "no," because Judah had had several kings in the thirty years past. Is it in the thirtieth year of the captivity of that particular group of Jews with whom Ezekiel found himself? Again the answer is "no," for in verse 2 the prophet makes plain that the thirtieth year fell in the fifth year of Jehoiachin's captivity, which according to our calculations would be in or near the year 596 B. C. Perhaps the "thirtieth" year has reference to the age of Ezekiel, in which event he was born about 626 B. C. If this assumption is not correct, we can at present find no satisfactory explanation of the number.

The prophet grew up in his native, Palestine, probably at Jerusalem in the environment of the Temple, during Jeremiah's ministry. He was doubtless in his very young manhood when carried captive from Judah to Babylon with Jehoiachin (601 B. C., based on Book of Mormon), about eight years after the Prophet Daniel's deportation. The Jewish historian Josephus says that he was a youth at the time he was taken. [^3] In Babylonia Ezekiel seems to have settled with other exiles in a community known as Tel-abib ("hill of an ear of grain") by "the great canal," the river Chebar. (Ezek. 1:1; 3:15) This community was in all probability a concentration-camp, with the deported Jews furnishing the forced labor in the Babylonian irrigation system. Ezekiel was married and had a home where in later years the elders of the land came to consult him. (3:24; 8:1; 24:18) Near the beginning of the siege of Jerusalem in 593 B. C., the prophet's wife died. It appears from certain of Ezekiel's prophecies that his ministry lasted until at least 574 B. C., but beyond that year we are unable to penetrate. Ezekiel's influence upon his people was remarkable in many respects, and his teachings deeply affected their thinking.

The Content and Literary Style of Ezekiel.—;The Book of Ezekiel may be easily studied according to the following scheme of analysis:

Introduction: Chapters 1:1-3:21. Ezekiel's vision and call to the ministry.

A. The prophet's vision of God's glory. (1:1-28)

B. His call and mission. (2:1-3:15)

C. His responsibility as a watchman unto the house of Israel. (3:16-21)

I. Chapters 3:22-24:27. Prophecies against Judah and Jerusalem.

A. Chapter 3:22-27. Ezekiel commanded to be temporarily silent.

B. Chapters 4-7. Prediction of utter destruction of Judah and Jerusalem.

(1) Through symbolical actions. (4:1-5:4)

(2) Through prophecies. (5:5-7:27)

C. Chapters 8-11. Vision of the wickedness and punishment of Jerusalem.

(1) Israel's idolatry. (8:1-18)

(2) Jerusalem's punishment. (9:1-11:25)

D. Chapters 12-19. The nation doomed because of universal corruption.

(1) Two symbolical acts portray Jerusalem's impending downfall. (12:1-28)

(2) False prophets and doom of the corrupt city. (13:1-14:23)

(3) Allegories of useless vine and foundling child portray Jerusalem's ripeness for judgment. (15:1-16:63)

(4) Meaning of an allegory—;Zedekiah's disloyalty, one of his posterity transplanted. (17:1-24)

(5) Moral freedom and responsibility of the individual. (18:1-32)

(6) Lamentation over princes of Israel—;Allegory of the Lioness and her whelps. (19:1-14)

E. Chapters 20-24. Threatening prophecies before destruction of Judah and Jerusalem.

(1) Judah's persistent rebellion against the Lord. (20:1-32)

(2) Punishment and eventual gathering of Israel. (20:33-44)

(3) Symbolic representation of Judah's overthrow. (20:45-49; Hebr. 21:1-5)

(4) Imminent destruction of land of Israel. (21:1-32)

(5) Utter depravity of Jerusalem and the inevitable retribution. (22:1-31)

(6) Judah's and Israel's history allegorically portrayed. (23:1-49)

(7) The investiture and capture of Jerusalem allegorically portrayed. (24:1-14)

(8) Ezekiel's refusal to mourn his wife's death a sign that the Lord will permit His sanctuary to be profaned and Jerusalem to be destroyed. (24:15-27)

II. Chapters 25-32. Prophecies against foreign nations.

A. Ammon. (25:1-7)

B. Moab. (25:8-11)

C. Edom. (25:12-14)

D. Philistia. (25:15-17)

E. Tyre. (26:1-28:19)

F. Sidon (28:20-24), the restoration of Israel. (28:25, 26)

G. Egypt. (29:1-32:32)

III. Chapters 33-48. Prophecies concerning the restoration of Israel.

A. Chapters 33-39. Restoration of Israel's dispersed to their promised lands.

(1) The responsibility of a prophet. (33:1-33)

a. The prophet a watchman to Israel. (33:1-9)

b. Men responsible and judged by their acts. (33:10-20)

c. The abominations of Israel's exiles. (33:21-33)

(2) The work of Israel's shepherds. (34:1-31)

a. False shepherds to be restrained and held responsible for their deeds. (34:1-10)

b. The Lord as the good shepherd to seek out His sheep. (34:11-22)

c. Descendant of David by same name to be prince among the Lord's people. (34:23-31)

(3) The contrast between Edom's destiny and that of Israel. (35:1-36:38)

(4) Vision of Israel's restoration. (37:1-28)

a. The valley of dry bones. (37:1-14)

b. The stick of Judah and the stick of Ephraim. (37:15-23)

c. David's descendant to be prince over Israel. (37:24-28)

(5) The Lord's triumph over Gog, Israel's enemy. (38:1-39:29)

a. Gog and his hosts attack Israel. (38:1-16)

b. Israel's enemies annihilated. (38:17-39:20)

c. The Lord's glory among the nations and Israel's restoration. (39:21-29)

B. Chapters 40-48. The Lord's temple in Latter-day Jerusalem and the distribution of the land by lot for an inheritance among the tribes of Israel.

(1) The architecture and dedication of the temple. (40:1-43:27)

a. Temple's specifications shown Ezekiel. (40:1-42:20)

b. The Lord's glory fills the Temple. (43:1-12)

c. The altar and measures prescribed for the Temple's dedication. (43:13-27)

(2) Regulations governing the administration of Temple precincts, personnel, and sacred days. (44:1-46:24)

a. Eastern gate to be permanently closed. (44:1-3)

b. Aliens among Israelites to be excluded from Temple. (44:4-9)

c. Limited functions of Levites. (44:10-14)

d. The conduct, duties, and rewards of priests. (44:15-31)

e. Allotment of territory to priests, Levites, and Prince. (45:1-8)

f. Israel's princes to execute justice and righteousness. (45:9-12)

g. Sacrifices and holy days. (45:13-46:24)

(3) Disposition of the tribes of Israel in Palestine. (47:1-48:35)

a. The life-giving river issuing from the Temple. (47:1-12)

b. The division and boundaries of Israel's inheritance. (47:13-23)

c. The portion of the seven northern tribes. (48:1-7)

d. The Lord's portion. (48:8-22)

e. The portion of the five southern tribes. (48:23-29)

f. The Holy City's twelve gates. (48:30-35)

The Book of Ezekiel is of great importance to Latter-day Saints, especially chapters 33-48, which deal with phases of the great restoration of Israel in the latter days. For that reason I have made the outline of the book quite detailed, in order that the reader may easily follow what the prophet was trying to say.

The style of Ezekiel's writings reveals an almost paradoxical variety of moods. In this respect he reminds us of Dante.

The contrast between Ezekiel's animated, lively, vivid poetry and the dullest of his prose is even more noticeable than in the writings of Jeremiah. Most of his poems are allegorical. Notice those in chapters 15, 16, 17, 19:2-9 as examples. These allegories are not only difficult for us in this day so far removed from Ezekiel's time and from the temperament and psychology of the Hebrews; they were difficult for his own people. "Then said I, Ah Lord God! they say of me, Doth he not speak parables?" (20:49)

This fact seems to have nettled the prophet no little with the result that he took pains to give explanations of his parables.

The literary critic would probably classify Ezekiel's allegories as descriptive and visionary rather than dramatic and realistic. The finest of the prophet's poetry is to be found in the allegories in chapters 19:2-6, 8f; 27:3-9, 25-36.

Ezekiel's prose ranges in quality from clear, understandable, and fair at its best to monotonous and tedious at its worst. Most writers would agree that Ezekiel had a keen mind and a great soul, but definitely displayed a lack of rhetorical skill. He was no orator.

Ezekiel's Vision and Call to the Ministry.—;The prophet's opening vision and call to the ministry are described in chapters 1:1-3:21. He states that his first vision took place "in the fifth day of the month, which was the fifth year of king Jehoiachin's captivity." This year, as we have seen, was in or very near 596 B. C. It was about four or five years after father Lehi and his company had left Jerusalem and nearly six years before the fall of that city. (Book of Mormon chronology) Ezekiel's description of his opening vision (1:1-28) is to us almost fantastic, and the meaning of the things which he saw is about completely lost on us at this day. Out of a great cloud, with the flashing of fire round about, the prophet sees the likeness of four living creatures. Each of these was in general form human, but each had four faces and four wings. Their feet were straight, and the soles of their feet were like the soles of a calf's foot; and they sparkled like burnished brass. One of their faces was that of a man, the one on the right side was that of a lion, that on the left side was of an ox, and the fourth face was that of an eagle. The wings of each of these creatures, when outspread, just touched those of its companion on either side of it. Besides each creature was a double wheel sparkling like beryl (or topaz), made of two wheels set at right angles about a common diameter; one was in the north-south plane, the other was in the west-east plane. Apparently the creatures and wheels did not turn—at is, if the present text may be trusted; their motion was always due north, south, east or west, in a manner quite incomprehensible to us. The felloes of the wheels were full of eyes, so that no blind path was followed. Moreover, the Spirit insured harmony of motion. Ezekiel heard the beating of the creatures' wings as they went, and the noise was as of great waters, like the voice of the Almighty. (1:24; cf. D. & C. 110:3)

Above the creatures and wheels was a throne in the appearance of a sapphire. Upon the throne was seated a personage whose glory and brightness seemed almost impossible for the prophet to describe. This being was God, who was now to call Ezekiel to the ministry.

The celestial personage upon the throne, whose appearance was "of the glory of the Lord," commanded Ezekiel to stand on his feet and hear a message. The prophet was filled with the spirit, and the glorious being gave him a charge to go among his own people with a message of warning. The children of Israel were described as rebellious, impudent (lit. "stiff of face"), and obdurate in heart. Ezekiel was told not to be afraid of them and to deliver the words as he was commanded. They were to know that a prophet had been among them. A heavenly message was conveyed symbolically to the prophet by his eating a scroll written on both sides with lamentations and mourning and woe. (2:8-3:3) Ezekiel was sent forth among the exiles and came to Tel-abib among his own people for seven days. He was then told by the Lord that he had been made a "watchman unto the house of Israel." The responsibility of the prophet—, we take it, every prophet at any time—;was laid down as follows: Whenever the wicked are to be warned and the prophet does not assume this responsibility, they die in their sins, but their blood will be required at his hand. If the wicked are duly warned, however, and they die in their sins, no responsibility attaches to him. Again, if a righteous man turns to wickedness and dies without being warned, his righteousness "shall not be remembered," but his blood will be required at the prophet's hand. If, however, he is warned and turns again to righteousness "he shall surely live," and the prophet's soul is "delivered." (Ezek. 3:17-21)

Latter-day Saints have today a special responsibility in proclaiming the Gospel and warning our generation. We may well take to heart the charge given by the Lord to Ezekiel.

Ezekiel Commanded to Remain Silent.—;After again beholding the glory of the Lord, Ezekiel was ordered to remain silent for a time and shut himself up in his own house. "For," said the Lord, "they [the people] are a rebellious house." (3:26)
