The main Godbeite contributions to modern Utah and modern Mormonism, then, seem to be timing and effect. By drawing on the established resources of members within the LDS community, the New Movement initiated changes in Utah earlier and with a greater finality than outsiders might have done; Godbeitism did not have to wait for the slow growth of non-Mormon strength to start the Tribune and the Liberal Institute or begin the mining of precious ores. Likewise, the Stenhousesâ books did not so much create the Mormonsâ damning nineteenth-century image as reinforce it. As with so much of the Godbeite endeavor, the result was one of degree.

If Godbeitismâs role as an agent of change was limited, it nevertheless was important. First, the movement was significant in terms of biography. The men and women of the New Movement were more than interesting; they were able individuals who articulately grappled with fundamental issues of religious dissent. Studying their lives provides insight into the human condition. Why does an individual dissent from his or her religion? And what are the benefits and perils of doing so?

Biographical study provides only partial answers. Religious commitment ultimately is a personal matter, often unexpressed or unconscious. Why some individuals abandon their faith therefore remains a mystery. Yet each of the New Movement reformers left hints that help to explain his or her actions. As is evident in this study, sometimes a preliminary explanation can be found in emotional instability, an unrealized ambition, an unbridled restlessness, or the incompatibility of opposing personalities. Or alienation might have its origin in a semiprivate disease like alcoholism. Clearly, some of these factors influenced the Godbeite reformers.

Men and women, however, are more than the sum of their emotional or physical struggles. Today, the Godbeites are interesting, not so much because of their human frailties but because of their ideasâideas that eventually persuaded them to leave Mormonism. Unlike other intellectually inclined converts who remained Latter-day Saints, the Godbeites concluded that they could not reconcile the special claims of nineteenth-century Mormonism with the larger cultural world they encountered. In a contest between their religion and their intellectual understanding, their religion lost.

This intellectual struggle suggests another bequest of Godbeitism. By studying Godbeite ideas, we learn about nineteenth-century Mormonism. For instance, one of the concepts that most troubled Godbeites was the Mormon idea of prophetic leadership and related doctrines. From the beginning of Joseph Smithâs ministry, Smith insisted that he, as a modern-day Enoch, should direct the church and its members in practical as well as spiritual matters. This extensive claim of executive power was at the heart of several apostasies that disrupted Mormonism during Smithâs life, including the one that led to his death. Clearly, some Mormon converts were unwilling to grant their church and its leader such a large jurisdiction over their lives.

For many church members this question was settled before Brigham Young led his followers west. Those Mormons who were unwilling to accept Joseph Smithâs model of theocratic leadership remained behind along the LDS trail in New York, Ohio, Missouri, and Illinois. However, because most Godbeites arrived in Utah in the 1850s and 1860s, this struggle of faith came later, long after the earlier apostasies.

Two factors that predisposed them to dissent provide an important insight into first-generation Mormonism. First, most of the Godbeites were British-born or had strong ties with the cultural environment of Great Britain. They were therefore products of one strain of early Mormonism, which, in this study, I have called âBritish Mormonism.â This was a fervent and relatively simple proselyting faith that held within it the seeds of social, economic, and especially religious revolution. Unlike Brigham Youngâs Zion in Utah, the Godbeitesâ homeland had conditioned them to many viewpoints, an atmosphere of dissent, and nineteenth-century liberalism. Second, many of the âNew Moversâ were merchants, particularly such leaders as William Godbe and Henry Lawrence. The huge merchant caravans that Tullidge spoke of so fulsomely were in fact symbols of a quest for personal wealth, which ran counter to Youngâs teachings of a community composed of roughly equal citizens. Merchants brought individualism, social and economic diversity, and a power base independent of the churchâs headquartersâanathema to Zionâs first purposes and spirit.

Reflecting the Godbeitesâ intellectual, religious, and cultural background, the New Movement brought into focus two very different and warring versions of society. On the one hand there was Brigham Youngâs Zion, with its emphasis on unity, cooperation, obedience, homogeneity, separateness from the society at large, Christian tenets, and centralized management and control. On the other hand, Godbeites rejected Mormonismâs heavenly vision and instead called for integration with the prevailing currents of the time. The New Movementâs tone was secular, material, and individualistic. Similar conflicts had convulsed other ideal American communitiesâsuch as John Winthropâs Boston or William Pennâs Quaker commonwealthâas their societies evolved and matured. The New Movement also revealed something about the spirit of Zion. If events surrounding the Godbeite revolt are an indication, the Mormon Zion was no haven for pluralism nor was it a great defender of civil rights. While Godbe, Lawrence, and Stenhouse probably exaggerated the point, each of them complained of losing their fortunes because some Mormons refused to pay their debts after the Godbeites left Mormonism. Many of the dissenters also complained of being ostracized by neighbors and former friends, and they resented the fiery epithets pronounced upon them from the Mormon pulpit, many by second- and third-level Mormon leaders. The New Movement was also important because of the questions it raised. On one level, Godbeitism was an encounter between traditional faith and modernism, and many of the issues that the various New Movement periodicals raised had a timeless quality. How do church sacraments sanctify? Can only one church have special or unique authority? How may the claims of science and faith be reconciled? While believers answer these questions differently, the Godbeites had challenging arguments.

History seemed to bear out some of the Godbeite predictions. With the federal government continuing its exertions against the Mormon kingdom, church leaders during the 1890s finally abandoned publicly sanctioned plural marriage, their partisan Peopleâs Party, the large-scale gathering of the European Saints, and the church-based cooperative schemes that earlier Mormon leaders had so strongly defended. The social machinery of Zion had transformed and elevated the Mormon convert. Of all the ideas raised by the Godbeites, the question of church authority, or the balancing of the respective claims of institutional and personal authority, had the most familiar or classic overtones. The Mormons had tried to find a solution to this age-old dilemma. As early as 1830, founding prophet Joseph Smith established and defended hierarchal authority against the challenge of a supposed private revelation: âThou shalt not command him who is at thy head, and at the head of the church,â Smith told the erring elder.15 But other revelations received by Smith had also required the membersâ âcommon consentâ in church matters, and still others recognized an individualâs need for private communion and inspiration.16

These revelations worked well enough when leader and follower agreed. They even worked in the case of minor disagreement: an organization probably becomes the more healthy by having a variety of views and ideas from leaders and followers alike. But what of the Godbeite claim for the supremacy of personal conscience in religious matters? What might have happened had Mormon church authority accepted such a claim?

There is, of course, no way of knowing, although the Church of Zionâs struggle to maintain an organization based on severe individualism was instructive. Believing that the spirits would speak to others as they had to them, Godbe and Harrison assumed that the otherworldly voice, aided by reason, would be a reliable guide to govern their church. It is one of Godbeitismâs ironies that it was unable to establish a workable organization in the face of its criticism of the supposed assumptions of Young.

Even as the men and women of the New Movement criticized Mormons for not being sufficiently âadvanced,â the âNew Moversâ own spiritualism and freethinking were tied to the times, more than they ever realized. In determining the outcome of the New Movement, Young played a central role. The centrifugal tendencies of most young religions usually break them into competing schisms that eventually destroy the larger movement. Faced with the opposition of the New Movement, Young acted to keep his church intactâjust as he had earlier in Nauvoo and during the perilous crossing of Iowa two years later. During the Godbeite challenge, Young had held an open hearing, and when the Salt Lake Stake court finally took action against Harrison and Godbe, there seemed little alternative to excommunication. While the full program of Godbe and Harrison remained concealed, Young had defended his churchâs authority and discipline.

More than one hundred years after the Godbeite revolt, unresolved questions remain. Could a softer attitude toward merchants and intellectuals prior to 1869 have kept many of the dissenters in the church? By limiting what Zion permitted and admired, had the theocracy unintentionally persuaded these merchants and intellectuals to take a radical and ultimately nonconformist position? Had such measures as the commercial boycott pushed these difficult-to-manage Saints further than everyoneâincluding themselvesâwanted? Or were the Godbeites more a victim of their own hubris? The Mormon view at the time described the reformers as having an intellectual pride that made them spiritually unmanageable.

Whatever the spiritual or inner forces working on the Godbeites personally, their revolt brought consequences both for Mormonism and for themselves. Mormonism was diminished by losing the diversity and talent of the reformers and their descendants. On the other hand, there were moments when the dissenters themselves seemed to feel a loss. Casting off the ballast of their old religious faith, almost certainly they sailed into deeper waters than they first intended, and at the end of their journey, many of them looked longingly backward, unable to cast off Mormonismâs spell entirely. Most of them continued to accept some of their old LDS teachingsâespecially Mormonismâs positive view of humankind and its eternal perfectibility. Even more compelling to the Godbeites were the warmth and friendships of Brighamâs social Zion, which, for all their complaints about Youngâs regime, continued to have a claim on them. And that serious, earnest, idealistic behavior that was so much a part of many nineteenth-century Mormons remained a characteristic of the dissentersâ personal lives.

In the end, Mormonism passed through the commercial and railroad crisis of 1869â70 rather easily. In navigating this passage, the rank-and-file Mormon had a role. Those members who ignored Youngâs merchant boycott and who ventured into the mines were undoubtedly motivated by narrow, materialistic reasons. These members were secular Saints, living in the community but not partaking of its spirit. Still other Mormons, perhaps a majority, were unaffected by events. Either due to lack of interest or other pressing concerns, they did not get involved in community issues. A third group helped to determine the outcome of the Godbeite revolt. For many in this group, faith was enough: they did not need to know when President Youngâs voice was his own or when it spoke for Providence. They simply believed. Also in this group were people like the sturdy Bishop Edwin Woolley, who, while sensing the need for change within the community, nevertheless made obedience a virtue.

When the Godbeite revolt first broke out in October 1869, outsiders believed that they saw the issue clearly. Wasnât this a simple declaration against priestly, heavy-handed rule? Historical events are seldom so clear-cut, as this book suggests. An event like the Godbeite revolt remains compelling because of its complexity and because many of its opposing ideals and contrasting virtues remain dilemmas to the present time.

With the exception of two or three pockets of gentile mining activity in the territory, the Mormons remained in control. The Tribune, of course, chose to overlook this reason for the churchâs change in policy. Instead, the newspaper gleefully credited the Reformers with having âconvertedâ the Mormon leaders to mining.12

There was another possible indication of the Godbeitesâ influence. During the October 1870 general conference, Young stunned the assembled thousands by tendering his resignation as the churchâs business or temporal trustee-in-trust. It was a dramatic moment. Despite the Saintsâ usual compliance with Youngâs motions, the highly agitated congregation refused to follow his direction. When casting their negative vote, many raised not just the customary right hand in the air, but, spontaneously standing, they extended both arms in a sea of protest. It was a poignant demonstration of affection for the aging leader.13

Had the biting criticism of the Godbeites and of some Saints regarding Youngâs temporal-mindedness influenced his attempt to resign? Or could the move be explained, as the church leader himself claimed, by his desire to escape the minutiae of the churchâs secular business? Whatever his motives, and they were probably mixed, Young proposed the same motion at another conference two-and-a-half years later, and this time he had his way. In addition, during the same month, April 1873, he also resigned from the presidency of both ZCMI and the Deseret National Bank. His trustee-in-trust duties were assumed by President George A. Smith and a committee of twelve assistants, and his corporate positions went to leading local investors.14

With some members ignoring the Mormon trade embargo, seeking mineral wealth contrary to counsel, and disparaging leaders (as perhaps evidenced by Youngâs resignations), it was clear that a new spirit was making its way into Utah society. Were the Godbeites responsible? Certainly Godbe, Harrison, and their followers had predicted many of these events and worked to create them. Yet, however ambitious their after-the-fact claims, the conclusion seems inescapable that the âNew Moversâ did less to shape events than reflect long-term trends. The transcontinental railroad would have brought precious ore mining with or without Godbeite support. Likewise, the end of Utahâs relative isolation made inevitable the introduction of a broader-based and pluralistic society, ushering in an atmosphere of public debate and greater individual liberty. Ironically, the sweeping power of âAmericanizationâ that the Godbeites welcomed and proclaimed made possible the achievements of the New Movementâand, in the end, eclipsed the movement itself.

The main Godbeite contributions to modern Utah and modern Mormonism, then, seem to be timing and effect. By drawing on the established resources of members within the LDS community, the New Movement initiated changes in Utah earlier and with a greater finality than outsiders might have done; Godbeitism did not have to wait for the slow growth of non-Mormon strength to start the Tribune and the Liberal Institute or begin the mining of precious ores. Likewise, the Stenhousesâ books did not so much create the Mormonsâ damning nineteenth-century image as reinforce it. As with so much of the Godbeite endeavor, the result was one of degree.

If Godbeitismâs role as an agent of change was limited, it nevertheless was important. First, the movement was significant in terms of biography. The men and women of the New Movement were more than interesting; they were able individuals who articulately grappled with fundamental issues of religious dissent. Studying their lives provides insight into the human condition. Why does an individual dissent from his or her religion? And what are the benefits and perils of doing so?

Biographical study provides only partial answers. Religious commitment ultimately is a personal matter, often unexpressed or unconscious. Why some individuals abandon their faith therefore remains a mystery. Yet each of the New Movement reformers left hints that help to explain his or her actions. As is evident in this study, sometimes a preliminary explanation can be found in emotional instability, an unrealized ambition, an unbridled restlessness, or the incompatibility of opposing personalities. Or alienation might have its origin in a semiprivate disease like alcoholism. Clearly, some of these factors influenced the Godbeite reformers.

Men and women, however, are more than the sum of their emotional or physical struggles. Today, the Godbeites are interesting, not so much because of their human frailties but because of their ideasâideas that eventually persuaded them to leave Mormonism. Unlike other intellectually inclined converts who remained Latter-day Saints, the Godbeites concluded that they could not reconcile the special claims of nineteenth-century Mormonism with the larger cultural world they encountered. In a contest between their religion and their intellectual understanding, their religion lost.

This intellectual struggle suggests another bequest of Godbeitism. By studying Godbeite ideas, we learn about nineteenth-century Mormonism. For instance, one of the concepts that most troubled Godbeites was the Mormon idea of prophetic leadership and related doctrines. From the beginning of Joseph Smithâs ministry, Smith insisted that he, as a modern-day Enoch, should direct the church and its members in practical as well as spiritual matters. This extensive claim of executive power was at the heart of several apostasies that disrupted Mormonism during Smithâs life, including the one that led to his death. Clearly, some Mormon converts were unwilling to grant their church and its leader such a large jurisdiction over their lives.

For many church members this question was settled before Brigham Young led his followers west. Those Mormons who were unwilling to accept Joseph Smithâs model of theocratic leadership remained behind along the LDS trail in New York, Ohio, Missouri, and Illinois. However, because most Godbeites arrived in Utah in the 1850s and 1860s, this struggle of faith came later, long after the earlier apostasies.

Two factors that predisposed them to dissent provide an important insight into first-generation Mormonism. First, most of the Godbeites were British-born or had strong ties with the cultural environment of Great Britain. They were therefore products of one strain of early Mormonism, which, in this study, I have called âBritish Mormonism.â This was a fervent and relatively simple proselyting faith that held within it the seeds of social, economic, and especially religious revolution. Unlike Brigham Youngâs Zion in Utah, the Godbeitesâ homeland had conditioned them to many viewpoints, an atmosphere of dissent, and nineteenth-century liberalism. Second, many of the âNew Moversâ were merchants, particularly such leaders as William Godbe and Henry Lawrence. The huge merchant caravans that Tullidge spoke of so fulsomely were in fact symbols of a quest for personal wealth, which ran counter to Youngâs teachings of a community composed of roughly equal citizens. Merchants brought individualism, social and economic diversity, and a power base independent of the churchâs headquartersâanathema to Zionâs first purposes and spirit.

Reflecting the Godbeitesâ intellectual, religious, and cultural background, the New Movement brought into focus two very different and warring versions of society. On the one hand there was Brigham Youngâs Zion, with its emphasis on unity, cooperation, obedience, homogeneity, separateness from the society at large, Christian tenets, and centralized management and control. On the other hand, Godbeites rejected Mormonismâs heavenly vision and instead called for integration with the prevailing currents of the time. The New Movementâs tone was secular, material, and individualistic. Similar conflicts had convulsed other ideal American communitiesâsuch as John Winthropâs Boston or William Pennâs Quaker commonwealthâas their societies evolved and matured. Indeed, the competition between managed and market-driven communities is one of historyâs longest ongoing struggles.

The New Movement also revealed something about the spirit of Zion. If events surrounding the Godbeite revolt are an indication, the Mormon Zion was no haven for pluralism nor was it a great defender of civil rights. While Godbe, Lawrence, and Stenhouse probably exaggerated the point, each of them complained of losing their fortunes because some Mormons refused to pay their debts after the Godbeites left Mormonism. Many of the dissenters also complained of being ostracized by neighbors and former friends, and they resented the fiery epithets pronounced upon them from the Mormon pulpit, many by second- and third-level Mormon leaders. Moreover, the Stenhouses were victims of assault. President Young might preach the inviolability of personal rights, but Utah was only then emerging from a frontier theocracy that had disturbing rough edges.

On the other hand, the Mormons had never been the iron-fisted autocrats of popular nineteenth-century imagination, and during the Godbeite dissent, leaders proceeded with relative moderation. Youngâs equanimity before the nonconformist Peep Oâ Day and later the Utah Magazine, his wish to avoid the confrontation that the Godbeite leaders, especially Harrison, thrust upon him, his temporary grant of the Thirteenth Ward Assembly Rooms for the New Movementâs use, his continuing work with Henry Lawrence despite the latterâs continued intransigence, and his counsel to fellow church members not to abuse or infringe on the rights of the dissentersâthese acts were partly pragmatic responses by a pragmatic man. Young wanted to avoid unnecessary confrontation and division. But his acts also suggested a warmer and more tolerant portrait than is often accorded the church leader. Indeed, the Keep-A-Pitchinin was proof that the community could respond to social stress with deflecting good humor.

The New Movement was also important because of the questions it raised. On one level, Godbeitism was an encounter between traditional faith and modernism, and many of the issues that the various New Movement periodicals raised had a timeless quality. How do church sacraments sanctify? Can only one church have special or unique authority? How may the claims of science and faith be reconciled? While believers answer these questions differently, the Godbeites had challenging arguments.

History seemed to bear out some of the Godbeite predictions. With the federal government continuing its exertions against the Mormon kingdom, church leaders during the 1890s finally abandoned publicly sanctioned plural marriage, their partisan Peopleâs Party, the large-scale gathering of the European Saints, and the church-based cooperative schemes that earlier Mormon leaders had so strongly defended. In their place Utah accepted American institutions, and âMormon Countryâ became a subsection of the social, economic, and cultural national whole. The Mormonsâ bold experiment, their idiosyncratic âGreat Basin Kingdom,â was thus brought to a haltâbut not before it had largely performed the mission that Young had hoped for it. The social machinery of Zion had transformed and elevated the Mormon convert. In the future, individual members, individual families, and individual congregations continued to pursue the ideal of Enoch, but not a geographically based, church-state society.

Of all the ideas raised by the Godbeites, the question of church authority, or the balancing of the respective claims of institutional and personal authority, had the most familiar or classic overtones. The Mormons had tried to find a solution to this age-old dilemma. As early as 1830, founding prophet Joseph Smith established and defended hierarchal authority against the challenge of a supposed private revelation: âThou shalt not command him who is at thy head, and at the head of the church,â Smith told the erring elder.15 But other revelations received by Smith had also required the membersâ âcommon consentâ in church matters, and still others recognized an individualâs need for private communion and inspiration.16

These revelations worked well enough when leader and follower agreed. They even worked in the case of minor disagreement: an organization probably becomes the more healthy by having a variety of views and ideas from leaders and followers alike. But what of the Godbeite claim for the supremacy of personal conscience in religious matters? What might have happened had Mormon church authority accepted such a claim?

There is, of course, no way of knowing, although the Church of Zionâs struggle to maintain an organization based on severe individualism was instructive. Believing that the spirits would speak to others as they had to them, Godbe and Harrison assumed that the otherworldly voice, aided by reason, would be a reliable guide to govern their church. Instead, Youngâs predictions that spiritualism would be a ârope of sandâ proved true. It is one of Godbeitismâs ironies that it was unable to establish a workable organization in the face of its criticism of the supposed assumptions of Young.

Church governance was not the only matter that required the Godbeites to exercise their faith. Even as the men and women of the New Movement criticized Mormons for not being sufficiently âadvanced,â the âNew Moversâ own spiritualism and freethinking were tied to the times, more than they ever realized. This was evident in some of the unusual behavior that went on at the Liberal Institute. Magnetic healing, psychometry and physiognomy, trance speaking, sÃ©ances, cabinet mediumsâthese colorful activities were very much nineteenth-century products that required a suspension of reason and intellect. The same was true of the Godbeitesâ acceptance of the ideals of progress and science. Twentieth-century wars, national and racial strife, and even the increasing awareness of scienceâs own unpredictability made faith in progress and science (and the link between the two) far less sure.

In determining the outcome of the New Movement, Young played a central role. The centrifugal tendencies of most young religions usually break them into competing schisms that eventually destroy the larger movement. Faced with the opposition of the New Movement, Young acted to keep his church intactâjust as he had earlier in Nauvoo and during the perilous crossing of Iowa two years later. During the Godbeite challenge, Young had held an open hearing, and when the Salt Lake Stake court finally took action against Harrison and Godbe, there seemed little alternative to excommunication. While the full program of Godbe and Harrison remained concealed, Young had defended his churchâs authority and discipline.

More than one hundred years after the Godbeite revolt, unresolved questions remain. Could a softer attitude toward merchants and intellectuals prior to 1869 have kept many of the dissenters in the church? By limiting what Zion permitted and admired, had the theocracy unintentionally persuaded these merchants and intellectuals to take a radical and ultimately nonconformist position? Had such measures as the commercial boycott pushed these difficult-to-manage Saints further than everyoneâincluding themselvesâwanted? Or were the Godbeites more a victim of their own hubris? The Mormon view at the time described the reformers as having an intellectual pride that made them spiritually unmanageable.

Whatever the spiritual or inner forces working on the Godbeites personally, their revolt brought consequences both for Mormonism and for themselves. Mormonism was diminished by losing the diversity and talent of the reformers and their descendants. On the other hand, there were moments when the dissenters themselves seemed to feel a loss. Casting off the ballast of their old religious faith, almost certainly they sailed into deeper waters than they first intended, and at the end of their journey, many of them looked longingly backward, unable to cast off Mormonismâs spell entirely. Most of them continued to accept some of their old LDS teachingsâespecially Mormonismâs positive view of humankind and its eternal perfectibility. Even more compelling to the Godbeites were the warmth and friendships of Brighamâs social Zion, which, for all their complaints about Youngâs regime, continued to have a claim on them. And that serious, earnest, idealistic behavior that was so much a part of many nineteenth-century Mormons remained a characteristic of the dissentersâ personal lives.

In the end, Mormonism passed through the commercial and railroad crisis of 1869â70 rather easily. In navigating this passage, the rank-and-file Mormon had a role. Those members who ignored Youngâs merchant boycott and who ventured into the mines were undoubtedly motivated by narrow, materialistic reasons. These members were secular Saints, living in the community but not partaking of its spirit. Still other Mormons, perhaps a majority, were unaffected by events. Either due to lack of interest or other pressing concerns, they did not get involved in community issues. A third group helped to determine the outcome of the Godbeite revolt. For many in this group, faith was enough: they did not need to know when President Youngâs voice was his own or when it spoke for Providence. They simply believed. Also in this group were people like the sturdy Bishop Edwin Woolley, who, while sensing the need for change within the community, nevertheless made obedience a virtue.

When the Godbeite revolt first broke out in October 1869, outsiders believed that they saw the issue clearly. Wasnât this a simple declaration against priestly, heavy-handed rule? Historical events are seldom so clear-cut, as this book suggests. An event like the Godbeite revolt remains compelling because of its complexity and because many of its opposing ideals and contrasting virtues remain dilemmas to the present time.
