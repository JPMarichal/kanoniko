18:35 he was come nigh: This expression, coupled with notations about âthe way sideâ in this verse and âthe multitude pass byâ in the next, brings forward concretely the sense of movement, the action of Jesusâ continuing journey to Jerusalem,112 complete with dust kicked up by disciplesâ sandals and the irregular but constant drumbeat of footfalls, especially because we have seen no notice of Jesusâ movement since 17:11 (see the Notes on 9:51; 10:38; 14:25; 17:11; 19:28). The notation of place, then, ties off Lukeâs long journey section and represents geographically the lowest place that Jesus travels in contrast to the uplands of Jerusalem in the hills of Judea. Analysis

We now witness the fulfillment of one of the pillars of the Saviorâs announced agenda when he steps into his public ministry, the ârecovering of sight to the blindâ (4:18; see the Note thereon).131 To be sure, a personâs blindness may be spiritual and therefore, with divine aid, recovery of sight really consists in acquiring a proper spiritual perception. But the literal sense of Jesusâ declaration stands front and center, that he has come to give sight to the blind (see Mosiah 3:5). For Luke writes about Jesus healing âmany that were blind,â though he does not rehearse the stories (see 7:21â23). Matthew, of course, writes about Jesus healing two blind men in the city of Capernaum (see Matt. 9:27â31), and Mark reports that Jesus gives sight to a blind man in Bethsaida (see Mark 8:22â26). Luke may know these stories without repeating them, though we cannot be certain. In any event, Luke chooses to feature Jesusâ merciful action for the blind man who sightlessly patrols the road outside Jericho, the road that links to Galilee in the north and to Perea, the territory on the east bank of the Jordan River.132

The theme of discipleship continues to push itself forward. What appears to be a happy story about a forgotten blind man begging loudly for Jesus to give him the unimaginable gift of sight turns out to be a proof of Jesusâ words that among those who accept invitations to his kingdom will stand âthe poor, and the maimed, and the halt, and the blindâ (14:21; see the Note on 14:13). For after receiving his sight, the man âfollowed him, glorifying God,â with the sense that he persists in following Jesus (18:43; see the Note thereon). Mark adds the phrase, âfollowed Jesus in the way,â clearly implying that the blind man joins Jesusâ followers on the trek up to Jerusalem (Mark 10:52; emphasis added). As a continuing theme, discipleship will reappear as a major element in the following report on the tax collector Zacchaeus.

The account of the blind manâs healing also forms a conclusion of sorts for the journey section that begins at 9:51. In one sense, Jesusâ journey from Galilee to Jerusalem involves one last stop, in Jericho, the town that he and his entourage are approaching. Luke identifies this town alone throughout the whole itinerary, drawing attention to it just as he does to Jerusalem at the journeyâs beginning. Moreover, even when Jesus finally enters the capital city, Luke records that he goes into the temple area, saying nothing about Jerusalem (see 19:45, 47; 20:1). As a further touch, this incident forms the last recorded interaction between Jesus and those of lower social status, although the throngs in the temple certainly include unidentified poor and suffering people (see 21:1â4).

As a final note, the relationship of Luke to the other Synoptists, Matthew and Mark, bears reviewing. When placed side by side, the differences among the three accounts jump out at a reader much more brightly than the similarities. For example, according to Mark and Matthew, Jesus is on his way out of Jericho when he meets the blind man rather than entering the town (see Matt. 20:29; Mark 10:46). In an unusual twist, Matthew holds that Jesus heals two blind men, not one (see Matt. 20:30). In Markâs case, he reports part of a conversation between bystanders and the blind man as well as the fact that the blind man sets aside âhis garment,â then ârose, and came to Jesusâ without any apparent assistance (Mark 10:49â50). Another difference arises in Matthewâs narrative when he writes that âJesus had compassion . . . and touchedâ the eyes of the blind, aspects missing in Luke (Matt. 20:34). A final divergence, found in Mark, consists in Jesusâ additional words to the blind man, âGo thy way,â inviting him to go to family and friends even though he instead follows Jesus âin the wayâ (Mark 10:52). Although these differences do not completely discourage a view that Luke takes his story from one or both of the other Gospels, the fact that the report of the sons of Zebedee seeking special places in the kingdom stands before the healing of the blind at Jericho in the narratives of Matthew and Mark, but not in Luke, suggests the strong possibility that Luke does not depend fully or even partially on either of them for his story of the blind man (see Matt. 20:20â28; Mark 10:35â45).

We now witness the fulfillment of one of the pillars of the Saviorâs announced agenda when he steps into his public ministry, the ârecovering of sight to the blindâ (4:18; see the Note thereon).131 To be sure, a personâs blindness may be spiritual and therefore, with divine aid, recovery of sight really consists in acquiring a proper spiritual perception. In one sense, Jesusâ journey from Galilee to Jerusalem involves one last stop, in Jericho, the town that he and his entourage are approaching. As a further touch, this incident forms the last recorded interaction between Jesus and those of lower social status, although the throngs in the temple certainly include unidentified poor and suffering people (see 21:1â4).

When placed side by side, the differences among the three accounts jump out at a reader much more brightly than the similarities.
