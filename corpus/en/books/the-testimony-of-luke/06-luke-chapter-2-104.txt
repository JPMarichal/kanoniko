Analysis

In these memorable lines, Luke paints the broad imperial setting for following events, highlighting an important moment in the Roman Empire, the tax enrollment in the eastern provinces, particularly in Syria-Palestine. In our mindâs eye, we see streams of people trudging to their hometowns to enter their names on tax rolls. Then Luke beams light on one young couple, Mary and Joseph, who make the hundred-mile journey from Nazareth through the Jordan Valley to Bethlehem where, in humble surroundings, they welcome their first child.

As we have noted, a major issue has to do with the timing of the census. No Roman written source points to a tax enrollmentâeither local or empire-wideâthat occurs at the time of Jesusâ birth. Hence, some dismiss the accuracy of Lukeâs notation and, thereby, hold that Mary and Joseph are already living in Bethlehem, with no need to travel from Nazareth.24 But there are three cautions to advance against such a view. First, it is not uncommon that a single surviving source preserves important information about the ancient world. One thinks immediately of the anonymous Periplus of the Erythrean Sea, composed about AD 50, which survives in a single manuscriptâin Heidelberg from the tenth century ADâand offers an important account about seaborne trade between Arabia and the Roman world, opening a view onto antiquity that no other source preserves. Concerning the census in Lukeâs report, whether it is accurate or not, it stands as the only source. Second, it is largely a series of accidents that preserves most ancient sources, except perhaps those that are inscribed on a durable substance such as stone. Those written on perishable materialsâpapyrus, leatherâare at risk to loss from water, insects, fire, and humans. In the eastern and southern Mediterranean area, the dry climate of Egypt and the Dead Sea has sheltered a wealth of documents, virtually all of which have been discovered in unexpected places. Third, Lukeâs story fits what is known about Roman taxing practices from a century or so later, as census records from Egypt show. Hence, Lukeâs account does not stand out as unique, an aspect that would make it suspect.

This said, the actual dating of the census that Luke reports continues to present scholars with a chronological challenge because of the ten-year difference between the birth of Jesus during Herodâs reign (dies in 4 BC) and the only known census when Quirinius governs Syria (AD 6â7). Perhaps only the discovery of fresh historical evidence will bring a solution (see the Note on 2:2).

Although it is uncertain whether Mary or Joseph, or both, owns or stands to inherit property in or near Bethlehem, it is certain that Josephâs family originally comes from the town and it is most likely that Maryâs family originates there as well because of her descent from David (see Rom. 1:3). These observations carry the further likelihood that Maryâs family is tied to the Bethlehem area through living relatives or property. However, if relatives of the couple reside in or around Bethlehem, it seems that Mary and Joseph are late-coming guests to their home because of the location of Jesusâ birthâin a stable, which typically takes up the homeâs lower floor, or sits in a cave. Analysis

At the heart of these verses beats the principle of respect for law. In a concrete sense, Mary and Joseph fit snugly within this picture. Analysis

The temple serves as the anchor in the series of stories that begins with the visit of Mary and Joseph to perform the required sacrifices and to offer the redemption gift following the birth of Jesus. Those accounts finally lead us to Anna who is known openly in the city as one associated with the temple and its services. Lukeâs record, of course, will bring temple-related activities to a conclusion in chapter 2 with the story of Jesusâ Passover visit at age twelve (see 2:40â52). But a major focus of this chapter rests on events during one momentous day, one on which Jesusâ parents present the Christ child at the temple. Before the end of that day, God leads both Simeon and Anna to the child and inspires them in their praise. Annaâs known gift of prophecy (see 2:36), here manifested within the temple complex, confers on the infant Jesus a visible, palpable stamp of divine approval. To be sure, other events will do the same, but Annaâs arrival and subsequent witness borne to others will carry weight into the minds of bystanders.

As with Simeon, Annaâs praise arises within sacred precincts, linking the unfolding story of the Christ child more tightly to holiness. Her praise, too, rounds off the sense of promise and fulfillment that weave their way through the songs of Mary and Zacharias and the angels. Further, her status as a respected woman elevates the unfurling events, conferring on them a dignity and a feminine quality that they otherwise lack.

Annaâs name brings us back to the question of whether the story of Hannah influences Lukeâs narrative. Even if it does, this does not mean that we should see Anna as fictional, as a mere symbol. Even if much in Lukeâs narrative here links back to Hannah and her son Samuel, it is plain that Anna is a real person who comes by inspiration to where Joseph and Mary are. That said, summarizing statements about Jesus seem to tie to similar observations written about Samuel (see 1 Sam. 2:19, 26; 3:19). The statements about Jesus read: âthe child grew, and waxed strong in spirit, filled with wisdom: and the grace of God was upon himâ (Luke 2:40) and âJesus increased in wisdom and stature, and in favour with God and manâ (2:52). As an additional piece, Maryâs song as she enters the home of Elisabeth resembles that of Hannah (see 1 Sam. 2:1â10; Luke 1:46â55). And, of course, both Samuel and Jesus come as children of promise, dedicated to God.

Analysis

Although some commentators see the story of the youthful Jesusâ visit to the temple as an independent account that ties to little else that comes before or after it,106 in reality it stands as a connecting bridge between Lukeâs first two chapters and what follows. First, the temple as the center of divine activity holds the early reports together. Within the temple complex, the angel appears to Zacharias; the parents of the infant Jesus present him there; Simeon and Anna find him with the aid of the Spirit; and Simeon prophesies about the infant. This account of the youthful Jesus continues and ties off these temple-centered events. At the other end of the Gospel story, when Jesus comes to the temple as an adult, he is no stranger and, significantly, exhibits unabashed ownership when he takes control of the grounds, rids the sacred precinct of those who have made it âa den of thieves,â and calls it âMy houseâ (see 19:45â47).107

Analysis

In these memorable lines, Luke paints the broad imperial setting for following events, highlighting an important moment in the Roman Empire, the tax enrollment in the eastern provinces, particularly in Syria-Palestine. In our mindâs eye, we see streams of people trudging to their hometowns to enter their names on tax rolls. Then Luke beams light on one young couple, Mary and Joseph, who make the hundred-mile journey from Nazareth through the Jordan Valley to Bethlehem where, in humble surroundings, they welcome their first child.

As we have noted, a major issue has to do with the timing of the census. No Roman written source points to a tax enrollmentâeither local or empire-wideâthat occurs at the time of Jesusâ birth. Hence, some dismiss the accuracy of Lukeâs notation and, thereby, hold that Mary and Joseph are already living in Bethlehem, with no need to travel from Nazareth.24 But there are three cautions to advance against such a view. First, it is not uncommon that a single surviving source preserves important information about the ancient world. One thinks immediately of the anonymous Periplus of the Erythrean Sea, composed about AD 50, which survives in a single manuscriptâin Heidelberg from the tenth century ADâand offers an important account about seaborne trade between Arabia and the Roman world, opening a view onto antiquity that no other source preserves. Concerning the census in Lukeâs report, whether it is accurate or not, it stands as the only source. Second, it is largely a series of accidents that preserves most ancient sources, except perhaps those that are inscribed on a durable substance such as stone. Those written on perishable materialsâpapyrus, leatherâare at risk to loss from water, insects, fire, and humans. In the eastern and southern Mediterranean area, the dry climate of Egypt and the Dead Sea has sheltered a wealth of documents, virtually all of which have been discovered in unexpected places. Third, Lukeâs story fits what is known about Roman taxing practices from a century or so later, as census records from Egypt show. Hence, Lukeâs account does not stand out as unique, an aspect that would make it suspect.

This said, the actual dating of the census that Luke reports continues to present scholars with a chronological challenge because of the ten-year difference between the birth of Jesus during Herodâs reign (dies in 4 BC) and the only known census when Quirinius governs Syria (AD 6â7). Perhaps only the discovery of fresh historical evidence will bring a solution (see the Note on 2:2).

Although it is uncertain whether Mary or Joseph, or both, owns or stands to inherit property in or near Bethlehem, it is certain that Josephâs family originally comes from the town and it is most likely that Maryâs family originates there as well because of her descent from David (see Rom. 1:3). Moreover, the fact that the youthful Mary can readily find her cousin Elisabeth (see 1:39â40) indicates that she is familiar with the region and, when a child, must have visited there with her parents. These observations carry the further likelihood that Maryâs family is tied to the Bethlehem area through living relatives or property. However, if relatives of the couple reside in or around Bethlehem, it seems that Mary and Joseph are late-coming guests to their home because of the location of Jesusâ birthâin a stable, which typically takes up the homeâs lower floor, or sits in a cave. This humble beginning for Godâs Son, born to parents who are without a permanent home in Bethlehem, stands in marked contrast with the grandeur and opulence that encircle the Roman emperor whose decree sends Mary and Joseph scurrying to Bethlehem.25

The episode with the shepherds raises to view the matters of humility and, again, birth. Humility has to do with the social status of shepherds. Analysis
