Obert C. Tanner, Christ's Ideals for Living ( 1955)

Peace

"Let the peace of God rule in your hearts."

One great hope of mankind is that war will be replaced by arbitration laws as a means of settling international differences. In the meantime, war is a real possibility. We must therefore learn what we can about the causes of war and the elimination of these causes.

Our present position on war seems to be that if force and violence are used anywhere unrighteously, then force and violence may be righteously used to overcome such aggression.

Against this present attitude of force against unrighteous force, we face the teachings of Christ, of returning good for evil. The problem may be put thus: If we meet force with force, ill will with ill will, violence with violence, can we ever escape the vicious cycle it produces? One war sows the seeds for another. Is not our only hope to break the cycle by generosity and patience-infinite patience? All of us must see wisdom in the patience expressed by George C. Marshall: "We must take the nations of the world as they are, the human passions and prejudices of peoples as they exist, and find some way to secureÂ�a peaceful world.'' [^1]

Most men would prefer to risk war than avoid it at the cost or even the risk of slavery. America is strong today because of this natural feeling of those who love liberty. The question is this: besides building great military strength, are we building great moral and spiritual strength to handle this power? The preamble to the UNESCO Charter says that wars are made in the minds of men, and the question now is whether we are making war or peace in our attitudes and understanding of the present world situation.

This much is clear: that our moral and religious ideals may not win out in the race with our lower animal natures — fear that breeds hatred, pugnacity that is careless of consequences, prejudice that will not listen to reason, and minds that are poorly trained for a new world outlook — a world full of "small-scale individuals."

The answer is not difficult for those who believe in Christ. While there may be justification for righteous wrath, we must yet strive with all our might to replace impatience with patience, retaliation with generosity, ill will with good will, violence with restraint, and above all, plain ordinary blind prejudice must be replaced with a mind that is open to new facts and able to face changing circumstances. It boils down to this: In our national life and in our personal attitudes we must follow the teachings of Christ and "overcome evil with good" if we are to break the terrible cycle of violence for violence and ill will for ill will.

Christ taught an all-inclusive and undiscourageable love, even toward our enemies. In substance, He asked us to rise above the common standard of reciprocity; to be patient whether we can count on patience from others or not; to have restraint even if the other person becomes emotional and unrestrained; to understand the adversary whether He reciprocates in trying to understand or not; in short, not to let our position of what is right be influenced by the conduct of the other person. To be such a person is to be a peacemaker whom Jesus called blessed; it is to live without hatred and vindictiveness, to live with pain and suffer death rather than cause them.

While all or most men would agree that wars are undesirable, many still believe they are inevitable. They observe that individual men are mere "chips in the irresistible stream of social forces." They add that when rulers are war-minded or fail to intelligently solve national or international problems, war is ever the price of such failure. But a great psychologist, Professor Gordon W. Allport, describes a deeper reason for the causes that bring us to war:

There are no social forces excepting as they are created by, and resident in, men themselves. There are no rulers or leaders who are not themselves fallible men maintaining their positions by the consent or forceful control of the governed. At bottom, the causes of war must be sought in the mental and social conflicts that bedevil the minds of followers and leaders. The disorder may be personal. A man on bad terms with himself may grow warlike; and if he is a Hitler, a Stalin, or a Mussolini, may do incalculable damage. Or the disorder may be inter-personal. Men may be so constantly irritated by the failure of their social relationships that they grow to think of brute force as the only possible solution.

But a man who is badly frustrated and irritated by his neighbor does not ordinarily resort to killing him. He contrives somehow to live on terms of uneasy but essentially moral relations with him. He may arbitrate his difficulties, forgive the man, or at least restrain his own destructive impulses. Greater difficulties arise, however, when the irritant is a group of persons or a nation distant from the self. The moralization of group relations seems always more difficult to achieve than the moralization of personal relations. Over and over again we see how group conflict runs its course on a lower plane than does inter-personal conflict. When the adversary is remote, decency tends to disappear. Conscious only of group differences (imagined or real), the Nazis exterminated the Jews; whites segregate and humiliate negroes; nations impersonally slaughter one another. [^2]

This dilemma, so well depicted by Reinhold Niebuhr in his Moral Man and Immoral Society, seems to have two possible solutions. One is to fortify the imagination of individual men so that each identifies himself with every fellow mortal of whatever race, religion, or nationality. Such universalizing of a man's sentiments is theoretically achievable, for in all lands we find individuals who would never knowingly injure physically, financially, or in any other way, any other human being. With proper training, habits of justice in dealing with out-groups can become as ingrained as habits of justice in dealing with members of one's own in-group.

The other means of moralizing group contacts is by the creation of interlocking loyalties. Let smaller groups become parts of larger groups. Let school children be taught loyalty to the United Nations as well as loyalty to their respective countries. It is a fortunate fact that minds are capable of developing concentric loyalties, the larger including but not effacing the smaller. Wendell Willkie was no less devoted to his nation and to his culture because his daily spiritual diet consisted of the Hindu Scriptures, the Koran, and the Christian hymn, Lead, Kindly Light.

Yet the task of moralizing group behavior is extremely difficult. It is everywhere considered virtuous for individual men to give up personal advantage in favor of justice to others, but as J. C. Flugel, the British psychologist, has said, "We are only beginning dimly to realize that the same holds true of states, that modesty, humility, charity, self-sacrifice, and neighborliness may be as becoming to states as they are to individuals, and that boastfulness, bullying, aggressiveness, and breach of faith are as morally contemptible in a nation as they would be in an individual.'' [^3]

States will never have these virtues unless their citizens and leaders develop them, for states have no minds of their own. What is needed is for the citizen who has these virtues in his personal relations to insist that they be observed by those who form the policy of the nation. It is not an easy thing to do. It is far easier for the harassed citizen to embrace precisely the opposite course of action. As threats mount — and especially when the leaders of an "enemy" country show no signs of human virtue — the individual is likely to divest himself of personal responsibility and hand over his conscience to his rulers. And while there have been great national leaders in times of trouble, few of them have even tried to transcend the interests of the particular nation and point a way for peace and progress for all nations alike — including the enemy.

Perhaps the day is not far off when decency will maintain itself, even though the adversary is remote. Latter-day Saints, believing in the fatherhood of God and the brotherhood of man, are deeply concerned about the quality of our moral leadership in international relations. It makes no difference whether the adversary is remote or near, the eternal principles of right must prevail in our dealings with all, for all are children of God, infinitely precious to Him. Our leaders in foreign affairs are judged by these principles of fair play, by their attitudes of good will and patience and the golden rule, which Christ expects of us in every human relation, far and near.

The price of peace is an enlargement of intellectual and spiritual horizons. One does not love his country less but more when his ideas and devotions consider all mankind. When Socrates said, "I am a citizen, not of Athens or Greece, but of the world," he was yet a loyal and patriotic Athenian. One does not love his family less but more by devotion to good community causes. Love for all mankind begins at home. Like a hot-house plant that is transplanted to a larger environment, love can grow to include the neighborhood, the community, the nation, and finally, all the children of God, wherever they may be and whatever differences they possess. Such is a basic principle of the Gospel. The world has become a neighborhood in a physical sense by the sciences of communication. It has yet to become a community of neighbors within our minds and hearts. Here the Gospel has much to tell us and we have much to learn.

Would the nations of the earth that are at war with each other be at war as they are, if the Spirit of God Almighty had pervaded their souls and moved and actuated them in their designs? No; not at all. Worldly ambition, pride, and the love of power, determination on the part of rulers to prevail over their competitors in the national games of life, wickedness at heart, desire for power, for worldly greatness, have led the nations of the earth to quarrel with one another and have brought them to war and self-destruction. I presume there is not a nation in the world today that is not tainted with this evil more or less. It may be possible, perhaps, to trace the cause of the evil, or the greatest part of it, to some particular nation of the earth; but I do not know. This I do believe, with all my heart, that the hand of God is striving with certain nations of the earth to preserve and protect human liberty, freedom to worship Him according to the dictates of conscience, freedom and the inalienable right of men to organize national governments in the earth, to choose for themselves their own leaders; men whom they may select as standards of honor, of virtue, and truth; men of wisdom, understanding and integrity; men who have at heart the well-being of the people who choose them to govern, to enact and execute the laws in righteousness. I believe that the Lord's hand is over the nations of the world today, to bring about this rule and this reign of liberty and righteousness among the nations of the earth. He has some hard material to work with, too. He is working with men who never prayed, men who have never known God, nor Jesus Christ whom He hath sent into the world, and whom to know is life eternal. God is dealing with nations of infidels, men who fear not God, and love not the truth, men who have no respect for virtue, or purity of life. God is dealing with men who are full of pride, and ambition; and He will find it difficult, I fear, to control them and lead them directly in the course that He would have them pursue to accomplish His purposes; but He is striving to uplift. God is striving to bless, to benefit, to happily, to ameliorate the condition of His children in the world, to give them freedom from ignorance and a knowledge of Him to learn of His ways and to walk in His paths, that they may have His spirit to be with them always, to lead them into all truth. -Joseph F. Smith

Only love can bring peace. Anyone who permits himself to hate any person, or any people, makes his own small but vital contribution towards the discord and trouble, while he who seeks to love others, even his enemies, makes his own priceless contribution to peace. True, lasting peace, when it comes to the world, will be made up of the many tricklets of love flowing from the hearts of the people to a common understanding and a common goal. The gift of peace on earth and good will to men was offered to the world in the gospel of living taught by our Savior in his earthly ministry. He made love of God and love of mankind fundamental, and declared, "On these two commandments hang all the law and the prophets." ( Matt. 22:40) The power of love alone can permanently disarm the world. [^6]
