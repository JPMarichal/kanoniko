The ideal of progress as a real solid factor in human life has had a number of set-backs in the twentieth century. There is now a mood of uncertainty and doubt about the future. At least the shallow optimism of the last century, up to 1914, is now gone, as it should be. The atomic age is a sobering one. Man is now pictured as a child playing with toys that can utterly destroy him. Present literature is telling us more about the cussedness and downright wickedness of human nature. Nearly all of the old optimism is gone. We are taking a new look at ourselves and the view is not encouraging.

It is true we make progress in advancing our knowledge. Libraries keep adding to their shelves and this cumulative knowledge continues at a rapid pace. Physical conveniences expand with new scientific discoveries. But of the progress of man, his character and his happiness, we are not at all assured of progress just at these points where progress would count so much. Is progress real for man in the way he gives his allegiance to the moral law, to broader sympathies — justice, decency, and humaneness? If not, scientific advances and new political organizations will not save us. Indeed, they may destroy us, physically or culturally.

President Antoine R. Ivins has analyzed our situation. In a simple yet profound way, he has not only stated the problem, but a solution that is the only one that is meaningful to Latter-day Saints:

If we could teach the world the proper concept of God and Christ and our relationship to them, and do it in such a way that it would sink into their hearts and impel them to study the teachings of the Savior, then apply them in their lives, all these troubles that we worry about now would cease. But the trouble is, men are avaricious; they are selfish; they are ambitious for power. They have nearly all of the attributes which, if left unbridled, lead to these terrible consequences. It is only the Spirit of God and Christ coming into the heart of man that can change his nature so that he will serve properly his fellow men. That, I think, is the purpose of the gospel of Jesus Christ, that we should learn that great commandment of God, the second, that we should love our fellows as we love ourselves; if we love God and serve him, we will serve our fellows, because serving them is the only way we can manifest to Him that we are willing to serve HimÂ�

Progress is so thoroughly a part of Latter-day Saint philosophy that Mormonism might be called a religion of progress. There are two reasons for this. The first is faith in God and the Gospel plan which is a full denial of grounds for any deep pessimism. After all, God is the answer to every discouragement that would liken man's lot in the universe to the position of one on a cake of ice that is gradually melting, to sink him and all his hopes. With God there can be no genuine despair. With Him there is purpose and destiny and love. The second ground for faith in progress is the Mormon conception of man. Man is not essentially evil and undeserving, depending solely on the grace of God for salvation, as most Christians believe. Man is essentially good, and lives that he might have joy — joy in a life that is liberated by a faith in the reality of Gospel ideals — obedience to moral laws, living with God as the unseen Companion and Helper of the way, and a love for every child of God in every part of the world — serving just as wide a circle of God's children as opportunity allows, with a love that grows until it includes all of humanity. And beyond this life we continue with higher capacities and fuller powers. One might express this ideal of eternal progress by repeating an unknown writer: "We are reading the first verse of the first chapter of a book whose pages are infinite." [^2]

Such a view gives zest and exuberance to life which lightens the way, even in darker hours. It is the Gospel of Christ. One of its finest expressions was given by Dr. Henry Eyring:

I am happy to represent a people who throughout their history have encouraged learning and scholarship in all fields of honorable endeavor, a people who have among their scriptural teachings such lofty concepts as these:

"The glory of God is intelligence, or in other words, light and truth." (Doctrine & Covenants 93:36)

"It is impossible for a man to be saved in ignorance." (Doctrine & Covenants 131: 6)

"Whatever principle of intelligence we attain unto in this life, it will rise with us in the resurrection." (Doctrine & Covenants 130: 18)

To us has come the following which we regard as a divine injunction:

"Teach ye diligently and my grace shall attend you, that you may be instructed more perfectly in theory, in principle, in doctrine, in the law of the gospel, in all things that pertain unto the kingdom of God, that are expedient for you to understand;

"Of things both in heaven and in the earth, and under the earth; things which have been, things which are, things which must shortly come to pass; things which are at home, things which are abroad; the wars and the perplexities of the nations, and the judgments which are on the land; and a knowledge also of countries and of kingdoms." (Doctrine & Covenants 88:78-79) [^3]

The teachings of Christ have this recurring theme: "Ye have heard that it was said by them of old timeÂ�But I say unto you."

The ideal of progress has never found a better expression. For example, Jesus lived in a day when strong faith in God was prior to faith in man, and He greatly disturbed people with the idea that "He that loveth not his brother whom he hath seen, cannot love God whom he hath not seen." Jesus upset the religious people of His time, who thought forgiveness from God came before forgiveness of each other. He said, "If ye forgive not men their trespasses, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses." People of that time put great store on proper worship and considered that love of God would be followed by love of fellow man. But Jesus reversed this idea completely: "If, therefore, thou art offering thy gift at the altar, and there rememberest that thy brother hath aught against thee, leave there thy gift before the altar, and go thy way, first be reconciled to thy brother, and then come and offer thy gift." Concerning the attitude he wanted toward himself, Jesus put the matter on a basis of service to the lowliest and least. "Â�Inasmuch as ye have done it unto one of the least of these my brethren, ye have done it unto me." ( Matt. 25:40)

The road to the heart of God was to be found by the road that leads to the heart of man. This does not mean that faith in God first will not be a powerful factor in love and service to man, but it is true that progress of love to God generally begins, and is measured by love and service to man.

Christ's life reveals another factor of progress, that is, a faith in values that do not leave a man at the mercy of circumstances and other people. It is a fellowship with God and obedience to a way of living that "overcomes the world." So much of our happiness is contingent upon others and upon our outward circumstances. But there is an inner peace Christ speaks of, that "surpasseth all understanding," because it puts us right with moral and intellectual and spiritual truths — the language of God that lifts us above little things to see the larger scene under the eye of eternity. President McKay writes: "From the forty days' fast on the mount of temptation to the moment on the cross when he cried in triumph: "It is finished!" Christ's life was a divine example of subduing and overcoming. Full of significance are his words spoken in his farewell address to his disciples: 'These things I have spoken unto you, that in me ye might have peace. In the world ye shall have tribulation: but be of good cheer; I have overcome the world.' ( John 16:33)" [^4]

In the parable of the unprofitable servant, Jesus spoke of those which do "that which was our duty to do." It is a test of progress, to do more than is requested; to live in the spirit that goes beyond commandments; to forgive more than seven times; to go two miles where only one is requested; to do more than anyone has a right to expect. Shakespeare portrayed this ethic of Jesus in describing his character, Desdemona: "She is of so free, so kind, so apt, so blessed a disposition, she holds it a vice in her goodness not to do more than she is requested.'' [^5]

Progress, in the last analysis, is spiritual. It is of the unseen world, of values related to the good, the true, and the beautiful; of the mind's upreach in companionship with better minds, for progress depends upon being transformed "by the renewal of your mind." Finally, progress leads to God and His will, carried out in the life of each one who believes in the promise, "Â�Ask, and it shall be given you; seek, and ye shall find; knock, and it shall be opened unto you." ( Luke 11:9)

I was not ever thus, nor prayed that Thou Shouldst lead me on; I loved to choose and see my path; But now Lead Thou me on! I loved the garish day, and spite of fears, Pride ruled my will; remember not past years!

So long Thy power hath blest me, sure it still will lead me on. O'er moor and fen, o'er crag and torrent, till The night is gone, And with the morn those angel faces smile, Which I have loved long since, and lost awhile! [^6]

Sometimes the words of Christ may be unconvincing to the hard-headed realists who feel that some other way to progress is better. Here is the opinion of Francis B. Sayre, Professor of Criminal Law of the Harvard Law School: "Christ is often pictured as an impractical dreamer, an idealist ignorant of the cold realities of this workaday world. That is profoundly untrue. He was the most downright and practical realist the world has ever known. Nineteen centuries of human experience have proved that lives and institutions built on his teachings bear infinite fruit and that those built on an opposing philosophy prove ultimately self-defeating, and perish.'' [^7]

Progress in our personal lives is difficult to measure. John Ruskin tries it with these thoughts: "He only is advancing in life whose heart is getting softer, whose blood warmer, whose brain quicker, whose spirit is entering into living peace. And the men who have this life in them are the true lords or kings of the earth-they and they only.'' [^8]

Henry Drummond wrote the following paragraph which one might call a signpost to further progress:

(1) Pray. (2) Think. (3) Talk to wise people but do not regard their decision as final. (4) Beware of the bias of your own will, but do not be too much afraid of it. God never necessarily thwarts a man's nature and likings, and it is a mistake to think that his will is in the line of the disagreeable. (5) Meanwhile, do the next thing, for doing God's will in small things is the best preparation for doing it in great things. (6) When the decision and action are necessary, go ahead. (7) Never reconsider the decision when it is finally acted upon. And (8) You will probably not find out till afterward, perhaps long afterward, that you have been led at all. [^9]

Dr. Fosdick writes:

What is it that gives dignity to life, lifts it out of mediocrity, saves it from futility and insignificance and makes it in the long run worth living? To get one's eye on the best, even in a bad time, and to stand by that! We are not much as individuals, we can terribly despise ourselves; but there is something that can give even our small lives dignity and significance. To have seen the best in our time, and to have stood by it — that does elevate even the humblest life to dignity and worth. [^10]

Concerning the road to progress that lies ahead for young people, Henry Ward Beecher wrote these cautioning lines:

Let me say to every one that is beginning life: Do not begin with exaggerated ideas of your own worth. Do not think that you, without battle, ought to be victor, and walk from the beginning with those laurels about your head which are to be twined there, if at all, only at the end of the campaign. Do not mistake your own turbulent pride. Do not mistake your own false interpreting, lying vanity. Do not begin your life feeling that such a fine fellow as you are — one so spruce, so handsome, so well-dressed, so accomplished in various ways — deserves a high place. Do not flatter yourself that life owes you any more than it owes anybody else. It owes you, in common with all others, just as much as, climbing, you can bring down. It owes you a chance to be something. It will give you that, and nothing more. It is better for every man to begin with this understanding: I have a chance to carve out my own way. This is all I want. Having that, I will take the consequences. [^11]

I do not ask to walk smooth paths Give me such courage I can scale Nor bear an easy load. The hardest peaks alone I pray for strength and fortitude And transform every stumbling block To climb the rock strewn road. Into a stepping stone. [^12]

-Gail Brook Burket

Any discussion about personal progress will not go far until it runs into the need for humility — learning from mistakes, for our failures and mistakes can be valuable guide — posts to progress if we allow their lessons, however painful, to leave their marks, not regretfully, but gratefully, as we go forward.

John Homer Miller writes:

Your size is determined by the manner in which you take criticism. The moment he is criticized the little person flies to his own self-defense. He seeks to justify himself. He shows resentment. He does not even consider whether the criticism is intended to be hurtful or helpful. The big person, on the other hand, listens to all kinds of criticism attentively, appreciatively, and understandingly. He expects it. He knows he cannot possibly please everybody all the time. He is the kind of person who, if he did please everybody all the time, would think something was wrong with him. It is said that even Jupiter cannot please everybody. In conversations I have listened to, even God comes in for more than his share of criticism. He is about the most criticized person in the universe. [^13]

From compromise and things half-done, Keep me, with stern and stubborn pride. And when, at last, the fight is won, God, keep me still unsatisfied. [^14]

-Louis Untermeyer

Besides individual progress, the social aspect of progress is now of grave concern in the present world upheaval. Presently men give their faith or doubt to an organization like the United Nations. Progress will be greatly advanced if men give up war as a means of settling differences. At present the United Nations organization offers hope for this advancement. There is no other international organization, and if men have faith in it, the day may not be far off when war will be outlawed, just as slavery and child labor are now becoming relics of the past. Some way must be found for men to live under international law. International anarchy — each nation a law unto itself — has caused one war after another. A law-abiding world will require the willingness of nations to give up a portion of their sovereignty. Whether they are willing to do so, before another war, is uncertain.

Man comes a pilgrim of the universe, Out of the mysteries that were before The world, out of the wonder of old stars. Far roads have felt his feet, forgotten wells Have glassed his beauty bending down to drink.

At altar-fires anterior to Earth His soul was lighted, and it will burn on After the suns have wasted in the void. His feet have felt the pressure of old worlds, And are to tread on others yet unnamed — Worlds sleeping yet in some new dream of God. [^15]

-Edwin Markham

The social progress within nations moves forward day by day. Medical science has removed most of the plagues that have cursed man's history. Scientific agriculture gives promise of food, now inadequate for the great majority of the twenty-three hundred million people on earth. Opportunity for learning is being given to larger numbers of people throughout the world. Democracy has its losses, but the gains outweigh them. Labor and management work together. Race prejudice is melting away like ice before the sun.

For Latter-day Saints the future is bright as the Church grows all over the world. Mormonism has a dynamic power the world has never been able to understand. Among many reasons is an ideal deeply believed in and practiced by the Latter-day Saints — the ideal of eternal progression.

The Lord is extending the Saints' understanding. Restoring their judges and all as at first. The knowledge and power of God are expanding, The veil o'er the earth is beginning to burst. [^16]

-William W. Phelps

Dr. M. Lynn Bennion, former supervisor of L.D.S. Seminaries, writes:

Religion does not teach us everything at once. It does not clear up all our difficulties. Some religious teachers have seemed to think that it is a reproach to religion not to be able to give a categorical answer to every question. They make assertions where the wise and reverent person often will be silent or recognize his lack of insight. There are many things which still are wrapped in wonder, and many ways in which we see God not fully but only in glimpses, like sunshine through the clouds. [^17]

We believe all that God has revealed, all that He does now reveal, and we believe that He will yet reveal many great and important things pertaining to the Kingdom of God. [^18]

Nevertheless, my brethren and sisters, we are laboring and progressing in this work. Zion is advancing; the kingdom of God is rolling on. It is these young men and maidens who dwell in these valleys of the mountains that have got to assume this responsibility. Upon their shoulders this kingdom has got to rest, when their fathers and elders have passed to the other side of the veil. This is before me just as plain as the light of the sun in the firmament of heaven. [^21]

Knowledge lying dormant and not employed to useful purposes is of very little value. One may attain membership in the Church, for example, by complying with all the requisite initiatory ordinances, but if he stops there, as some do, he may not assume that his salvation is assured, no matter how correct in the abstract his personal conduct may be. One must progress or retrograde. One cannot stand still. Activity is the law of growth, and growth, progress, is the law of life. Obedience to the governing law, cooperation with others, helping them to build up the kingdom of God, is indispensable. [^22]

-Albert E. Bowen

Spiritual understanding and power and discernment and self-mastery do not come gratuitously. They must be won. Jesus Himself, whose penetrating vision pierced through and beyond physical boundaries, wrestled in the wilderness for forty days before entering upon His great ministry of self-consecration and spiritual supremacy, and He called mightily upon the Father for comfort and support. But from the day of His victory over temptation His life was one of active, aggressive practice of the virtues He taught.

There is in the plan for life as proposed by religion no promise of freedom from care or sorrow or suffering or disparagement or want. But through all these, fidelity to the overarching purpose must stand, unyielding and undismayed. Through experience and mastery and purification and spiritual expansion man fits himself for greater things both here and beyond, while living life among men in this material physical world. [^23]

-Albert E. Bowen

We have been placed upon this earth because of our faithfulness in having kept our first estate. The labors that we performed in the sphere that we left before we came here have had a certain effect upon our lives here, and to a certain extent they govern and control the lives that we lead here, just the same as the labors that we do here will control and govern our lives when we pass from this stage of existence. [^24]

-Heber J. Grant

The highest calling the Lord ever called any human being to, in any age of the world, has been to receive the Holy Priesthood, with its keys and powers. [^25]

-Wilford Woodruff

Some of the brightest spirits who dwell in the bosom of the Father are making their appearance among this people, of whom the Lord will make a Royal Priesthood, a peculiar nation that He can own and bless, talk with, and associate with. [^26]

-Brigham Young

Whether we are young or old in years, it is never safe to set aside any proved principle. For each promise and penalty will follow our performance as surely as this day has followed all those that have preceded it.

That we shall move quickly or that we shall perform spectacularly is not of first importance — but that we shall move surely and safely from moment to moment, from hour to hour, and from day to day, is the procedure for all who would reach their highest possibilities, for after the portals of this life open and close behind us, we shall still have ourselves to live with, and our performance to account for.

The greatest rewards, here and hereafter, are for consistency of performance. "If you keep my commandments and endure to the end" — not only for today, not only for tomorrow, but also this day, and always — "you shall have eternal life, which gift is the greatest of all the gifts of God.'' [^27]

-John A. Widtsoe
