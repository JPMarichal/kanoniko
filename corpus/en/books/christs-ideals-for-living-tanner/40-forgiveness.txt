"Her sins, which are many, are forgiven for she loved much."

Forgiveness is defined as "an act or decision of an offended person whereby the offender ceases to incur the displeasure of the injured party and is released from penaltiesÂ�" [^1]

Forgiveness has two spheres of meaning: ( 1 ) Forgiveness of God to man, and (2) Man's forgiveness of man.

1 . God's forgiveness is never fully understood unless one can comprehend the sin of which man is capable — the devastated continents in war, the suffering of dissevered families, ruined childhood, disease, prisons, hunger, greed, hate — man's inhumanity to man makes a staggering total. One can begin to appreciate God's forgiveness by first comprehending the sins which bind us down. Sins can be so enslaving that every youth should first soberly count the cost. This is the darker background of man's life, concerning which the Savior came to free us. And while the psychiatrists are probably right in distrusting religious teachings that undermine self-respect by their aggravation of excessive guilty feelings, nevertheless, we must be realists and stop talking about sin in apologetic terms. Sin is terrible, and we ought to face it frankly, without excuses, without sparing ourselves.

2 . The second aspect of this ideal is the forgiveness of man by his fellow man. One of the great statements on forgiveness was given by President Joseph F. Smith and repeated by Elder Matthew Cowley in General Conference, April 1950:

We hope and pray that you will go from this conference to your homes, feeling in your hearts and from the depths of your souls to forgive one another and never from this time forth bear malice toward another fellow creature. I do not care whether he is a member of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints or not, whether he is a friend or foe, whether he is good or bad.

It is extremely hurtful for any man holding the Priesthood and enjoying the Gift of the Holy Ghost to harbor a spirit of envy, or malice, or retaliation, or intolerance toward or against his fellowmen. We ought to say in our hearts, "Let God judge between me and thee, but as for me, I will forgive." Jesus made clear that the great obstacle to the right relations between men is their unwillingness to overlook injuries. We cannot be guided by the behavior of others toward ourselves, for this would limit the good we can do in the world. We must forgive and forget more than others are willing to do if we are to come to the best relationships with man and God. Since one of the great attributes of God is forgiveness, our worthiness to be His children depends on how much capacity we have to share this attribute with Him. Here, as always, Jesus asks that we go beyond the law, rules, customary practices, and have within us a spirit in which resentments do not dwell. And so be able to forgive, "not seven times; but until seventy times seven."

Tennyson expresses this ideal where King Arthur speaks to his Queen, Guinevere, lying at his feet in a nunnery, now penitent of her treason:

Thou hast not made my life so sweet to me, That I the King should greatly care to live; For thou hast spoilt the purpose of my life.

Yet think not that I come to urge thy crimes, I did not come to curse thee, Guinevere, I, whose vast pity almost makes me die To see thee, laying there thy golden head, My pride in happier summers, at my feet. The wrath which forced my thoughts on that fierce law, The doom of treason and the flaming death, (When first I learnt thee hidden here) is past.

And all is past, the sin is sinn'd, and I, Lo! I forgive thee, as Eternal God Forgives. [^6]

Forgiveness is not just a sentimental pardon of one person toward another, nor pardon to all indiscriminately.

Forgiveness requires a move from the one to be forgiven, penitence, like the prodigal son who decided "I will arise and go." Could his father have forgiven him when he was living in debauchery in a far country? He was surely willing to do so. But repentance is not so simple a matter.

The other person, the wrongdoer, must be penitent before real forgiveness can take place. Forgiveness, like all ideals of the Gospel, has some prior conditions to its realization. One of these is mercy by the forgiver, and the other is penitence by the one forgiven. Christ was willing to forgive those who crucified Him, and He prayed for God to forgive them — but the moral law of forgiveness would yet demand their penitence. Jesus made the hypothetical nature of forgiveness clear-the "if" involved, when He said: "If thy brother trespass against thee, rebuke him; and if he repent, forgive him."

The spirit of forgiveness, nevertheless, always lives with a disciple of Christ, even though such a spirit alone does not fulfill the law of forgiveness. So Whittier writes of a spirit that would rise above all life's hurtful sufferings:

My heart was heavy, for its trust had been Abused, its kindness answered with foul wrong; So, turning gloomily from my fellow-men One summer Sabbath day I strolled among The green mounds of the village burial-place; Where, pondering how all human love and hate Find one sad level; and how, soon or late, Wronged and wrongdoer, each with meekened face, And cold hands folded over a still heart, Pass the green threshold of our common grave, Whither all footsteps tend, whence none depart, Awed for myself, and pitying my race, Our common sorrow, like a mighty wave, Swept all my pride away, and trembling, I forgave!
