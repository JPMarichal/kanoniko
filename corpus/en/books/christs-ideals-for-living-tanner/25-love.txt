Obert C. Tanner, Christ's Ideals for Living ( 1955)

Part Three: Christ's Ideals for Social Living

Love

"I say unto you, Love your enemies."

Love is the heart of the life and teachings of Christ. Jesus made it the test of discipleship. "By this," he said, "shall all men know that ye are my disciples, if ye have love one to another." ( John 14:35)

History is filled with the failures of this ideal. But then, so are our lives today. It is a difficult standard to live by.

Especially painful are failures in religious life-just where failures of this ideal should not be found. For example, in centuries of Christianity the misguided followers of Christ made war instead of peace, hostile divisions instead of unity, excommunications instead of friendly tolerance, legalistic harshness in place of kindness, and bigotry where humility should have been. Yet when one adds up the sins that follow in the path of men's frailties-man's love of power, envy, jealousy, vanity — the failure of love is hardly to be wondered at. Rather it is amazing so much love and fellowship prevail.

It was said by a professor in one of our large universities, a non-member of the Church, that perhaps the one place in all the world where one may find genuine fellowship of a whole community, is in a Mormon community. This could very well be true because theory and practice, the ideal and the actual, are always closely associated in Mormon practice. Church leaders in every ward are our fellow-workers in daily life. In our Sunday Schools, priesthood meetings, sacrament services, etc., men are presiding who teach and preach. These men and women are drawn from a work-a-day world with all its struggles and temptations. With their work in the Church the ideal is brought to earth, and the earth is made to reach for the ideal.

And only so, can Christianity be restored to the earth when the ideal and the practice are brought together.

Christ's ideal of love makes two particularly difficult demands: (1) Love of enemies, and (2) love that goes beyond the barriers of race, class, and family.

1 . The love of enemies is so difficult that many frank and honest people have called it unattainable perfectionism. The customary standard is reciprocity — being fair to those who are fair to us, giving our good will to those from whom we have received good will, etc. "Far past that went Jesus' thinking," writes Dr. Fosdick. "Be decent, he said, whether others are decent or not. Exercise good will whether they do or not. Never let your rightness depend upon anothers' conduct. Do not hate because you are hated, or revile because you are reviled. If two blows must be given, prefer taking both to giving one. Never let vindictiveness be the motive or retaliation the standard of your conduct, else your conduct will be at the mercy of others' ill behavior. Keep your own criteria of character intact and independent. Love even when hated, bless even when cursed." [^1]

Socrates expressed the ideal, though not in the affirmative, outgoing way that Christ called for. Socrates said that it was better to suffer injury than to inflict one. For he who hurts another hurts himself more — the wound is deeper in the person who does a wrong than in him who receives it. (Plato's Georgias 468.)

But Christ called for more:

Ye have heard that it hath been said, An eye for an eye, and a tooth for a tooth:

But I say unto you, That ye resist not evil: but whosoever shall smite thee on thy right cheek turn to him the other also.

And if any man will sue thee at the law, and take away thy coat, let him have thy cloke also.

And whosoever shall compel thee to go a mile, go with him twain. ( Matt. 3:38-41)

In the above scripture there is one phrase that has caused trouble to many. Jesus said, "resist not evil." Yet Jesus resisted evil. But whenever He did so, it was an evil that was done to others, not to Himself. We have no record of Jesus resisting an evil done to Himself, it was always a public or social evil He sought to redress. This point is made by Robert Louis Stevenson: "The truth of Christ's teaching seems to be this: In our own person and fortune, we should be ready to accept and pardon all; it is our cheek we are to turn; it is our coat we are to give away to the man who has taken our cloak. But when another's face is buffeted perhaps a little of the lion will become us best. That we are to suffer others to be injured and stand by, is not conceivable and surely not desirable." [^2]

2 . The second demand in Christ's ideal of love is a love of such inclusive good will that it reaches those not of our family, not of our community, not of our country, not of our race, but includes all of humankind as children of one God — all brothers because of one Fatherhood.

Like all ideals, this one of brotherhood — deep, genuine fellowship and a feeling of kinship with every man and woman on earth — this ideal is infinitely more easily believed than practiced. Indeed, religion can easily fail most at this point — dividing rather than uniting mankind, as the history of religions so amply testifies. Yet it is on this ideal that we come near to Christ or remove ourselves from Him. This is the crucial test: whether our discipleship to the Savior breaks down the barriers of our prejudices, or whether we miss the gospel of Christ because in our minds and hearts are attitudes and feelings that Christ Himself would condemn. The parable of the Good Samaritan is often referred to as the essence of Christianity. The identity of the man who fell among thieves in Christ's story remains unknown. But we do know that whatever, or whoever, he was, he was a neighbor of every man who henceforth would call himself a Christian. Christ left the man's identity in this parable unknown because He is answering the question of the lawyer: "Who is my neighbor?" Our neighbor is every man in need-not alone the need of first aid, but the need of friendliness, understanding, and service, regardless of race or creed or merit. There is to be no boundary to love, no limitation to good will.

On this ideal of love, Christ is our "eternal contemporary." How desperately we need more kindness and sympathy today is obvious to all who read of man's new weapons of war. Christ does not need to be brought up to date. We need to be brought up to date with Him. The need is clear: The plain, simple Gospel of our Lord Jesus Christ. On this one issue, our civilization will stand or perish. There is no third alternative. Our hearts must be cleansed of hatred, our minds must be opened with more facts, our spirits must be attuned with Christ's. The alternative is clear, and the future is sealed if we fail. To be a Latter-day Saint with a saving Gospel for all humanity, is to be a person with love for all the human race, with a mind aware of world situations, of world problems, of possible world solutions. Once we sought food and shelter in mountain valleys, but we have always gone to the world as missionaries with Christ's gospel of love, with a broad understanding of our world, that this Restored Gospel might take root and be a blessing to all people.

As stated above, the great need of mankind is the application of Christ's gospel of love. The world must look to Christ. If we rely, as in the past, upon reciprocity — hatred for hatred, greed for greed, inhumanity for inhumanity — where will it lead but to endless misery? Somewhere the chain must be broken. Somewhere good must be returned for evil, love for hatred, confidence for mistrust, and sympathetic understanding for prejudice. And this is the essence of Christ's gospel — to break the chain of the past and start a new series of events where love will produce more love until the earth is redeemed.

The other teaching of love relates to our narrow loyalties: "For if ye love them which love you, what reward have ye? Do not even the publicans the same?" ( Matt. 5:46) We naturally love those who love us. But the publicans and sinners do as well. We must go beyond it. We must extend our sympathies so that we appreciate the lives of people beyond our family, our group, our race, our nationality. This challenge is expressed by Reinhold Niebuhr:

Isn't it a fact that most of us are anti-social in conduct, not purely because we are selfish, but because we are selfish for our group? We hide our selfishness behind our unselfishness; it is because we seek advantages for our children, for our family, for our race or our nation, for our class, that we are as selfish as we are, and it is for that reason that Jesus' terrible strictures against the family are in an ultimate sense justified: "Who serves father and mother more than me is not worthy of me." You have here again, an insistence that the love ideal must be made absolute and universal against the forces of history and against the impulses of nature, a suggestion which, if followed to the ultimate conclusion, will issue in an unnatural life- but suggestion, nevertheless, which is continually necessary to save you from the group selfishness which common sense creates. [^3]

Dear Brethren: It is a duty which every Saint ought to render to his brethren freely — to always love them, and ever succor them. To be justified before God we must love one another: we must overcome evil; we must visit the fatherless and the widow in their affliction, and we must keep ourselves unspotted from the world: for such virtues flow from the great fountain of pure religion, strengthening our faith by adding every good quality that adorns the children of the blessed Jesus, we can pray in the season of prayer; we can love our neighbor as ourselves, and be faithful in tribulation, knowing that the reward of such is greater in the kingdom of heaven. What a consolation! What a joy! Let me live the life of the righteous, and let my reward be like his! [^5]
