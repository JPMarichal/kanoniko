Probing into the hinterland of our motives can be a disturbing experience. Some of the basic human drives have unpleasant names such as vanity, rivalry, love of power, etc. Little wonder, therefore, that one of the greatest, if not the greatest of Christian virtues, is that of humility. Self-confidence and self-esteem have their place. We need confidence to go forward with our work, and high self-esteem to resist temptation. But we also instinctively admire the spirit of a modest and humble man. President George Albert Smith said, "I would avoid the publicity of high positions and discourage the flattery of thoughtless friends." [^1]

Humility is defined as a "state or quality of being humble in spirit; freedom from pride and arrogance." President McKay writes: Modesty is a shining light; it prepares the mind to receive knowledge, and the heart for truth. Humility is the solid foundation of all the virtues." [^2]The late Dr. Milton Bennion, former General Superintendent of our Sunday Schools, defines humility as "a desire to learn by individual study, prayer and divine guidance, and a willingness to learn from others. In the very nature of things, no mortal can have a monopoly on knowledge."

Jesus often speaks of humility as the distinctive mark of His followers. It is one of the greatest, if not the greatest of Christian virtues. Why?

Because the opposite of humility is pride. Pride comes between man and God, and between man and his fellow men.

A proud man loses all sense of the relation in which he stands to God. He puts himself in the place of God. His basis of thought and action proceed from the wrong center.

With such pride the basis of action becomes "my will," not "Thine," be done. A follower of Christ is to feel that God is over him and therefore there is a standard of perfection that can never be attained. He lives in the presence of God whose ideals are infinite and the source of all that is worthy in us.

And every virtue we possess, And every victory won, And every thought of holiness Are His alone. [^3]

-Unknown

A man who is humble toward God will cease to be arrogant among his fellow men. Measured by God's standard he will see that any superiority he may claim is so slight that it is not worth mentioning.

A proud man cuts himself off from others. Feeling thankful he is "not as other men areÂ�even as this publican" ( Luke 18:11), he becomes blind to his own condition and loses sympathy for his fellow men. In God there is no frontier to love, and the love Jesus taught is likewise to be boundless and therefore possible only to the humble. He asked of us a deep, inward, and positive humility that would break down our self-satisfaction and likewise the barriers between man and man, and between God and man. Jesus did not like the forwardness and personal advertisement He saw in the Pharisees. They did their deeds to be seen of men, made their phylacteries ostentatiously broad and their tassels long, loved places of honor at feasts, the best seats in the synagogues, and salutations in the market place ( Matt. 23:5-7); and worst of all, they thought of themselves as"Â�just persons which need no repentance." ( Luke 15:7)

The ideal of humility in our lives may be considered in two parts: (1) Personal and (2) Social.

A . Humility gives us a proper perspective in the vastness of God's creation. It has been said that there are few possessions a man prizes so much as some claim, or claims, whereby he holds himself superior to others. Self-esteem has its place, yet who would deny, in a rare moment of honest humility, the statement of George Elliot: "The best of us are but poor wretches just saved from shipwreck?"

Brooding over the whole of our fragile mortality, William Knox wrote the following poem which is known to have been a favorite with Abraham Lincoln:

Oh why should the spirit of mortal be proud? Like a swift-flitting meteor, a fast-flying cloud, A flash of the lightning, a break of the wave, Man passeth from life to his rest in the grave.

The maid on whose cheek, on whose brow, in whose eye, Shone beauty and pleasure-her triumphs are by; And alike from the minds of the living erased And the memories of mortals who loved her and praised.

The hand of the king, that the scepter hath borne; The brow of the priest, that the miter hath worn; The eye of the sage, and the heart of the brave, — Are hidden and lost in the depths of the grave.

The peasant whose lot was to sow and to reap; The herdsman, who climbed with his goats up the steep; The beggar, who wandered in search of his bread, — Have faded away like the grass that we tread.

The saint who enjoyed the communion of heaven, The sinner who dared to remain unforgiven; The wise and the foolish, the guilty and just, Have quietly mingled their bones in the dust.

Yea! hope and despondency, pleasure and pain, Are mingled together in sunshine and rain; And the smile and the tear, the song and the dirge, Still follow each other like surge upon surge.

'Tis the wink of eye, 'tis the draught of a breath, From the blossom of health to the paleness of death, From the gilded saloon to the bier and the shroud — Oh, why should the spirit of mortal be proud? [^4]

-William Knox

Thomas A. Edison expressed a sufficient code of humility about his own field. In 1921 he wrote: "We don't know the millionth part of one percent about anything. We don't know what water is. We don't know what light isÂ�We don't know what enables us to keep our feet when we stand up. We don't know what electricity is. We don't know what heat is. We don't know anything about magnetism. We have a lot of hypotheses about these things, but that is all. But we do not let our ignorance about these things deprive us of their use." [^5]

And if, as the great inventor said, "we do not let our ignorance deprive us of their use," so we do not let ignorance of the principles of the Gospel deprive us of their use in our personal growth, as in prayer, the sacrament, and every opportunity for service whereby we find the highest for our self-realization.

The vastness of God's creation should ever keep us humble. The new telescope at Mt. Palomar enables man to photograph planets over one billion light years away. This distance in miles amounts to a total of 186,000 (miles per second) x 60 seconds x 60 minutes x 24 hours x 365 days x 1,000,000,000 (years). How many billions of planets there are, no one can guess.

One astronomer, when asked how he could believe in a God, replied, "I keep enlarging my idea of God." Someone has tried to orient our place in God's creation thus: "In the visible world the Milky Way is a tiny fragment. Within this fragment the solar system is an infinitesimal speck, and of the speck, our planet is a microscopic dot. [^6]

"The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament sheweth his handy-work." ( Ps. 19:1)

B. The practical value of personal humility is obvious: It lies in the capacity to heed a failure and humbly recognize the lesson from that failure. Here is the difference between those who move ahead, in spite of mistakes and disappointments, and those who do not. No rule of progress surpasses this: If you would move forward, in business, in science, in any profession or labor — keep humble. Learn quickly by your mistakes and failures.

The test of humility, however, comes when a man is making a great success. He is then tempted to forget that all his power and achievements are simply an entrustment from God. Men deserve the success they win, but after winning it if they fail on a more important test they do not deserve to keep the success they have won.

C. Humility is a prized quality in the development of character itself. In one sense, humility is but one form of a lofty self-respect. He who would grow in the stature of noble character must ever be sensitive to temptations and his own weakness. Being mindful of shortcomings is a necessary condition of personal progress. Moreover, a person who would realize his own highest capacities will easily appreciate that self-esteem and self-conceit are opposites. As someone has expressed it: "The real reason why so many people think too much of themselves is that they do not think enough of themselves." They lack humility necessary to personal growth. They are too easily satisfied. Great achievements come only from those with the loftiest ideals who are, therefore, ever humble before goals unattainable.

Humility can help us in what someone has called the game of life:

Our business in life is not to get ahead of other people, but to get ahead of ourselves. To break our own record, to outstrip our yesterdays by todays, to bear our trials more beautifully than we ever dreamed we could, to whip the tempter inside and out as we never whipped him before, to give as we have never given, to do our work with more force and a finer finish than ever — this is the true idea — to get ahead of ourselves. To beat someone else in a game, or to be beaten, may mean much or little. To beat our own game means a great deal. Whether we win or not, we are playing better than we ever did before, and that's the point after all — to play a better game of life. [^7]

D . Humility is a condition for service. The proud man loses sympathy with his fellow men, and so cannot help them or serve them. How often in a community does wealth, or learning, or social standing create exclusiveness and unbrotherliness?

Pride is a fearful barrier between men. It takes many forms: the white man's conceit in the paleness of his skin, the self-righteousness of the church-goer, the condescension of the scholar, the haughtiness of wealth, the cruelty of social climbers-these are the sins which only a love born of humility can ultimately overcome.

He who would lead must first himself be led; Who would be loved be capable to love Beyond the utmost he receives, who claims The rod of power must first have bowed And being honored, honor what's above: This know the men who leave the world their names. [^8] -Bayard Taylor

2 . Social humility is the essence of democracy. Not one ruler, and not a few rulers, but all the people are to be trusted to rule themselves. The result is a continuous re-examination of goals and purposes, and new experiments in the means to achieve those purposes. Therefore, only the humble can be trusted to govern in a democracy, where the solution of each problem creates newer problems, and rulers hold their positions tentatively and humbly in facing these ever-new situations. In our present world, where the single issue is human freedom, one gains clearer perspective on the importance of humility.

No organization in the world relies so heavily on the ideal of humility as taught by Christ, as does the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. This is so because the Church calls for universal participation — every member with an assignment.

The result is two-fold: First, a man who walks humbly in life with few worldly honors, may preside and administer in Church affairs. Second, a man with many honors and position in the community may do a very humble service in the Church.

Which is the easier, to find one's self elevated from the humble ranks of life to Church administration, yet bear it with deep and genuine humility? Or to be a leader in worldly affairs, yet submit gladly and humbly to the leadership of others less prominent in community achievements?

Thus, the Church relies heavily upon humility: that learning, wealth, power, and social standing will not ruin men by a false pride and thus separate them from other faithful yet less influential members. But even more: it relies on deep, genuine humility to reside in the hearts of those who lead, that they follow the parable of Christ and never be thankful "that I am not as other men areÂ�even as this publican."
