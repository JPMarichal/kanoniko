The words of Christ, "take therefore no thought for the morrow; for the morrow shall take thought for the things of itself," appear as impractical to our busy lives. But there are deeper meanings in this verse, and the following are two of them:

1 . The verse immediately preceding reads: "But seek ye first the kingdom of God, and his righteousness; and all these things shall be added unto you." ( Matt. 6:33)

To put the kingdom of God and His righteousness first, does not mean one neglects health, and business, and relaxation, and home. Rather, all these important phases of life are made better by being subject to the kingdom of God. Indeed the kingdom of God on earth is made up in part of a wonderful family life, a business where men make manhood as well as money, recreation where the spirit is renewed in fine fellowship, health so one can serve, and worship where the soul is restored through "the peace of God, which passeth all understanding."

But in any scale of values, only one can come first, and Christ asks that this be the cause of God on earth, so that we sacrifice for it when necessary, and subordinate all other activities in due proportion, taking "no thought for the morrow" if necessary to keep our highest value first on the scale.

2 . "Take no thought for your life, what ye shall eat, or what ye shall put on. Is not life more than meat, and the body than raiment?" This apparently impractical teaching is explained within the verse itself: "Is not life more than meat?" We are all driven by "beyond-the-body-hungers." We need something to live for, as well as just to live — some cause bigger than ourselves to which we give ourselves, some purpose that fills our minds, and our physical hunger loses its pangs. Paul tells what this was for him in the following words, unsurpassed in Christian literature-words in which there is no trace of anxiety: "For I am persuaded that neither death, nor life, nor angels, nor principalities, nor powers, nor things present, nor things to come,

Nor height, nor depth, nor any other creature, shall be able to separate us from the love of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord." ( Rom. 8:38-39)

Strong Son of God, immortal Love, Whom we, that have not seen thy face, By faith, and faith alone, embrace, Believing where we cannot prove;

Thine are these orbs of light and shade; Thou maddest Life in man and brute; Thou maddest Death; and lo, thy foot Is on the skull which thou hast made.

Consider six values in the ideal of serenity:

1 . Serenity is a need in modern living. The value of serenity — calm, poise, inner peace, tranquility, and the unperturbed mind-all these values are made greater by the very tempo of modern life.

The description of "simple living and moderate desires" hardly fits our present-day outlook. Rather we believe in progress and the price of progress will always be instability — competition, uncertainty, insecurity, success, possessions, excitement, risk, self-realization, "name, fame, and accomplishment" — these are words which characterize modern living. But for all their values, they sometimes exact a high price — that of peace of mind that comes from simpler living and contentment that comes from moderation. Most of us are striving for a goal beyond our reach, and these goals, even if won, would turn out to be much doing and getting that never brings lasting satisfaction and genuine peace of mind. So Matthew Arnold writes:

Calm soul of all things! make it mine To feel, amid the city's jar, That there abides a peace of thine, Man did not make, and cannot mar!

The will to neither strive or cry, The power to feel with others give! Calm, calm me more! nor let me die Before I have begun to live. [^3]

A man can lose his soul under the pressure and preoccupation of busily trying to realize all his ambitions. In the spiritual life described by Jesus in the scriptures above, the unhurried atmosphere of flowers and birds was used to illustrate an unworried life for his disciples — to live day by day, "borrowing no trouble from tomorrow, doing one's best and leaving the consequences in God's hands" — this was the saying of Christ to the overtaxed life of our generation.

Drop Thy still dews of quietness, Till all our strivings cease; Take from our souls the strain and stress, And let our ordered lives confess The beauty of Thy peace.

Breathe through the heats of our desire Thy coolness and Thy balm; Let sense be dumb, let flesh retire; Speak through the earthquake, wind and fire O still small voice of calm! [^4]

- John Greenleaf Whittier

2 . Serenity is a reconciliation with the world.

There is a serenity that comes with the contemplation of our universe, its vastness, its orderliness, and the impartiality in which our lives are set. When Carlyle was told of how a young lady had exclaimed in a happy, heroic manner: "I accept the universe," he replied: "She'd better!" William James held that mortality and religion were based finally on the manner in which we accept the universe — in rebellion for its seeming injustice? — in resignation with stunned submission? — or with reconciliation that whatsoever else is true of it, good can be found that far outweighs the evil?

The dignity of man is enhanced by this courageous and honest effort to find out the nature of the world and live by what is found. It cannot fail to bring tranquility and peace of mind, as the great Spinoza so nobly testified.

Oliver Wendell Holmes wrote:

The great division between human beings, is into the if's and the as'es. If it were, if it might be, if it could be, if it had been. One portion of mankind goes through life always regretting, always whining, always imaginingÂ�

As it is — this is the way the other class of people look at the conditions in which they find themselves. They may be optimists or pessimists — they are very largely optimists — but, taking things just as they find them, they adjust the facts to their wishes if they can; and if they cannot, then they adjust themselves to the facts. [^6]

The peace of mind in accepting the universe as God's creation is expressed in these lines:

3 . Serenity comes with a full acceptance of ourselves.

There comes a time in every man's life when to some degree he must "lay down the flag of revolt and learn a large, serene, and wise resignation." Not only must the universe be accepted, but we have the more difficult task of accepting ourselves. There is an inevitability about the laws of our world, and there is considerable inevitability about ourselves. Sometimes wisdom leaves no alternative: we must adjust our first-rate ambitions down to our second-rate abilities, despite the exhortation that "he can who thinks he can." Tension and strain, not peace, is the lot of those who will not say, as one put it: "I, John Smith, hereby accept myself with my inherited endowments and handicaps and with the elements in my environment that I cannot alter or control, and, so accepting myself as my stint, I will see what I can do with this John Smith." [^8]

Added to this clash between our ambitions and abilities, there are other enemies that spoil our inner peace of mind and sometimes banish happiness — the clash between our desires and ambitions themselves — trying to go north and south at the same time; and if that were not enough, there is the clash between our powerful urges — pugnacity, sex, selfishness — between these and the conventions of society.

No wonder that of 275 college men and women, "over 90% of them suffered from having frustrated feelings of deficiency. The areas of their conscious inferiority were manifold — physical incompetence, ill health, unpleasant appearance, lack of social charm, failure in love, low-grade intellectual ability, moral failure, and guilt. To say that this disheartening sense of being inferior springs from inability to meet the demands of society is only part of the truth. The social demands strike inward; they conjure up an imagined self-competent, adequate, superior; the final tussle is not so much between the individual and the demands of society, as within the individual, between his dreamed-of-self and the self he thinks he actually is." [^9]

Ralph Waldo Emerson wrote: "There is a time in every man's education when he arrives at the conviction that envy is ignorance; that imitation is suicide; that he must take himself for better, for worse, as his portion; that though the wide universe is full of good, no kernel of nourishing corn can come to him but through his toil bestowed on that plot of ground which is given to him to till." [^10]

Author Christopher Benson drew up this description perhaps of some of us:

The truth is that most of us who have any ambitions at all, do not start in life with a hope of being useful, but rather with an intention of being ornamental. We want to be impressive, rich, beautiful, influential, admired, envied; and then, as we move forward, the visions fade. And then we make further discoveries; that influence comes mostly to people who do not pursue it, and that the best kind of influence belongs to those who do not even know that they possess it. [^11]

A period of prolonged disappointing failures from one's own inabilities will take enough tranquility from life; but add to these the "fell blow of circumstance" we did not create, and the victory of inner peace takes on its true proportion. Here is the story of one whose novels filled our youth with joy and excitement:

Sir Walter Scott's early life was exceedingly prosperous. He was an eminent poet, his novels were successful in an unprecedented way, his name was a word of mystery and charm over all Europe, he built Abbotsford, grew wealthy, and was knighted by the king. Then the storm came. First his publishers failed, involving him in debt of 130,000; then his wife died; then his health broke. He described himself as "at sea in the dark and vessel leaky, I think, into the bargain." To one who knows Sir Walter's bravery during those years of failure, his refusal to take advantage of a legal technicality to escape his debt, his patience, good cheer, unshaken faith in God, and undiscourageable work, the deep quality of his character is revealed then more than in his days of achievement. [^13]

4 . Serenity comes from a knowledge of human nature. To live at peace with others is possible to those who will study human nature; who know and understand the sources of human motivations, and the inevitability of environmental factors that make people act as they do. There is peace and calm that comes to those who seek for causes and explanations. This use of the intellect in finding the reasons why people act as they do, sometimes necessarily so, takes away the emotional upsets we feel toward them. They are acting largely as they must act, considering their character pattern — their experience or environment — or both.

Albert Schweitzer has appraised one condition for a calm life. He said: "Anyone who expects to do good must not expect people to roll stones out of his way but must accept his lot calmly even if they roll a few more into it." [^14]

Finally, serenity comes when we take the long view of others' lives, a long perspective of human nature as did Christ himself, "neither do I condemn thee."

In men whom men condemn as ill I find so much of goodness still, In men whom men pronounce divine I find so much of sin and blot, I do not dare to draw a line Between the two, where God has not. [^15]

-Joaquin Miller

I told Him that this war was His, and our cause His cause, but we could not stand another Fredericksburg or Chancellorsville. The feeling that God had taken the whole business into His own hands, and that things would go right at Gettysburg, and that is why I had no fears about you."

Only the superficial or arrogant would attempt to brush such experiences away as being illusory or coincidental. [^16]
