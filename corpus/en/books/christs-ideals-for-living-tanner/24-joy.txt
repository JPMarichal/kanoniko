Religion produces goodness, public and private, for which the world has always stood in desperate need. But there is a particular kind of goodness, especially characteristic of the Latter-day Saint faith, which is goodness mixed with joy.

Some goodness is repressive, constraining, and imprisoning to the mind and spirit. Various forms of Christianity have produced all too much of this negative goodness-prohibitions, denials, vows, and fears that cast a cloud over man's spirit.

Hugh Elmer Brown sees the truer side of Christianity:

Christianity is not the religion of sorrow and gloom; it is the religion of the morning, and carries in its heart the happiness of heaven.

Christianity is not a restraint but an inspiration — not a weight but wings; not subtraction but addition.

Christianity brings bloom for faded hearts, rejuvenation for the prematurely old, imagination for the dry, literal mind.

Christianity is not a kill-joy at the feast of life, nor a kind of incarnate "Don't"; it bristles with great affirmatives, fires the soul with permanent enthusiasms and durable loyaltiesÂ�

Christianity brings zest and sparkle to life; it is sunshine on the flowers rather than moonshine on the snow; it is life more abundant; it is leaving the little narrow life behind and leaving it forever. [^1]

The Restored Gospel has a freshness that is liberating and buoyant. It is positive and forward-looking. It offers a deep joy that comes with purpose and destiny. It is life — abundant life — here, and now, and for the future. Brigham Young put it in words like bullets: "What are we here for? To learn to enjoy more, and to increase in knowledge and in experience." [^2]The key to this deeper joy is clear: God has spoken again. He leads the Church. The teachings of Christ are restored in their eternal simplicity.

Consider the meaning of life with God as expressed in this great hymn:

We'll sing of His goodness and mercy. Then on to eternal perfection We'll praise Him by day and by night, The honest and faithful will go, Rejoice in His glorious Gospel, While they who reject this glad message And bask in its life-giving light. Shall never such happiness know. [^3]

This philosophy, that life can lead "on to eternal perfection," is explained by Dr. John A. Widtsoe:

The determining law of the gospel is the possibility of eternal progresionÂ�Progress means a moving forward from place to place, from knowledge to knowledge, from action to action. It is a process of adding to that which we now possess, by the elimination of errors, by the actual accretion of new truth and by the development of greater self-mastery. It is a process by which increased power of every faculty is gained. It is a process of growth and development, a movement towards greater maturity. It is a steady approach to the likeness of God. [^4]

There is real depth in the joy of a faith that stands by man through life's disappointments and tragedies. It is the faith of the Church expressed in another great hymn:

Now let us rejoice in the day of salvation. No longer as strangers on earth need we roam. Good tidings are sounding to us and each nation, And shortly the hour of redemption will come, When all that was promised, the Saints will be given, And none will molest them from morn until even, And earth will appear as the garden of Eden, And Jesus will say to all Israel, Come Home. The centuries of Christianity pretty well buried the true personality of Christ. He did not turn His back upon the world. He loved life and wanted men to be happy. He had a cheerful temperament. The Gospel, He said in effect, is like an invitation to a royal banquet. (See Matt. 22:2) He loved children and became indignant when His disciples tried to keep them away. (See Mark 10:13-16) He loved nature and said that Solomon in all his glory was not arrayed like the beauty of flowers on the hills of Galilee. ( Matt. 6:29) When a single sinner repented the angels in heaven rejoiced. ( Luke 15:7, 10) He was exuberant and joyful. He wanted the sabbath to serve man's needs, and of fasting He said, "Moreover, when ye fast, be not as the hypocrites, of sad countenance." Nor could this dynamic, exuberant spirit be submerged by disappointments and sorrow.

In this respect He is the master of all men — no sorrow was so deep that His spirit does not rise above it. Even at the end, in the upper chamber with a terrible tomorrow, He said to His disciples: "Be of good cheer, I have overcome the world." Again, "My joy I give unto you, that your joy no man taketh from you." On one occasion He told them that when they were persecuted they should "rejoice and be exceedingly glad." Such an unconquerable spirit has sources of joy that are not at the mercy of men, nor at the mercy of adverse circumstances. For surely no one faced so many adversities and disappointments. Indeed, His joy is meaningful because of so many sorrows in His own life — loneliness, the hostility of His own people, the slowness of His followers to understand, the shadow of the cross that grew darker each day — yet a radiant cheer and confidence that rose above all His troubles. In all history, here was a spirit unmatched in radiance and gladness, victorious over all that men and circumstances could do to Him.

This joy He gave to His followers, a kind of spiritual triumph. It is expressed by Paul, unmatched perhaps in all Christian literature, for its depth of thought and clarity of language:

For which cause we faint not; but though our outward man perish, yet the inward man is renewed day by day.

For our light affliction, which is but for a moment, worketh for us a far more exceeding and eternal weight of glory;

While we look not at the things which are seen, but at the things which are not seen: for the things which are seen are temporal; but the things which are not seen are eternal.

For we know that, if our earthly house of this tabernacle were dissolved, we have a building of God, a house not made with hands, eternal, in the heavens. ( See II Corinthians 4: 16-18; 5: 1 )

The two aspects of life are, first, our environment and those elements in it which we cannot change. The second aspect is our response to those facts — this we can change. There is a hard reality in the "out there" which we may not change for the asking. There is an inner response to the "out there" about which we can have something to say. The Gospel calls this response side of our lives, free agency. In a game, it can be a response like one written over the exit of a university's field house: "Sixty minutes of action, no alibis, no regrets"; or it can be a response of regret that rankles and endures, spoiling happier moments with vain regrets.

In the game that is played for keeps — real life love, temper, glory, failure, guilt — one cannot dismiss these so easily with "no alibis, no regrets." Finally, there is illness and old age. And from all life's joys and sorrows we gain insight or bitterness, wisdom or rebellion, understanding or frustration, reconciliation or despair.

Why fear to-morrow, timid heart? Why tread the future's way? We only need to do our part To-day, dear child, today. The past is written! Close the book On pages sad and gay;

'Tis this one hour that God has given; His Now we must obey; And it will make our earth His heaven To live to-day-to-day. [^6]

Jesus gave us a key to joyous living, to live above the hurts of men and adverse circumstances. "I have overcome the world," He said. And our ultimate joy rests also upon this achievement.

We are not compelled to live with gloom, fear, and doubt. We can look on the sunny side, and walk there too.

Sir Walter Scott wrote: "One hour of life, crowded to the full with glorious action, and filled with noble risks, is worth whole years of those mean observances of paltry decorum, in which men steal through existence, like sluggish waters through a marsh, without either honour or observation."
