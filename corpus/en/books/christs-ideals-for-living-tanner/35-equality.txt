The ideal of equality is that all men should have an equal opportunity to become all that they are capable of being. This is the ideal expressed in the constitution, "that all men are created equal" — not equal in ability, for everyone knows that men are not equal in capacity. Some have greater intellectual powers, some have greater spiritual capacity, and some have greater practical ability. Inequality would seem to be the basis of creation, applicable in all realms — physical, intellectual, and spiritual. And yet our civilization is founded on the ideal that "all men are created equal" before the law, equal in the chance for an education, equal in the right to leisure, equal in opportunity for economic advancement, and equal in opportunity for leadership in fields of activity. This ideal of equality of opportunity is the essence of our democracy.

Where did it come from? Democracy has many tributaries, but the well-springs of its principal source will be found in Jesus of Nazareth. Our civilization is referred to as Graeco-Christian, and the deepest roots are those which began in Palestine — that personality is sacred because it is divine, illustrated in the well-known scripture, that in helping "even the least of theseÂ�ye have done it unto me," and to hurt "even the least" is to offend God.

Woodrow Wilson wrote: "The beauty of democracy is that you never can tell when a youngster is born what he is going to do with you, and that, no matter how humbly he is bornÂ�he has got a chance to master the minds and lead the imaginations of the whole country." [^1]

The heart of democracy is the belief in the infinite worth of the individual. Such an appraisal came primarily from Jesus — his life, parables, and sermons. Charles Kingsley wrote:

Let us try to follow the Savior's steps; let us remember all day long what it is to be men; that it is to have everyone whom we meet for our brother in the sight of God; that it is this, never to meet anyone, however bad he may be, for whom we cannot say, "Christ died for that man, and Christ cares for him still. He is precious in God's eyes, and he shall be precious in mine also.'' [^2]

Latter-day Saint scriptures are replete with references to the ideal of equality, not only equality of opportunity for education and economic advancement, but equality within the Church itself — every man who holds the Priesthood does so without distinction of learning or community standing, or wealth, or preference of any kind.

In the Palestine of Christ's time, caste lines were drawn sharply on the basis of religion, wealth, learning, etc., but Jesus ignored them all. He ate with Simon, a Pharisee of distinction, and with despised publicans and sinners. He was a friend of the poor and His heart was ever with them; but He loved the rich, and of one rich young ruler who refused discipleship, the scripture relates: "And Jesus looking upon him, loved him." At a time when women were not emancipated, He associated with them on equal terms-Mary and Martha, Magdalene, Joanna, Susanna, the widow of Nain, Salome, and many others. "Daughters of Jerusalem," He said on His way to Calvary, "do not weep for me, but weep for yourselves and for your children." Jesus associated with children and with men of guilty conscience, grafters like Zacchaeus. He knew and attracted the hated soldiers of Rome, one a captain of whom Christ spoke highly, "I have not found so great faith, no, not in Israel."

It is no wonder that He was finally rejected. In His life, Jesus ignored all the barriers of that day — racial, economic, social, and religious. He loved people for themselves, one by one; rich and poor, black and white, ignorant and learned, the powerful and the disinherited, the children and the soldiers, women and foreigners — all were equally precious to Him as sons and daughters of God. This was an individualism coupled with a universalism in Christ's gospel that the world has a long way to catch up with.

The individualism of Christ's gospel, that each and every person is of infinite worth; and the universalism in His gospel that includes every and all persons on the earth — these two ideals are supported on every page of the Gospels. Writing of the clash between Jesus and the Judaism of that day, Klausner, a Jewish scholar, summarized:

A religion which possesses only a certain conception of God and a morality acceptable to all mankind, does not belong to any special nation, and, consciously or unconsciously, breaks down the barriers of nationality. This inevitably brought it to pass that his people, Israel, rejected him. [^4]

Harnach, another Jewish New Testament scholar writes: "Jesus Christ was the first to bring the value of every human soul to light."

Such is the foundation of our democracy, that if given an opportunity, great leaders and fine citizens may come from minorities, from unsuspected places, unfortunate backgrounds; if only there is equality of opportunity.

The ideal of equality is not to be a standard of what is common or mediocre. Equality of opportunity is the chance to be uncommon, above average, a leader, "That nation," wrote Sinclair Lewis, "is proudest and noblest and most exalted which has the greatest number of really great men." Latter-day Saints emphasize leadership and have made records unsurpassed in this attainment. However, the Gospel is love, and love must take in every son and daughter of God — the weak and the poor, as well as those of leadership. The issue is clear: Since men are unequal, strong and weak, the strong must help the weak. That is the religion of Christ. Paul expressed it exactly: "We then that are strong ought to bear the infirmities of the weak, and not to please ourselves." ( Rom. 15:1) The Gospel is not a doctrine of survival of the fittest, nor a Nietzchean goal of supermen. On the contrary, it is a doctrine that the strongest should be at the service of the weakest. It is a philosophy summed up in these words: "Â�though he was rich, yet for your sakes he became poor, that ye through his poverty might be rich." ( 2 Cor. 8:9) It follows that a culture which gives men the freedom to be strong in private initiative and leadership, and also have a testimony of Christ and His teaching — these are the truly strong who are most capable of helping the less fortunate.

And this is the Church Welfare Program — outstanding leadership of quality and ability to "bear the infirmities of the weak." At its best this means, not just immediate food to those in a crisis of need, but long-term planning and teaching that attacks the causes of human misery at the roots.

This is one of the accepted missions of the Church — not simply aid to present needy, but to teach against the sins of greed and jealousy that cause one man to exploit another. It asks that the golden rule be applied in the face of business temptations — not alone fairness in profit-taking, but fairness in giving a day's work. The Church is Christ's kingdom, whose work is the establishment of righteousness in personal character and social relations. Whatever hurts personality in business, in education, in government — in every walk of life — that is the business of the Church. The Church is the champion of each individual whose life is abused by another, or by any system, or by any institution on earth. An important part of its work in the community is to liberate and enrich the lives of its own members, and equally those who are not members, by giving encouragement and help, both practical and spiritual, to advance "the acceptable year of our Lord." This means to treat all human beings as brothers and children of our Father in Heaven.

The second great commandment was equal to the first, the Master said.
