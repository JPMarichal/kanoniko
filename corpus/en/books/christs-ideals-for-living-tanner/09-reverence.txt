Reverence

"Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy strength, and with all thy mind; and thy neighbor as thyself."

The ideal of reverence is defined as a sense of the sacred, a "profound respect, mingled with love and aweÂ�The state of being revered or entitled to reverence," "a recognition of transcendent goodness."

We might further describe it as a hidden shrine in one's life, concerning which there is a profound sense of the sacred, a sanctity so deeply felt that to violate it is sacrilege.

Another way to express this meaning of reverence is to say that "it is identical with devotion to God."

In the history of man there have been many forms of misplaced reverence which amounted to superstition; the awe felt for nature objects: mountains, rivers, trees, the sun, sky, and countless other objects. Scientific explanations have removed the sense of the mysterious and awe men once had for objects and events they could not understand. In the quest for truth and understanding, truth itself becomes an object of reverence. John Locke said, "After all, the greatest reverence is due the truth." And Kant held that no subject, including religion, could claim respect unless it stood the test of free and careful examination. Even so, it is probably true, as Rauhin has stated, that "to veil some things is to ennoble them, and in literature and life we may desecrate truth and love by too familiar handling of them."

The relationship of reverence to love was expressed by Newman: "No one really loves another, who does not feel a certain reverence toward him." And Dante said that, "Love intends the welfare mainly of the thing it loves."

Reverence has been referred to as "a sure index to the moral value of any society." Latter-day Saints would accept this index. Man is made in the image of God and should aspire to a God-like character. Our bodies are to be reverenced as temples of the spirit. And the spirit of man is God's creation, infinitely sacred, with illimitable possibilities. It is sacrilege, therefore, to hurt another personality, and it is God's service to protect another from all that may hurt, crush, or profane human life. Our reverence for the sacred in life is great or small as we are able to see what is sacred. In one of the finest insights concerning the sacred in life, Dr. Fosdick writes:

To have friends whose lives we can elevate or depress by our influence is sacred. To be entrusted with little children is sacred. To have powers by which we can make this earth a more decent place is sacred. To be a child of God is sacredÂ�Such is the insight by the leverage of which the spiritual seers have lifted men, and by which high-minded parents have trained high-minded children. For when anyone vitally believes that anything is sacred, he will shrink from sacrilege. [^1]

President McKay has said that the deepest meaning of reverence is expressed in the first and second great commandments, quoted at the beginning of this chapter. When one accepts this as the standard of reverence, it has a timelessness that transcends all other forms of reverence. The ideal of reverence as lived and taught by Christ has two hubs toward which all His teachings center: (1) His reverence for God, (2) His reverence for persons.

1 . His reverence for God is inexpressable. No words could begin to explain it. Like the sun in the heavens, it lighted His every thought and action: A familiar description of His activity reads: "And it came to pass in those days, that he went out into a mountain to pray, and continued all night in prayer to God." ( Luke 6: 12)

The following from John's gospel is perhaps unsurpassed in fulness of meaning for those who love the Lord with heart, soul, and mind: "But the hour cometh, and now is, when the true worshipers shall worship the Father in spirit and in truth: for the Father seeketh such to worship him. God is a spirit: and they that worship him must worship him in spirit and in truth." ( John 4:23-24)

2 . Christ's reverence for persons ranks as the highest expression of esteem ever given on this earth. Here lies the clean-cut issue between Christ and a materialistic view of life. For Jesus, human life is infinitely sacred, of infinite value. Life is not reducible to physical atoms; we are not cogs in a big machine, "born of dust, doomed to dust without spiritual origin, spiritual meaning, and spiritual destiny." Against such materialism, Christ said that personality is sacred, divine in nature, spiritual in origin, endless in possibilities, and infinite in worth. This is the sum and substance of the Gospel — the infinite worth of personality. Christ measured all events and His own mission in terms of whether it would add to the abundant life. "God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten sonÂ�"Such a reverence for persons, one by one, is the heart of Christianity and the heart of our democracy, too. We are bad enough with our wars and iniquities and oppressions, but we do strive for better education, better hospitals, better economic justice, and more freedom. At present we are in a desperate struggle for the preservation of man's dignity; the preservation of individuals, of every race and creed, who are children of God and who must not be abused by tyranny. Why? Not the least of the answers, and perhaps the best one, is because long ago the Master spoke a faith we believe: "Inasmuch as ye have done it unto the least of these, my brethren, ye have done it unto me."

In addition to Christ's reverence for God and his reverence for man, examine the ideal of reverence in our lives with regard to (1) Truth, (2) Beauty, and (3) Christ.

1 . Reverence for truth is the key to so many things: a free spirit of inquiry is the key to progress. It means a free society where no one or group can enforce their particular ideas. It will help to create a free soul whom God loves for integrity. And one great reward is our great Church where free discussion and humility, always with humility, leads to the insights and inspiration of a noble people with clear vision.

Nowhere, perhaps, may one find an expression of more reverent devotion to the ideal of truth than in these words by President Joseph F. Smith, spoken to a graduating class of the Latter-day Saints College on June 5, 1895:

This knowledge of truth, combined with proper regard for it, and its faithful observance, constitutes true education. The mere stuffing of the mind with a knowledge of facts is not education. The mind must not only possess a knowledge of truth, but the soul must revere it, cherish it, love it as a priceless gem; and this human life must be guided and shaped by it in order to fulfill its destiny. The mind should not only be charged with intelligence, but the soul should be filled with admiration and desire for pure intelligence which comes of a knowledge of the truth. The truth can only make him free who hath it, and will continue in it. [^4]

2 . A feeling of reverence for beauty is one of the distinguishing characteristics in a life of quality. Responsiveness to the beautiful-beauty in God's out-of-doors, beauty in worship, in our homes-nothing so quickly reveals a person's character as appreciation that requires fine discrimination. So many cheap things crowd in on our lives and crowd out the beautiful, so that we are urged to go along with the majority — a movie in preference to symphony, cheap magazines in preference to the classics. Thus preoccupied with many tasks and common practices, we lose the power to love the highest when it comes to us. The total result is irreparable injury to our characters, and a warning Jesus once gave, needs our thoughtful attention today: "Â�if thy eye offend theeÂ�if thyÂ�hand offend theeÂ�" (Read Matt. 5:29, 30)

On the positive side there is a joy beyond expression for those with a cultivated sense of reverence for the beautiful.

The following quotation from John Masefield's "Everlasting Mercy" reveals the reverence of both believer and even the unbeliever toward Christ:

In one of the most dramatic scenes in this autobigraphical poem, Miss Bourne, The Quaker, on her nightly mission to the "pubs," came upon Saul Kane half crazed with drink. Even the drunkards had always treated her with respect, but this night Saul was "beside himself." He greeted her with vile taunts, whereupon, grieved in spirit, she went to the bar and, emptying his half-filled tumbler upon the floor, —

"Saul Kane, she said, "when next you drink, Do me the gentleness to think That every drop of drink accursed Makes Christ within you die of thirst, That every dirty word you say Is one more flint upon His way, Another thorn about His head, Another mock by where He tread, Another nail, another cross, All that you are is that Christ's loss." [^5]

If you were going to meet the queen of England today you would make great preparation in anticipation of going into her presence. When Helen Keller and her teacher were invited to meet the king of Belgium, Miss Keller, who I think in many respects is the most distinguished woman in all modern history, seemed somewhat agitated as she stood in the waiting room. Though she had stood before dignitaries before, she sensed a responsibility in meeting His Royal Highness.

What I am saying is that whenever you go into the presence of a dignitary, an earthly dignitary, you make preparation. You know that that is one fundamental reason why we put on our Sunday best to come here to the house of God, to come into His presence. Such preparation is in and of itself a virtue. It has a psychological effect, and that father who, in his eagerness to make money, will take his son out in the field to work on Sunday without giving that son an opportunity to take a rest, change his clothes, and come in cleanliness of body and of mind into the presence of his heavenly Father, deprives his son of a blessing, whether he is in the Church or out of it.

There is a fundamental virtue in coming to the house of worship. One of the fundamental conditions contributing to a person's right thinking and acting is a reverence for God. A growing disbelief among the masses of mankind in a Supreme Being is a principal cause of crime. [^6]
