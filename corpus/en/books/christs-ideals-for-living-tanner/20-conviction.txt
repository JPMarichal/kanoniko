Obert C. Tanner, Christ's Ideals for Living ( 1955)

Conviction

"Thou art the Christ, the Son of the living God."

Religions do not lack for people who are strongly possessed of deep convictions. It is the special genius of religion to generate emotional zeal and strong beliefs. The trouble comes when conviction is made a goal or an end of itself. When this happens, the record of religion becomes one of cruelty and bigotry-of wars both hot and cold.

Latter-day Saints are a people of strong convictions. But they match it with a fundamental principle of the Gospel — progress. The result is truly an amazing accomplishment — conviction with tolerance, cherished beliefs with eagerness to learn, a resolute faith with genuine curiosity, a sense of destiny with a sense of adventure and progress.

Such a faith is true to the gospel of Christ. In Mormonism this Restored Gospel of Christ has produced a people of sacrificial devotion to their convictions and also a people of cultural achievements, some of which are perhaps unmatched in the world. The religious education program of the Church is considered by experts to be the finest in America. The seminaries, institutes, and a great university, the highest percentage of young people in schools, in places of leadership — all this comes from a people in whom a love for truth is deeply ingrained, a tolerance to new ideas, and a certitude that to love the Gospel and live by its standards is to love learning and to seek wisdom and grow in the image of God both now and for all eternity.

In the gospel of Christ both conviction and tolerance are necessary. For if there is tolerance without conviction the result is a sentimentality without direction or achievement. And if there is conviction without tolerance, the result is bigotry and tragedy without measure.

The ideal is to possess both conviction and tolerance — stand for something, yet cultivate the virtues of sympathy and humility. To be eager to learn and to give a sympathetic hearing to all opposition, yet hold basic convictions-this is the only sure path of progress, however painful and halting it may sometimes appear to be. So Tennyson has described it:

Perplext in faith, but pure in deeds, At last he beat his music out. There lives more faith in honest doubt, Believe me, than in half the creeds.

He fought his doubts and gather'd strength, He would not make his judgment blind, He faced the specters of the mind And laid them; thus he came at length.

To find a stronger faith his own, And Power was with him in the night, Which makes the darkness and the light, And dwells not in the light alone. [^1]

Whatever differences that exist among the churches of Christianity, this one common conviction has had its place of great importance to all: "Enter into thine inner chamber, and having shut thy door, pray to thy Father who is in secret." This was basic among the convictions of Christ. He said, "God is love; and he that abideth in love abideth in God, and God abideth in him." This is one of the noblest expressions of religion in all literature.

The religion of Christ was not complicated or involved with the fine points of reasoning. Jesus taught a reverence for personality and said that God is best understood in terms of all that personality involves-a Father in heaven.

Jesus revealed the importance of conviction in the way He spoke of John the Baptist:

And as they departed, Jesus began to say unto the multitudes concerning John, What went ye out into the wilderness to see? A reed shaken with the wind?

But what went ye out for to see? A man clothed in soft raiment? behold, they that wear soft clothing are in kings' houses.

But what went ye out for to see? A prophet? yea, I say unto you, and more than a prophet. ( Matt. 11:7-9)

Meeting the opposition of both the legalistic religious leaders, and the people hardened by sin, Christ was armed with fearless words, unbending endurance, and firm convictions. When He met cruelty and hypocrisy, He was anything but tolerant and sentimental.

The Carpenter was not afraid to die For his convictions. Though he was reviled, He would not stoop to safe conformity. The synagogue, by wanton greed defiled, He cleansed in spite of scribe and Pharisee. To wrong and evil he refused to yield.

It is not an easy matter to determine when one should conform and when one should rebel. Both are extremes, and extremes are dangerous business. But there is a sure guide: It is the gospel of Christ, the gospel of love. Whenever we deviate from love, we part from Christ. When we become slaves to a blind conformity, we are not disciples of Christ, who was far from a conformist himself. And when we are iconoclasts — lacking in appreciation of the past, we miss the Savior's example, for He himself possessed a knowledge of the Old Testament and quoted its profoundest truths throughout His ministry.

Conviction with love explains Christ's courage to fight all the evils He met — the legalistic religion of the Pharisees, the economic injustices that were current, the pride of the privileged in learning or power — these met Christ's opposition just because of His great love for people and His determination to defend them. This is the surpassing virtue of conviction. Great history is never written without men of deep and clear conviction — called by Latter-day Saints "a living testimony." James Freeman Clarke writes:

All the strength and force of man comes from his faith in things unseen. He who believes is strong; he who doubts is weak. Strong convictions precede great actions. The man strongly possessed of an idea is the master of all who are uncertain and wavering. Clear, deep, living convictions rule the world. [^3]

Ralph Waldo Emerson paid this tribute to the ideal of conviction:

To believe your own thought, to believe that what is true for you in your private heart is true for all men — that is genius. Speak your latent conviction, and it shall be the universal sense; for the inmost in due time becomes the outmost, and our first thought is rendered back to us by the trumpets of the last judgment. Familiar as the voice of mind is to each, the highest merit we ascribe to Moses, Plato, and Milton is that they set at naught books and traditions and spoke not what men but what they thought. [^4]

But conviction itself may bring a whole train of evils in its path: Holding unimportant matters to be of equal importance to "the weightier matters of the law"; conviction can be stubborn and shut the mind off from the growth and progress of promising possibilities and rewarding new truth; conviction can be obstinate in its pride of certitude and shut one off from fellowship with his fellow man, and even God.

The quality of the ideal of conviction, like other ideals, is determined by the matters upon which our conviction centers. Unrighteous men may have strong convictions. Men march under the banner of religion with strong convictions but without much real love in their hearts. Someone described these as being "as orthodox as the devil and just as mean."

The center of conviction for Latter-day Saints is Jesus Christ and His revelation of the will of God for our lives in these latter days. Mormonism is not a static religion. Its restoration is continuous, its truths unfold, its vision of God's will expands with the years and by its righteousness it will one day fill the earth — that is the clear and living conviction of Latter-day Saints. Discipleship to such a faith is maintained with every welcome and every wish for God to speed to new ideas, and news ways of work — of faith in "something ever more about to be." It is no wonder a stranger speaks occasionally of the lighter step, a brighter eye, a ready hand, among Mormons — they have a commanding faith of the here and now of life, and the tasks at hand that so unify each and all that there is hardly room for littleness in living nor the paralysis of skeptical doubts in thinking.

Our doubts are traitors, And make us lose the good we oft might win, By fearing to attempt. [^5]

And each one strives for this conviction, not as something to be inherited or transferred, but a conviction that comes from study and reflection and prayer. Christ gives us the clearest picture of what God is like — supreme love and perfect righteousness. He is himself the ideal of a perfect man, the son of God, whose life is the light of the world. But more, a true Latter-day Saint feels a sense of personal responsibility to represent Christ to the world, his spirit, his honor, his character — indeed his reputation. It is no small task to live so that one's life has an inward righteousness that refers to, or reflects from, the life of Christ itself.

One fault to be avoided by the Saints, young and old, is the tendency to live on borrowed light, with their own hidden under a bushel; to permit the savor of their salt of knowledge to be lost; and the light within them to be reflected, rather than original. Every Saint should not only have the light within himself, through the inspiration of the Holy Spirit; but his light should so shine that it may be clearly perceived by others. [^6]

-Joseph F. Smith

-Brigham Young

The guiding principles to the realization of the higher life are not many nor complex. Indeed, they are few and simple, and can be applied by everyone in any phase of life.

The first is a heartfelt recognition of the reality of spiritual values. This brings us into the very realm into which Jesus would lead us for the abundant life. He taught that men and women fail to live truly and really amount to nothing unless they have spirituality. This was the burden of His sermon when the people turned and left Him. And now, I repeat His question: "Will ye also go away?" [^8]

-David O. McKay

Nothing but a sterling desire to do the will of God will cause men to endure the contumely and reproach of their fellowmen and associate themselves with the people denominated Latter-day Saints or "Mormons." [^9]

-John Taylor

But in his zeal to save Jesus from death, he erred, though only in love; and we can fancy hearing one of the others saying, in effect, in commendation of Peter's anxiety,

If he be not one that truly loves you, That errs in ignorance, and not in cunning, I have no judgment in an honest man's face.

At any rate, we know that Jesus was pleased with Peter's testimony, and with his love, and would patiently wait the unfolding of his mind in the comprehension of the gospel plan. [^10]

-David O. McKay
