A remarkable passage in the Book of Mormon gives the basis for true brotherhood:

For none of these iniquities come of the Lord; for he doeth that which is good among the children of men; and he doeth nothing save it be plain unto the children of men; and he inviteth them all to come unto him, black and white, bond and free, male and female; and he remembereth the heathen; and all are alike unto God, both Jew and Gentile. ( 2 Ne. 26:33)

Now that science has made the world one neighborhood, these words of Nephi become of prime importance. Here is expressed the fundamental principle of the Gospel, namely, that God "denieth none that come unto him, black and white, bond and free, male and female; and he remembereth the heathen; and all are alike unto God, both Jew and Gentile."

These words have an urgency for our day. Nothing is so clear as the need for greater inclusiveness of spirit on the part of men everywhere. There is now a practical necessity that demands it. The ideal of brotherhood has always been basic to the Gospel, but presently we are driven to its implementation or we perish. It is well that we have this great ideal, that "black and white, bond and freeÂ�all are alike unto God," for with the threat of atomic war, we are driven now to attempt its realization. An ideal will pull us forward but sometimes we need a practical urgency to give us a push. The urgency at present will propel us toward greater world harmony and unity, or failing this, we plunge into some unspeakable and unprecedented catastrophe.

A growing cultural and spiritual unity of the world, where "all are alike unto God," will require real pioneering. But pioneering is not new to Latter-day Saints. The first step is to overcome the barriers against inclusive good will that exists within our own hearts and minds. President McKay has put this challenge of a broader outlook and a wider sympathy before us in these words:

Instead of condemning too vehemently international and group suspicions, enmities, and greed, let us first eliminate these enemies of peace from our own lives, from our own homes, our own neighborhoods; then, men, "seeing our good works," will supplant their suspicion with confidence. Deceit, chicanery and fraud in ordinary business affairs are breeders of distrust, destroyers of brotherliness and good will. For example, the mechanic or garage employee who exploits the stranger or passing tourist, the hotel guest who defaces costly furniture with lighted cigarets, the merchant who makes excessive profit when opportunity presents itself; the clerk in a store or bank or railway station who cunningly gives out short change — anyone who in daily intercourse seeks to take advantage of another is an enemy of good will and peace. [^1]

The foundation for lasting peace and good will rests finally in our personal attitudes and intellectual outlook. This was expressed by an expert on international affairs when he said, "The greatest obstacle between us and the building of an enduring world peace is the small-scale individual."

It takes a lot of good religion to change a small-scale individual into one of larger proportions. Such a religion was detailed by President McKay as follows:

The spirit of brotherhood prompts service, not conquest; cooperation, not exploitation; it involves fair dealing, not fraud and chicanery. It increases in man a greater appreciation of Christ's value of the individual soul. In short, it makes for a better world; and that, in essence, is the ideal for which your son and a hundred thousand other sons have given their lives in the recent World War. [^2]

Brotherhood is the heart of the Gospel. Jesus makes this clear in parables, sermons, and experiences He had with people. He said the second great commandment was like unto the first, "Â�thou shalt love thy neighbor as thyself." ( Gal. 5:14)

The meaning of "neighbor" is the whole world. Until one feels this deeply, "Â�all alike unto God," he will miss one of Christ's greatest teachings.

Jesus said, "Â�the kingdom of heaven is like unto leaven, which a woman took, and hid in three measures of meal, till the whole was leavened. ( Matt. 13:33) Here the thought is upon the great mass that is leavened — the entire world.

Jesus told the parable of the tares, and when His disciples asked for an interpretation, "He answered and said unto them, He that soweth the good seed is the Son of man;

The field is the world;Â�" (see Matt. 13:37-38) This is the turn in the road to the future: That our limited understanding of "foreigners" and our emotional prejudices be dropped like an old worn-out coat and never be put on again. If Christ has not pulled us up to His ideal, science and common sense and threat of war terrible beyond description should push us a long way — "the field is the world." The future belongs to those who think of the Gospel for all. It does not belong to those with narrow loyalties and restricted sympathies. Barriers we erect between race and class will not hold for believers in the Gospel of Jesus Christ. How could they exist for one God? If monotheism is true, the world is one, and all nations and races and peoples are under one divine sovereignty who is "Â�Lord of heaven and earth,Â�" (Matt. 11:25 ) Paul expressed this fundamental principle in a logical argument: "Is he the God of the Jews only? is he not also of the Gentiles? Yes, of the Gentiles also:

Seeing it is one GodÂ�" ( Rom. 3:29-30)

Paul expressed this ideal of monotheism very much as Nephi, quoted at the beginning of the introduction, "And hath made of one blood all nations of men for to dwell on the face of the earth, and hath determined the times before appointed, and the bounds of their habitation;" ( Acts 17:26)

Speaking to His disciples Jesus said, "And I say unto you, That many shall come from the east and west, and shall sit down with Abraham, and Isaac, and Jacob, in the kingdom of heaven." ( Matt. 8:11) He said to His followers, "Ye are the light of the world." Toward the end of His ministry He declared, "And this gospel of the kingdom shall be preached in all the worldÂ�Go ye therefore, and teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost." ( Matt. 28:19)

Besides this emphasis of Christ, of seeing the world under one "Lord of heaven and earth," there follows the commandments which He gave. These are to apply to all people, regardless of race or national lines, namely, to be humble, pure of heart, peacemakers, forgiving enemies and loving them, generous without display, merciful, free from fear, free from servitude to worldly possessions, sincerity in speech, etc. Every disciple of Christ knows the standard to be applied at the last judgment. It has to do with brotherhood that sees every individual as a child of God, infinitely precious in His sight, and equally worth sacrificing for. Jesus gave the test by which democracy and His Gospel are ever before the judgment: To see possibilities in unsuspecting places, and sacrifice for persons everywhere, even the prodigals, thieves, and beggars. His gospel is for all. Thirty-six times in Matthew's Gospel He used the universal prefix "whoever." "Whoever gives to one of these little ones even a cup of cold water"; "whoever would save his life shall lose it," etc. Jesus intended to include every son and daughter of God as eligible to hear and do the things he commanded.

The demand this mid-twentieth century makes on all of us-like it or not-is that we cannot survive if we are found to be "small-scale individuals." As science makes the earth smaller, we must make ourselves larger-larger in sympathies, larger in knowledge and understanding. Once our people were reasonably safe from interference here in these valleys of the Rockies. Now we are not safe anywhere. The challenge is application of the principles of the Gospel for a new international-mindedness. "The field is the world"; "All are alike unto God"; "Seeing it is one God"; "God hath made of one blood all nations of men for to dwell on the face of the earth"; "Ye are the salt of the earth"; "the light of the world"; "Go ye therefore, and teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and the Son and the Holy Ghost." All this means one thing: Brotherhood — brotherhood or war; the demand is alternative, one or the other, but not both.

Brotherhood is an ideal that begins to grow, like hot-house plants, in the protection of family, the neighborhood, and the community. Then it is transplanted into more rigorous climates — the state, the nation, and the world. It takes imaginative insight and faith in the principles of the Gospel, to transcend one's local environment and extend our sympathies and good will to include all of humanity — which means individuals, each one infinitely precious to God. For this is the sum and substance of the Latter-day Saint religion: God is our Father, we are His children, and therefore, all of us-all-Jew, Gentile, bond and free, are "brethren"; as Nephi stated, "All are alike unto God."
