Mercy may be defined as the attitude of forgiving helpfulness toward one who has done wrong and deserves condemnation and punishment. Strict justice may require punishment, but mercy gives the offender new hope and a new life. Mercy is forgiveness and a mitigation or abolition of punishment.

However, one may be merciful, and yet hostile to sin at the same time. "There is no mercy in allowing a bad man to go on in badness." That is, mercy and judgment are not in conflict if both are founded upon love.

God's mercy is free and unrestrained. It is also absolute. It covers all of life. And this mercy in God requires a merciful spirit in man. To seek God's mercy and refuse it to others is insincere. Moreover, if we lack a merciful spirit we can never appreciate the free and full and unearned mercy of God.

The commandment for our lives is this: "Be ye merciful, even as your Father is merciful." God's mercy is our standard and our inspiration. As such, our mercy is to overflow in fullness and munificence, and limited only by penitence of the offender. We are not merciful because we want to receive mercy, but because we know God is merciful, and being thankful, we try to emulate His example.

The mission of Christ was not to judge and condemn, but to heal and to save. It is true that He had to judge and denounce meanness and sin, but He did not leave the matter there. Rather it was the beginning. He sought out the salvation of each soul by love and understanding, and by mercy freely given and without limitation.

To the woman brought before him, He said, "Neither do I condemn thee: go, and sin no more." To Peter who protested, "Depart from me for I am a sinful man, O Lord," He replied, "Follow me, and I will make you a fisher of men." On the cross He prayed, "Forgive them Father, for they know not what they do."

In each man's situation He looked for all the circumstances. When Mary, needing mercy and forgiveness, "wasted money with expensive ointment, Jesus would not let them upbraid her because He saw the motive of love. "Let her alone," He said. He saw possibilities in prodigals, He attributed ignorance to those who crucified Him, and when His disciples fell asleep and could not watch with Him, He said, "The spirit indeed is willing, but the flesh is weak." ( Matt. 26:41) And even when a young man was not earnest enough to follow Him, He expressed his appreciation, "Then Jesus beholding him loved him." The entire story is immortal, as are these six words of description above.

Mercy as an ideal is appreciated when we understand that judgment by itself, condemnation, is negative. All of us slip easily into the attitude of judges. We simply condemn the wrong, when the need is for redeeming those caught in the meshes of circumstances or bad habits. People do not need a judgment which they could make as well or better on themselves; they need love. Criticism does little but sympathy helps.

It is a terrible risk to judge another, for the judgment should include all the factors of inherited temperament, environmental influences, and even more difficult, the hidden motives known only to God.

If we judge people by their actions, we can err badly, for we are never certain of their motives. Some may be high, and others are low. Some "do their alms to be seen of men." The motive is the test, and a widow's mite would be approved above larger amounts with improper motive. Some are handicapped by physical limitations, some by unequal opportunities, and all by personality differences. Therefore, mercy is the better part of every judgment because so much is unknown by everyone who would be a judge.

Actually, we never judge another by ourselves, but by an image we have formed of ourselves — an image that has all the good qualities we would like to have, and few of the bad qualities we can scarcely believe are true. We condemn others for sins we possess but do not allow ourselves to acknowledge. As Thomas a Kempis wrote: "How seldom we weigh our neighbor in the same balance with ourselves." We weigh him with far less advantage, for he is without the favor of excuses we reserve for ourselves.

The divine attribute of mercy is beautifully expressed in the following quotation from "The Merchant of Venice":

The quality of mercy is not strain'd, It droppeth as the gentle rain from heaven Upon the place beneath: it is twice bless'd; It blesseth him that gives and him that takes: 'Tis mightiest in the mightiest; it becomes The throned monarch better than his crown; His sceptre shows the force of temporal power, The attribute to awe and majesty, Wherein doth sit the dread and fear of kings; But mercy is above this sceptered sway, It is enthroned in the hearts of kings, It is an attribute to God himself, And earthly power doth then show likest God's When mercy seasons justice.
