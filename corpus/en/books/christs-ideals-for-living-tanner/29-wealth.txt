"Â�a man's life consisteth not in the abundance of the things which he possesseth."

Latter-day Saints give freely of time, abilities, and material possessions to the Church and community. A basic philosophy underlies this generosity. It is the acceptance of life, with its talents and possessions as a trust from God, to be used in His service for the benefit of others. God is the owner of all. "The earth is the Lord's, and the fulness thereof; the world, and they that dwell therein." ( Psalms 24: 1 )

Latter-day Saints are a practical people. Granting that man does not live by bread alone, he does live by bread. Work is the law of life. And if "a man's life consisteth not in the abundance of the things which he possesseth," he does need possessions for the welfare of body and soul. Therefore the Church recognizes the right to private property, and teaches that the laggard should not eat the bread earned by the sweat of another's brow.

We have included wealth (great or small) as an ideal because, if properly used, it can bless the owner, his loved ones, and the world about him. Jesus prayed to "give us this day our daily bread." Deep gratitude is expressed for it by Church families before each meal. Our first responsibility is to our family and their physical needs. But every Latter-day Saint is taught the true proportion of spiritual and temporal values. President John Taylor said, "Earthly riches are only little things in comparison with the great principles of eternal lives and exaltation in the Kingdom of God; these are the riches of eternity.'' [^1]Speaking to the graduates of the University of Utah, Elder Adam S. Bennion explained the meaning of "real wealth":

The rains of materialism have put out many of the Lord's candles. Who was it said, "Hold a dime close enough to the eye and it can shut out the world"? Sure you need money. All of us would risk a little more of it. But the quest of it should not rob us of spiritual riches of far greater worth. The young man who balances his life with wisdom and with friends and with kindliness toward others — it is he who comes into real wealth. Name me the half dozen most admired members of this class and you will find in them the answer to a merely materialistic approach to life. Nobody now asks how much Jesus or Lincoln was worth at the bank. [^2]

The goal of life is not to make money, but character-a useful, pure, and worthy character. Beside such character, money, crops, and pleasures are not to be compared.

Yet the Master has need of our practical ability also. The Church Welfare Program calls for it. Such practical ability saves those who give and those who receive, the giver as well as the aged and less fortunate in the hour of need.

One feels that Erasmus found in Sir Thomas More a person able, in spite of his wealth and position, to keep a humble, brotherly, Christian life. Of him he wrote: "Elevation has not elated him or made him forgetful of his humble friends. He is always kind, always generous. Some he helps with money, some with influence. When he can give nothing else, he gives advice. He is Patron-General to all poor devils."

One of the difficult sayings of Christ is the unlikelihood of a rich man to enter the kingdom of God. His judgment about rich men requires some reflection. We all marvel at handicapped men who rise above great adversities. But should we not marvel even more at fortunate men who rise above prosperity? Christ apparently thought so. He pointed out that it was nearly a superhuman task for a rich man to gain spiritual mastery over the success he has won. Making a success will sometimes make men, but having it will sometimes ruin them.

If humility, and willingness to help, and brotherly kindness are the ideals of life, which would have the greater obstacles to overcome: A boy born in a backwoods cabin or one born into wealth?

The problem of the rich young man in the parable above (B) was his financial success. He had gained it but he could not use it well. He "fell over the precipice of his own prosperity." This man is not represented at any time as being dishonest. He was no miser. He could save money, was a wise spender, and may have been a good family man and a good neighbor. In almost any American community, he may have been looked up to as one of the leading citizens. Yet Jesus said he was a fit subject for the title of "fool."

Notice the man's prosperity speech: "What am I going to do, for I have no where to store my crops?" Then he said: "This is what I will do; I will tear down my barns and build larger ones, and in them I will store all my grain and my goods. And I will say to my soul, "Soul, you have great wealth stored up for years to come. Now take your ease; eat, drink, and enjoy yourself." First of all, the man had the wrong idea of what was really his own. He did not see that

There are many hazards to the possession of wealth. One is the loss of sympathy for the poor. People with means find it hard to know where the shoe pinches the less fortunate. They may analyze it, but they do not feel the pinch. It is a terrible risk to rise above the poor and needy; terrible because we may not feel their wrongs and cruel inequities. And failing to feel them, we do nothing to correct them, thereby losing our own souls — lacking in sympathy and humility and our willingness to sacrifice. But more, we could lose our beloved America with all its freedoms, to some terrible judgment of social upheaval brought on by those for whom the pinch of poverty and fear is no longer endurable. Better then, that with whatever privileges and advantages we earn or inherit, we keep our humility and desire for a serviceable life; that we strive for justice for all, and help open wide the doors of equal opportunity for every person and especially for each new generation. Otherwise, we may become like the Pharisees with long prayers while widows were being robbed by excessive rentals. When Jesus said it was difficult for a rich man to gain entrance into the kingdom, He makes us aware that wealth should be carefully used as a trust and with great humility.

The fundamental conclusion about wealth for a Christian is this: Our economic life must "stand under the judgment of Christ" quite as much as any other activity. Evils existing in getting and spending money are no more to be tolerated than evils elsewhere. Great wealth and great poverty, indefensible inequalities of opportunity for education, health and leisure, reckless use of power by management or labor — every phase of the material side of life that oppresses or is harmful, is to be judged by the standard of Christ. That standard is simple enough: Whatever hurts personality is evil, whatever helps personality to fulfill its better possibilities is good. Jesus would look at every economic relation between one man and another — the fairness of a wage or the fullness of a day's work, the justice of claims to special privilege, the fear of poverty, the oppression or the fairness and kindness of overseers — all these and more, would be examined by what they do to people, their individual characters, their chances for a happy life, and judged accordingly.

Such a Christian look at our economic life does not lead to socialism or communism; it leads away from them. Free enterprise survives in proportion to the honest appraisal of its failures and their correction by free men devoted to the good of all, as well as devotion to their own private enterprise.

Speaking to a labor union in 1864, Lincoln said: "Let not him who is houseless pull down the house of another, but let him work diligently and build one for himself, thus by example assuring that his own shall be safe from violence when built.'' [^4]

And on the other side, there is the thought-provoking challenge of Albert W. Palmer, that we keep burning brightly the ideal of sacrificial service in business, quite as much as it burns in science and education. He writes, "Sometimes sitting side by side in this church are two men, friends and relatives. One is a distinguished scientist; the other a successful industrialist. The scientist searches for the secrets of nature that he may bless mankind. He asks only enough that he may live in modest comfort, educate his family, carry on his research, and provide for a frugal and independent old age. His friend, whose genius lies in another direction, expects, as a part of our system, that he shall accumulate a very large fortune in his own right. He looks for no particular acclaim of society, nor is his primary motive to serve society. Now one of these attitudes is Christian; the other — through no fault of the individual — is not Christian. I insist, as I stand near Christ's cross, that we cannot heal the open wounds of the world until the industrial genius is actuated by the same motive as the scientist, the educator.'' [^5]

Â�With God all things are spiritual. There is nothing temporal with him at all, and there ought to be no distinction with us in regard to these matters. Our earthly or temporal existence is merely a continuance of that which is spiritual. Every step we take in the great journey of life, the great journey of eternity, is a step in advance or in retrogression. We are here in mortality, it is true; but we are ahead of that condition we occupied before we came here and took upon us mortality. We are a step in advance of our former state. What is the body without the spirit? It is a lifeless clay. What is it that affects this lifeless clay? It is the spirit, it is the immortal part, the eternal being, that existed before it came here that exists within us, and that will continue to exist, and that by and by will redeem these tabernacles and bring them forth out of the graves. This whole mission of ours is spiritual. The work we have to do here, although we call it temporal, pertains alike to our spiritual and our temporal salvation. And the Lord has just as much right to dictate, to counsel, to direct and guide us in the manipulation and management of our temporal affairs, as we call them, as He has to say one word in relation to our spiritual affairs. So far as He is concerned, there is no difference in this regard. He looks upon us as immortal beings. Our bodies are designed to become eternal and spiritual. [^6]
