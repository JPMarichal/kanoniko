Justice

"Blessed are they which do hunger and thirst after righteousness."

Justice is defined as "what is right or due or fair." That which is generally considered as right or fair is determined both by the law courts and by the general practices and ideals of a people — their feelings or conscience as to what is fair and right. Latter-day Saints believe that God is the author of justice as He is of truth and goodness, and that the Gospel is His revelation of a way that life can be lived justly — a way that is fair and right. God is the author of that which is right and fair. His commandments reveal such a standard, and He likewise helps all the honest in heart to find and live by His justice.

The history of the ideal of justice is interestingly written about by the philosopher James H. Tufts. [^1]He distinguishes seven types of justice that are found in history:

1 . Justice in the early kinship group. Sharing in the products of the hunt,Â�and similar solidarity as to many other good is typical. "Kind"- ness meant what is due one's kin. Such a group or kin loyalty also produced the idea of blood revenge toward other groups for any injury suffered by any member of one's own group.

2 . Justice is an eye-for-an-eye retaliation in kind.

3 . Justice is for each to keep his place or station in life. This favored the aristocracy, and made it more serious for a slave to kill his master than for a master to kill his slave.

4 . Justice is for each to perform his function — a soldier to be a soldier, an artisan to be an artisan, and a guardian to be a guardian — each performing his function in society. This was Plato's ideal of justice as brought out in his book, "The Republic." Essentially, such an ideal of justice is aristocratic and can easily lead to exploitation by the leaders, though Plato tried to exclude this result by allowing all a chance to become a leader.

A study of the ideal of justice in the Gospel finds one of its clearest explanations in the writings of the early prophets, Amos, Micah, Hosea, Isaiah, etc. Here the ideal is honesty in business, "fair weights and balances," impartial justice in the law courts, and as God loves Israel "Â�in righteousness, and in judgment, and in loving kindness, and in mercies." ( Hosea 2: 19), so He expects men to treat each other. In his great and immortal statement, Amos cries out to his people: "But let judgment run down as waters, and righteousness as a mighty stream." ( Amos 5:24)

One can understand the positive ideal of justice when contrasting it with the conditions of injustice that existed at the time of these great prophets of Israel-conditions which existed then and will always exist until men are motivated to live by the eternal standards of justice that are found in nearly every page of the Gospel. Amos saw that wealth in his day had created a great gulf between the rich and the poor. He condemned those who used their wealth to "trample the face" of the poor — refusing honest wages and selling their debtors into slavery. ( Isaiah 3: 14; Amos 2:6; Micah 2:2, 3 :1) He condemned judges for taking bribes ( Amos 6:12; Micah 3:11), and religious leaders for using religion as a cloak to cover their wrong-doing and cruelties. ( Amos 2:7; Isa. 1:11) Perhaps the greatest of all descriptions of a truly religious life was given by Micah, when he gave this threefold principle of a just and righteous life: "Â�To do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God." ( Micah 6:8)

Examples of the ideal of justice in Christ's life are on nearly every page of the Gospels. When the woman accused of adultery was taken before Christ, it was not only their lack of charity for her that aroused his indignation; it was their injustice. They wanted her judged by a standard they were themselves unwilling to be judged by. They would inflict a judgment on her which they would not want visited on themselves. Here is a crucial test of discipleship to Christ: to be just and fair in our judgment of others; to try to understand other peoples' circumstances, their environment, their limitations and the human drives that motivate their actions. To be just in our judgment of others is to rid ourselves of both emotional resentments we may feel and likewise to rid ourselves of unrealistic sentimentality. There is no higher standard of justice than to be willing that the judgment we mete, shall me measured unto us. Jesus said, "Judge not, that ye be not judged.

"For with what judgment ye judge, ye shall be judged: and with what measure ye mete, it shall be measured to you again." ( Matt. 7:1-2)

It is a grave matter to judge others, because we cannot know for sure their motives-whether they are high or low, whether they "do their alms to be seen of men," or whether, like Mary Magdalene, "Â�Her sins, which are many, are forgiven; for she loved much:Â�" ( Luke 7:47)

It is a serious matter to judge another because no two people have exactly the same opportunities. Some have only one talent but by relentless determination they climb out of a poor environment to a finer character and a useful life. Others with every opportunity and many talents will waste them in laziness and careless living.

It is difficult to judge another fairly because the wrongful deeds men do may not tell all about them. There may be a strong minority vote in a man's deliberation which favored doing right but lost out in the final ballot when a wrongful decision resulted. But God knows of this better side. He knows that to judge by the deed alone does not take this minority of goodness into account.

It is nearly impossible to judge another fairly unless one knows the faith and the goal that guides a life. Some start at the bottom, but have high purpose and are willing to sacrifice in a long and difficult climb; while others start at the top and drift ever downward for lack of a noble faith. So one must know where a man is going — up or down — to judge him rightly at any given moment. From where has he come? What is his goal — his faith? Surely one who is aimlessly drifting from a beginning of great promise is not to be judged equal to one who climbs out from a discouraging situation. Some day these two will pass, and at that moment of passing they appear as equals. But they are not equals. One is on his way up from a poor environment, and every just appraisal of his work must take this struggle into consideration.

Besides the ideal of fairness and justice in our judgment of others, as taught by Christ, there is the ideal of social justice itself. This will be taken up in the following section of this chapter.

In the types of justice listed in the introduction of this chapter, the last one defines justice as a condition in society where people actually do face unequal opportunities, and yet in spite of this a just society will try to overcome these inequalities by offering to each boy and girl more and more equality of opportunity. This is the ideal of Christ; that those who are stronger help the weaker; that each boy and each girl be not handicapped by an unfortunate environment; that all are children of one Father in heaven who wants each to realize his fullest capabilities. So the state offers to each a free education, laws seek to prevent special privileges, and fair competition is held as a way to give everyone a chance to win a place in the sun.

A good society tries to do this — allow to each citizen the freedom to go as far as he can. This is democracy. A bad society will try to keep people where they are regardless of their talents and abilities. A bad society will see the privileged strive to keep their position regardless of merit. A good society is an aristocracy of only one thing — excellence. And each boy and each girl may have equal opportunity to try for it, though not all may attain it. But even here, the less fortunate may not be exploited by the more capable. Aristocracy of excellence means aristocracy of goodness, of truth, of beauty — but not at any price. There are inalienable rights that are equal to all, and the gifted may not deprive their slower companions of these rights.

Moreover, when a society allows excessive privileges to prevail, justice requires more equality. This is the basis of our graduated income and property tax. Social justice cannot prevail where a few are very rich and a great many are very poor. America overcomes such injustice by giving to each new generation a fair chance to win a share of the world's goods. We call it free enterprise or economic freedom. There is to be a chance for every boy to try his hand. America is "dedicated to the proposition that all men are created free and equal" — not equal in their several abilities, but equal in opportunity to do what they can with the abilities they do possess — this freedom and opportunity is the promise that is America.

Now in this statement from The Deseret News, we read: "We stand for the Constitution of the United States with its three departments of government as therein set forth, each one fully independent in its own field." I hope that every member of the Church subscribes to that declaration — also to the Deseret News. The preamble to the Constitution does not begin, "I, the king"; nor does it begin, "I, the President of the United States." It reads:

"We the people of the United States, in order to form a more perfect union, establish justice, insure domestic tranquility, provide for the common defense, promote the general welfare, and secure the blessings of liberty to ourselves and our posterity, do ordain and establish this Constitution for the United States of America." It is "We the people."

It was understood that the people would govern; of course, it would have to be by representation, but the control of government would be in the hands of the people.

As we read in the Book of Mormon, when the righteous rule, everything is well. King Mosiah gave up his throne with the idea that the people would have a republic, and he called attention to the dangers of a kingdom and a centralized government and the dangers that would arise should the wicked rule. The Lord has taught us to choose wise men and just men, and that was the understanding on the part of these men who formed the Constitution of the United States. [^2]

-Joseph Fielding Smith

If laws are needed to define the rights, privileges, and obligations of the respective contenders, let such laws be passed, but let them be fair, impartial, and unbiased laws. You will never cure the evil with laws that shackle one of the disputants while leaving the other to roam at large with unrestrained license to do evil. If tribunals be needed to administer and enforce the laws, let them be impartially constituted, not packed with personnel so biased that their decision may with certainty be predicted before the cause is heard. And when a judgment has been rendered by a duly constituted tribunal, let that body not be dissolved and its judgment vacated under pressure and another tribunal set up to render the kind of decision the dissatisfied party wants. That practice only brings the whole system into disrepute and the government itself into contempt.

The authority of law must be preserved, orderly procedure maintained, the rights of the unoffending but suffering public made secure regardless of the wishes of the contending parties or the pressures they may bring to bear. [^3]

-Albert E. Bowen

For it is out of that religion that the whole concept of the common brotherhood of men as the children of the same God derives, each equal before the law. So intimately are the two intertwined that democracy and the Christian religion must survive or perish together. Neither has worked perfectly in human hands. But the failure of the perfect working of the principles of free government probably is fairly in proportion to the failure of men to live the Christian religion. The perfect working of the latter would insure the perfect working of the former. We may not, except at our peril, discard either of them. Together they have provided an atmosphere in which, in spite of imperfections, we have lived and flourished as has no other nation is recorded history. It is important to note that in those countries where freedom has perished, there has gone side by side with its decline, a breaking down and denial of the Christian religion, and in that country where the breakdown has been most complete, there has been accomplished the most thorough regimentation of the bodies and spirits of men. But a superficial, unbelieving profession of the Christian faith will not withstand the disintegrating forces at play in the world today. Only a genuine, deep-seated religious conviction carried over into practice can do that. The disciples who established the Ancient Church were not men who rationalized Jesus and His doctrines away, nor were satisfied with proclaiming him merely a great ethical teacher. When Jesus asked His disciples, "Whom do men say that I am?" ( Mark 8:27), Peter gave him the various conjectures that men had ventured concerning him. Jesus then put it to him directly: "Â�Whom say ye that I am?" Peter answered without equivocation or hesitancy.

"Though art the Christ, the son of the living God." (see Matt. 16:13-16) That is the kind of living faith which carried the Christian religion into ascendancy in the western world and ultimately gave to the world our democracy. It is the only kind of faith that can save the world from the unchristian doctrine of regimentation and authoritarian dominance over the lives of men.

It is that faith which it is the mission of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints to keep alive in the hearts of men and to perpetuate in this land. [^4]
