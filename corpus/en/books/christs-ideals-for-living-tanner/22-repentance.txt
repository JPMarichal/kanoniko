If the word "repentance" is defined broadly enough, it could be said that the gospel of Christ is the gospel of repentance.

The word "repentance," as used in the first collection of books that now makes up our New Testament, has the positive meaning of "enthusiasm to work righteousness." It is positive and prospective, forward-looking to the transformation of the whole man. This is the meaning of the word "repentance" in Greek, the language of the early New Testament books.

The word "repentance" in Latin has a different meaning. Here the meaning is retrospective and carries an emotional feeling of mourning and lamenting of our past wrongs. This backward look to the evil of a sin once committed is expressed rather graphically by Lancelot:

Happier are those that welter in their sin, Swine in the mud, that cannot see for slime, Slime of the ditch: but in me lived a sin So strange, of such a kind, that all of pure, Noble, and knightly in me twined and clung Round that one sin, until the wholesome flower And poisonous grew together, each as each, Not to pluck'd asunder. [^1]

Then King Arthur speaks of repentance in the first meaning, of hope and renewal to a new life of "knighthood and pure nobleness; whereto see thou, that it may bear its flower."

Nay-but thou errest, Lancelot; never yet Could all of true and noble in knight and man Twine round one sin, whatever it might be, With such a closeness, but apart there grew, Save that he were the swine thou spakest of, Some root of knighthood and pure nobleness; Whereto see thou, that it may bear its flower. [^2]

Even though part of the true meaning of repentance is an emotion of remorse for offenses committed, it is the smaller part. Indeed it is a dangerous part, for it can lead to a morbid sense of guilt and inferiority that results in a loss of faith and self-respect. Excessive remorse can plunge the sufferer into a state of mind that paralyzes the moral energy necessary to reconstruct a life or right a wrong.

So Emerson writes, "Finish every day and be done with it. You have done what you could. Some blunders and absurdities no doubt crept in; forget them as soon as you can. Tomorrow is a new day; begin it well and serenely and with too high a spirit to be cumbered with your old nonsense. This day is all that is good and fair. It is too dear, with its hopes and invitations, to waste a moment on the yesterdays." [^3]

One feels the impending disaster in Macbeth resulting from a sin-sickness he was not able to overcome:

Macbeth: Canst thou not minister to a mind diseased, Pluck from the memory a rooted sorrow, Raze out the written troubles of the brain, And with some sweet oblivious antidote Cleanse the stuffed bosom of that perilous stuff, Which weighs upon the heart?

Doctor: Therein the patient Must minister to himself. [^4]

In Milton's Paradise Lost is another example of the darkness from vain imaginings:

(Satan:) The mind is its own place, and in itself Can make a heaven of hell, a hell of heaven.

Me miserable! which way shall I fly Infinite wrath, and infinite despair? Which way I fly is hell; myself am hell; And in the lowest deep a lower deep Still threat'ning to devour me, opens wide, To which the hell I suffer seems a heaven. O, then, at last relent! Is there no place Left for repentance, none for pardon left? [^5]

-John Milton

Latter-day Saints emphasize the positive approach to repentance. Prior to repentance one must have faith. There is no place for brooding that can only hurt the spiritual life. Faith in the Gospel requires one to be active in thinking good and doing good. Futile regrets are crowded out like weeds in a garden by carefully tended plants of the good and the beautiful in each day. And this is the true gospel of Jesus Christ. Jesus wanted an abundant life for all. "Be of good cheer," He said. And in all sacred literature there is no finer expression on the subject of repentance than this: "Â�He who has repented of his sins the same is forgiven, and I, the Lord, remember them no more." (Doctrine & Covenants 58:42)

They do me wrong who say I come no more When once I knock and fail to find you in, For every day I stand ouside your door And bid you wake, and rise to fight and win.

Wail not for precious chances passed away, Weep not for golden ages on the wane! Each night I burn the records of the day; At sunrise every soul is born again.

Laugh like a boy at splendors that have sped, To vanished joys be blind and deaf and dumb; My judgments seal the dead past with its dead, But never blind a moment yet to come.

Tho' deep in mire, wring not your hands and weep; I lend my arm to all who say, "I can!" No shamefaced outcast ever sank so deep But yet might rise and be again a man. [^6]

-Walter Malone

Jesus placed great emphasis upon repentance because He meant by it not only a change of behavior, but a change of mind. Always He stresses, not alone the outward act, but the inner life where is found our self-love and unjustified pride. From these spiritual sins comes self-righteousness that cuts us off from God and man; comes selfishness that denies our social responsibilities; come evil thoughts that poison our lives.

In the three parables quoted above, it is suggested that each one of them represents a different means of becoming lost: to wander off through ignorance; to become lost through circumstances we do not create; to become lost through deliberate recklessness of evil choice. And in all cases, Jesus is saying that God pursues and follows that which is astray. This was His own mission to the world. "The son of man came," He said, "to seek and to save that which is lost." And again, "I came not to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance." ( Luke 5:32)

He tried to awaken men's hearts for a better life. At times He must have been discouraged, for the scriptures read: "Then began he to upbraid the cities wherein most of his mighty works were done, because they repented not." ( Matt. 11:20) Even so, wherever He went, and down through these centuries, He has always awakened a consciousness of sin and a longing for a life that is better. This was what happened when He met Peter who said, when asked to join His cause, "Â�Depart from me; for I am a sinful man, O Lord." ( Luke 5:8) It happened to Zachaeus who felt his evil practices so keenly that he volunteered to restore his wrongs fourfold. It happened to the rich young ruler, who "went away," but at least "sorrowfully," because he lacked the will power to join His cause-he had too many possessions. It happened to the dying thief on the cross. It happens to each of us.

Great Master, touch us with thy skillful hand; Let not the music that is in us die! Great Sculptor, hew and polish us; nor let, Hidden and lost, thy form within us lie. Spare not the stroke. Do with us as Thou wilt, Let there be naught unfinished, broken, marred, Complete Thy purpose, that we may become Thy perfect image, Thou our God and Lord. [^7]

A man will do almost anything before he will honestly and frankly face and confess his own mistakes and blunders and sins. He will blame others first, or unavoidable circumstances, or rationalize and justify his actions — almost every excuse, before he will turn in on himself for a clear look at self-love, unjustifiable pride, or an unpayable debt of guilt for some wrong done to another.

Repentance, therefore, is no easy business. Let the great Phillips Brooks, an expert observer in this field, analyze the difficulties that confront a man enmeshed in sin — the reason so few say with the prodigal, "I will arise and go":

What is it that keeps a man down when he has fallen out of goodness and self respect? When the spring of his life is broken and the fresh hopefulness of the manly struggle to be high and pure and good is blurred and lost, when he has sinned and the burden of his sin is lying on him, what is it that keeps him down? What hinders him from springing back again into the strength and purity which he has lost? Mainly these things: first, his self-indulgence, the dreadful indolence and force of habit that takes possession of him; secondly, his loss of reputation, the feeling that nobody expects or thinks anything of him any longer; thirdly, his conceit and affection, which would take his sin and disgrace and trick it out in some disguise of virtue or brilliancy, and set him to boasting of it. Picture any fallen man, a man who used to be brave and good and sober and honest. Now he has gone. You never see him in the paths of reputable people. Men look to him no more for examples of upright living. What has caused all this? He came to some bad place. He sinned. He fell into disgrace. And the powers that have held him down, that have stereotyped and perpetuated his disaster, have been these: He has grown self-indulgent in his sin, with no enterprise or energy to rise up and cast it off; he has ceased to care what men think about him; and, having lost everything else to be proud of, he has grown proud of his disgrace, making believe to himself that it is honorable. [^8]

Difficult as the practice of the ideal of repentance may be, it is never impossible to the follower of Christ who has faith that God "seeth in secret," and through prayer gives understanding and help. For in such experience everyone will testify to a new life, a better life, a new beginning. One needs but attend testimony meetings to hear witnesses of God's infinite love.

Eldred G. Smith, Patriarch to the Church, has pointed out the continuing need of repentance as a principle of progress:

As the steel which is heated and quenched becomes much stronger and more valuable, as a result of that tempering, so do we become stronger and more valuable as we overcome our sins, be they great or small. Each one has his trials to go through, and they all seem to be different. In the process of this tempering of the soul of man, we learn one great principle among many others: The need for repentance. It is this spirit of repentance the Lord requests as a prerequisite to baptism, that through the act of repentance, man may start on the road to perfection and receive baptism and the Holy Ghost. But repentance does not stop there. That is the beginning of progress; and as soon as repentance stops, progress stops. [^9]

-Eldred G. Smith
