Aristotle said that "all men desire by nature to know." This is a high tribute to man's nature. God has made us so that by our very nature as human beings we want to know. What we want to know is the truth. What is the truth? One might answer with several similar phrases: The truth is the actual situation, the fact, the state of affairs, whatever is the case — no more, no less. Joseph Smith gave an expression of this ideal of truth which all men want to know: "And truth is knowledge of things as they are, and as they were, and as they are to come." (Doctrine & Covenants 93:24) This is what men want to know-the world and events as they actually are. Not as we wish they were; not necessarily as someone reports to us; not as we might hope — just the truth without false report or wish-thinking or vain hoping. This is what all men by their very nature want to know.

Without discussing the theories of truth in the philosophies of pragmatism and idealism, one could say that the most widely accepted definition of truth is the correspondence of an idea in the mind with a fact of the world outside the mind. When these two correspond, the idea and the external fact, we say that the idea is true, there is "fidelity to objective reality." A more careful statement would be to say that truth is the quality of a factual proposition, when the proposition states an environmental situation or condition that is verifiable, at least in principle. A fact, then, is what makes a proposition true or false.

The stake of religion in this definition is obvious: Do the claims made by religion correspond to the fact, the situation, the actual state of affairs? Such fundamental beliefs in religion as God, the soul, immortality — are these facts, objectively real, the actual situation — or, are they ideas in the mind only, without any reality corresponding to them? On this question religion, as we understand it, will stand or fall. Latter-day Saints believe in the objective reality of God as a person, in the objective reality of the soul, and in immortality as a factual situation. This knowledge is based upon much external evidence, but fundamentally on individual and personal testimony.

Evidence used by the scientist, such as observable physical laws, or memory and imagination in personality; these facts and others, such as beauty, add great support to prove the existence of God and the soul. But in the last analysis, our religious beliefs are rooted most firmly in personal testimony — each one knowing for himself — and this belief does not come so much through logical reasoning nor through observation by one of the five senses, but rather it is knowledge, or inspiration, a testimony given by the Holy Ghost, that transcends sensing and reasoning. Such inspirational knowledge will be discussed in a following section on "The Ideal of Truth In Our Lives."

Truth makes us free in two ways:

1 . It liberates our minds from possible enslavements and dogmatisms. Often we allow wish-thinking to take the place of realistic thought. Sometimes we make rationalizations because of what we want to be true, whether we have good reason or good evidence, or even in their absence.

2 . Truth also liberates our minds for new possibilities, new values, new truths, "Â�eye hath not seen, nor ear heard, neither have entered into the heart of manÂ�" ( 1 Cor. 2:9) Thus, truth frees us from past errors and opens wide the gates to future progress.

The spirit or method of achieving these two great goals in life was also given by our Savior: "Ask, and it shall be given you; seek, and ye shall find; knock, and it shall be opened unto you:" ( Matt. 7:7)

This determined persistence of continuous and unrelenting inquiry is, as Dr. Henry Eyring points out, the key to truth in science as well as truth in religion. [^1]

The first value of truth, that of compelling us to give up untruths, is most difficult to achieve. A man will say almost anything, give reasons and excuses to infinity before he will say, "I was wrong," or "I was in error."

Jesus gave an eternal observation on this human frailty: "Â�If the light that is in thee be darkness, how great is the darkness!" ( Matt. 6:23) History, especially the history of religions, is a running commentary on these words of Christ. Religion, unlike science, produces a zeal with its truth-claims, so that if the claims are not in fact true, "If the light that is in thee be darkness, how great is the darkness!" Paul illustrates this in a line he wrote about the enthusiastic Saints in Corinth and their erroneous beliefs. "I bear them record that they have a zeal for God, but not according to knowledge." ( Rom. 10:2)

The word "repent" in Greek, the language from which our New Testament came, means to "change your mind." This commandment to repent, or to change one's mind, is a difficult request to make because a new idea may demand the exit of an old one, long cherished; yet upon our willingness to do so, depends the chance of progress with our lives.

Jesus lived this ideal of replacing old ideas with new and better ones. Through His study of Old Testament scripture He changed the present and future with these significant words: "Ye have heard it was said by them of old timeÂ�But I say unto youÂ�"

Christianity through the centuries has not always been friendly to free and liberating inquiry. The price paid for this denial of freedom has been an accumulation of superstitions and evil persecutions. This aversion to free inquiry is foreign to the teachings and spirit of Christ, who said the greatest commandment was to "Â�love the LordÂ�with all thy mind." Had Christianity followed this instead of tyrannizing over the free spirit of men, how different the world of today might be! For in the last analysis, the apostasy from Christ in later Christianity was the loss of freedom. With this loss came the imposition of ideas without the chance of correction. So it has been through the centuries. The truth — open search for it — was denied, and therefore truth had no chance to fulfill Christ's great promise: "Â�the truth shall make you free."

In the introduction of this chapter mention was made of knowledge by inspiration. That is, in addition to the method of finding truth by observation and reason, truth may also be made known through inspiration. Such truth is gained by immediate insight or direct apprehension. It goes beyond reason and sense perception. It is experienced by a feeling of immediate certainty, sometimes described as "direct insight into the truth." Latter-day Saints speak in testimony meetings of the convictions arrived at in this way. They know the Gospel is true, not by observation through sense perception; not by reasoning out the logic of its truth — though either one or both of these methods may be the basis of a testimony — but there is yet another basis: It is direct insight, immediate awareness of the truth of God's commandments, etc. Such a testimony comes in many different ways, mostly through prayer, worship, partaking of the sacrament, or in quiet meditation.

Professor W. P. Montague of Columbia has given an analysis of the function of intuition as a method of understanding reality, in addition to the method of reason and sensory observation. He applies it to friendship and love and beauty, but one might apply it to God and moral laws and the whole plan of the Gospel itself. He writes:

We might take as an example of an elemental interest romantic love and the judgment of faith on which it is based. It would surely be a vain and preposterous undertaking to discover one's true sweetheart by accepting the authority of others, by using deductive reasoning and calculation, by cold-blooded empirical analysis of her perceivable qualities, or by considering the extent to which she might be a practical utility. All of these non-mystical methods would doubtless be appropriate in the selection of a business partner, a housekeeper, or even in making a marriage of convenience. But no one either could or would fall in love for any other reason than that the beloved appealed in a direct and unanalyzable manner to his heart or his feelings. In other words, the lover as such is and must always remain a mystic. And even in forming the belief on which one bases his choice of a friend, intuition is almost as indispensable as in choosing a sweetheart. True friendship is certainly not based upon either calculation or utility, but upon the direct appeal to our sympathies and affections. The same might be said of objects of art, the primary enjoyment of which is not based upon considerations that are rationally analyzable. [^3]

As indicated above, truth is something discovered from three different sources: by sense experience, by intellect, and by intuition, or inspiration. It is by inspiration that one can say, "I know the Gospel is true." This is knowledge by inspiration under the influence of the Holy Ghost. It is truth that is direct, immediate, and certain, an experience that transcends both knowledge from the senses and knowledge from valid reasoning.

Through the years many claims to truth by inspiration have been made, and everyone is aware that many such claims are false. How can one be sure? There are several ways. The foremost is to be sure one is trying to live the Gospel. Righteous living is a condition for divine help. Moreover, we think more clearly about life's problems when our motives are purest. We pray more earnestly if we possess a deep humility.

Another test by which we can feel certain of our knowledge gained through inspiration, is to be sure that we do not reach conclusions that are contrary to careful reasoning and everyday sense observation. God has given man his wonderful reasoning powers to be used. It is true that we cannot always find answers to our questions by reason alone. We need God's help and guidance. But God requires that we help ourselves through His gift to us of our reasoning abilities. One can be sure that there are times when God gives us light and direction beyond our capacity to reason out the answers, but we should continue to use our reason and sense observation, for these also are given of God to guide and help us.

In D&C 9 of the Doctrine and Covenants, verse 8, is given the admonition that inspiration requires the use of one's best reasoning powers, that to cooperate with God in finding truth, "You must study it out in your own mind." Then, having done one's part through good living and reflective thinking, God "will cause that your bosom shall burn within you; therefore, you shall feel that it is right."

But, behold, I say unto you, that you must study it out in your mind; then you must ask me if it be right, and if it is right I will cause that your bosom shall burn within you; therefore, you shall feel that it is right.

In the chapter on reverence, the attitude of Latter-day Saints toward the ideal of truth was explained, but a few more words may be added:

Those who love the Gospel will always have a wide tolerance for honest seekers of the truth. They will love them and encourage them. They will never fear them nor mistrust them, so long as they seek truth honestly and with steadfast devotion. A real and abiding testimony is one without easy mistrust of those who seek; without fear of them and without worry about them. For to possess truth and yet seek more of it is the religion of the Latter-day Saints. From the days of fearful persecution, that drove the Saints to build new homes far away in the wilderness, we are at last secure from physical harm, and at peace within. Truth gives this calm and serenity. There is nothing to fear now, unless it be a faith that is so weak it fears honest inquiry. Only those not certain of the Gospel's eternal truths are afraid of questions, and doubts, and seekers of truth. Those with a testimony of the Gospel's great and noble standards revealed from God will never have but love and encouragement in their hearts for others who would pioneer for new truths or struggle to comprehend old truths. This confidence and unshakable knowledge of God and His Gospel plan explains the sacrifice of the Latter-day Saints for education; their hunger for learning; their devotion to the Gospel. "It is impossible for a man to be saved in ignorance," is their standing admonition, and their banner is the ideal that "Whatever principle of intelligence we attain unto in this life, it will rise with us in the resurrection." (Doctrine & Covenants 130:18)

Oh say, what is truth? 'Tis the fairest gem That the riches of worlds can produce, And priceless the value of truth will be when The proud monarch's costliest diadem Is counted but dross and refuse.

Then say, what is truth? 'Tis the last and the first, For the limits of time it steps o'er. Though the heavens depart, and the earth's fountains burst, Truth, the sum of existence, will weather the worst, Eternal, unchanged, evermore. [^4]

To be sure, men often fail before the ideal of truth, as before other ideals, and failures here, as in other realms, always hurt our faith — but not fatally, nor even seriously. Men sometimes pay tribute to truth, but in their practices they frequently deny their faith. It is easy to speak of one's love for truth and so difficult to comply with the conditions for finding it — an open-mindedness born of deep humility, a heart full of love for all who are seekers, and even more, a heart of love for those who are not sincere seekers of truth. Even before a closed mind or insincerity, "love never faileth."
