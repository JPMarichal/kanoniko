Obert C. Tanner, Christ's Ideals for Living ( 1955)

Resolution

"Thou shalt worship the Lord thy God, and Him only shalt thou serve."

Many ideals are suggested in the baptism and in the temptations of Christ. This was a time in his life of dedication, decision, commitment, and firm resolution "to fulfill all righteousness." And so each one of us must decide for himself what he will do with his life, how he will face temptations, and to whom and to what he will give his highest loyalty. One might consider these questions under the ideal of resolution. The opposite of such an ideal is procrastination and indecision. Such a contrast of living calls for careful analysis, for upon it may determine how our lives finally turn out.

Here we are concerned with religion. Many people feel that their faith is something they need not now decide, and that, like many others matters, it can be postponed. They feel that their commitment to the Church and their faith in God can be put off. They would not decide against the Church, but they prefer to delay any decision. They fail to see that to postpone and suspend judgment is actually to make a decision — a negative one. For life gets itself made up one way or another. Life does not postpone itself, it does not wait. And therefore, to fail to act is to miss the positive values of a triumphant faith and fellowship that comes with resolving one's faith now.

A man on a train debating whether to get off at the next stop cannot resolve the questions by suspending his judgment. To delay or postpone or suspend judgment is to make the decision. So it is with religion. To suspend judgment about a vital and living faith, is to have made a decision against it. Many do not see this until the years pass by and they finally realize that years ago the decision was made by their lack of resolution.

There is a tide in the affairs of men, Which, taken at the flood, leads on to fortune; Omitted, all the voyage of their life Is bound in shallows and the miseries: And we must take the current when it serves, Or lose our ventures. [^1]

-William Shakespeare

It is said that William James closed his last class lecture each year with the following words which he called his "Faith Ladder." The following sentences illustrate the steps by which one faces life's great options, where a great value may be lost, not alone by denial of it, but lost by serious doubt that causes one to delay a decision and postpone and procrastinate until life has been lived and the values are lost. Consider some great religious matter you are now in doubt about. The evidence for or against it is not sufficient, nor do you feel such compelling evidence will ever be available. Then, James would say, consider this ladder as a way of solving the problem with a positive decision:

On the first round of the ladder we say of a momentous view of life and religion, that it is a possible view, it is not self-contradictory, it is not absurd;

On the second round we say it might be true so far as actual facts are concerned;

On the third round We say it may be true now for all that anybody knows;

On the fourth we add that it is fit to be true, it ought to be true; On the fifth we affirm it must be true;

Well, then, we say at the top of the ladder it shall be true (at any rate for me) because I am going to adopt it as my truth and live by it henceforth. [^2]

The setting in Christ's life when He made the decision of His baptism "to fulfill all righteousness," might have been something like the following:

Finally, we can imagine the arrival of a great day in his life. Jesus put away his tools for the last time, locked the carpenter shop, and gave the key to his brother. He said goodbye to his mother and brothers and sisters, and turned toward Jordan where John the Baptist was preaching. [^3]

When John began his teaching, the deeper convictions of Jesus responded. And then, here at His baptism, great revelations were given to Him. This was to be the decisive turning-point in His life. Whether or not as a boy He knew fully of His divine Sonship, all question of it was now cast aside. Jesus was called to a supreme work.

The baptism represented not the decision of a moment but the culmination of all his previous training and thought. He had lived in closest touch with the people of Nazareth and had moved among the throngs that filled the crowded cities of Galilee. He recognized their deep need of a spiritual guide who would lead them to their Divine Father and reveal to them the forgiveness and the inspiration which God was eager to give. In his own experience Jesus had found that eternal source of peace and strength. His clear consciousness that his own personal knowledge of God and of the way of life alone would satisfy the needs of the people, was the compelling force in his call. [^4]

He had, at the waters of baptism, dedicated His life-given over His will, to doing the will of the Father.

Here with the multitudes on the banks of the Jordan he identified himself with the sinful people whom he came to save. He identified his own soul with all the burdens and needs of his people. He was thinking of the unhappy, restless multitude who needed help. He was baptised into their need and into their destiny. He was about to bring to them the message of a great hope-the hope of a new kind of Kingdom: but, in that adventure, he had to risk the consequences of their sins. [^5]

And so, in His baptism, Jesus identified Himself with the cause of John-recognized the divine mission of the Baptist, fulfilled a condition for "all righteousness," and thus committed Himself to a great struggle against the organized religion of His day. [^6]In the temptations of Christ, one finds the ideal of resolution to resist temptation at its best. To each of the temptations He replied, "Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceedeth out of the mouth of GodÂ�Thou shalt not tempt the Lord thy GodÂ�Get thee hence, Satan: For it is written, Thou shalt worship the Lord thy God, and him only shalt thou serve."

The first temptation was for personal gratification, pleasures, and selfish ends. Against this He chose a life of sacrificial service to others on behalf of spiritual ideals.

The second temptation was for personal security, freedom from suffering. Against this He turned in the direction of insecurity, uncertainty, misunderstanding, and persecution.

The third temptation was for honor and glory, to lower His ideal and establish an earthly kingdom. Against this He committed Himself to a spiritual kingdom and a willingness to pay with the crucifixion if necessary.

These are the universal temptations men face in making up their lives: personal gratification (love of pleasures), freedom from suffering (love of security), and the insatiable appetite for the honors and glories of men (love of power).

All three temptations resolve on the question of how Christ was to use His powers. And His answer was the resolution not to accept an earthly kingdom, but rather, if necessary, be crucified for a spiritual one.

The same terrific, inward, spiritual struggle goes on in the minds and hearts of men and women today. The choice is ever between gratification, or sacrifice, one or the other, never both. And the temptation for the easier way is always present. Nor did Jesus resolve these and other temptations, once and for all. They were continuous, for the story ends with these significant words: "And when the devil had ended all the temptation, he departed from him for a season."

Later Peter tempted Him not to go down to Jerusalem where only the cross would result. In the Garden of Gethsemane, He made His final reply: "Not my will, but thine, be done." Between the time of these forty days' trial in the wilderness, and later in Gethsemane, there must have been many discouragements. The writer of Hebrews wrote of Christ's life: "In all things he was tempted like as we are, yet without sin."

Great temptation comes with great powers. "The acorn seedling may be impeded by a few dead leaves, but it will never shake in the grip of the tempest until it becomes an oak."

In the wilderness the Savior, hungry and haggard, was tempted beyond any power we may imagine. "If thou art the Son of God, command that these stones be made bread:" "If thou be the Son of God, cast thyself down;" "All the kingdoms of the worldÂ�if thou wiltÂ�worship me."

The ideal of high, purposeful resolution, to do the best we can with our lives, has many facets:

First, there is our work. To succeed in our vocation takes will power. As one writer puts it:

The man who succeeds above his fellows is the one who, early in life, clearly discerns his object, and towards that object habitually directs his powers. Even genius itself is but fine observation strengthened by fixity of purpose. Every man who observes vigilantly and resolves steadfastly grows unconsciously into genius. [^7]

Indecisive procrastination is fatal in youth. A vivid description of the difference between a life of resolution and one of indecision is described thus:

Some men, like captains of ocean liners, know that there is a course marked out particularly for them and they are trying not to miss it; some, like pleasure sailors out for fun, go any way as chance caprice suggests. [^8]

One ship drives east and another drives west With the selfsame winds that blow. 'Tis the set of the sails And not the gales Which tells us the way to go.

Like the winds of the sea are the ways of fate, As we voyage along through life: 'Tis the set of the soul That decides its goal, And not the calm or the strife. [^9]

-Ella Wheeler Wilcox

Second, there is the resolution to keep a high perspective, a high opinion of what life itself can be. John Homer Miller, writing on this thought, observes:

What many people need to keep their morale up to par is the ability to throw away their five-and-ten-cent store glasses and begin looking at life with the long lenses of eternity. We need to acquire the power to live beyond the "terminals of the day's happenings" and, in the words of Emerson, "to hear what the centuries say, not merely the days and the hours." A graduate student in one of our eastern universities recently said that new facts and points of view were heaped upon him so rapidly each day in such bewildering confusion that one of his greatest personal problems was to maintain his balance and keep himself from becoming neurotic. He confessed that his greatest help was to be found in a church service where he was able to refocus his attention. He said, "I found that all things fall into place and life falls into place only with God, who is the beginning, the meaning, and the end." [^11]

Latter-day Saints believe that God has a plan for each individual life. Our free agency and self-will may determine against it, and so it may be changed, but just as God has a plan for the whole of creation, He has a plan for each of its parts, and that includes a plan for every son and daughter of God if they have the resolution to seek it out with resolve to achieve it.

Laid on Thine altar, O my Lord divine, Accept this gift today, for Jesus' sake: I have no jewels to adorn Thy shrine, No far-famed sacrifice to make; But here within my trembling hand I bring This will of mine — a thing that seemeth small, But Thou alone, O Lord, canst understand How when I yield Thee this, I yield mine all.

-Author unknown

Spiritual understanding and power and discernment and self-mastery do not come gratuitously. They must be won. Jesus Himself, whose penetrating vision pierced through and beyond physical boundaries, wrestled in the wilderness for forty days before entering upon His great ministry of self-consecration and spiritual supremacy and He called mightily upon the Father for comfort and support. But the day of His victory over temptation, His life was one of active, aggressive practice of the virtues He taught.

There is in the plan for life, as proposed by religion, no promise of freedom from care or sorrow or suffering or disparagement or want. But through all these, fidelity to the overarching purpose we must stand, unyielding and undismayed. Through experience and mastery and purification and spiritual expansion, man fits himself for greater things, both here and beyond, while living life among men in this material physical world. [^19]

-Albert E. Bowen
