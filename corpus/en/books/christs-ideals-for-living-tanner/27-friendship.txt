Friendship

"Ye are my friends, if ye do whatsoever I command you."

Generally speaking, one gets what one gives in life, and this is especially true of friendships. In friendship we give of ourselves, and generally, though not always, we get about what we give.

The key to great friendship was expressed by the Savior: "Ye are my friends, if ye do whatsoever I command you." At first glance this appears an arbitrary condition for friendship. But consider what the Master is commanding: That we be friends on a high moral and spiritual level, namely, that we speak sincerely, act with the golden rule as our guide, and in all our friendships we be in our own person the kind of friend we would like to have in others-modest, trusting, thoughtful, and above all, have a spirit that is kind and friendly.

Like the sunbeams of morn on the mountains, The soul they awake to good cheer; Like the murmur of cool, pleasant fountains, They fall in sweet cadences near.

Let's oft, then, in kindly-toned voices, Our mutual friendship renew, Till heart meets with heart and rejoices, In friendship that ever is true. [^1]

-Joseph L. Townsend

This is the clear meaning of Christ's ideal of friendship — elevating it to a plane that will endure through the years and all eternity, because such friendships are bound with the chords of ideals that are themselves eternal — doing the things which He commands us to do.

Friendship can be best described in words that Paul used to describe love: Friendship understands all things, endures all things, and outlives all things. And it is true that one of the best definitions of a friend is he who knows all about you and yet likes you.

He who has a thousand friends has not a friend to spare, And he who has one enemy shall meet him everywhere. [^2]

Truly great friendships, however, are based on moral and spiritual foundations — fair play, honesty, integrity, and loyalty to great causes; the Church, democracy, and universal brotherhood. Since friendship is the giving of ourselves, and since the supply of ourselves is so limited, we generally do not make more than a few real friends in a lifetime. It is best therefore to find in those friendships a common love for things beautiful, devotion to truth, and a common effort to make the world a little better.

Robert Louis Stevenson summed up the task of life in terms Of friendship for others and also friendship for ourselves:

To be honest, to be kind — to earn a little and to spend a little less, to make upon the whole a family happier for his presence, to renounce when it shall be necessary and not be embittered, to keep a few friends but these without capitulation — above all, on the same grim condition, to keep friends with himself — here is a task for all that a man has of fortitude and delicacy. [^3]In a larger perspective, friendship is more than a matter of congenial intimacies. Dr. Fosdick describes friendship as the great fundamental need of our world:

Friendship is an expansive spirit that overthrows vindictiveness and takes in enemies, overpasses jealousy and embraces rivals. Too great and too glad to be stopped by prejudice, it seeks the good of all sorts and conditions of folk across all the barriers that caste, class, and race can erect. Such magnanimous friendship is an elemental test of character. Such undiscourageable good-will is the indispensable foundation for the brotherhood of man. [^4]

This, then, is Christ's meaning of friendship: "Ye are my friends, if ye do whatsoever I command you." ( John 15:14) The best gift one can give his friends is himself at his best.

Mormonism might be characterized as a religion of friendships. There are two outstanding reasons for this: (1) Friendships within the Church are numerous because of universal participation in Church activities. (2) Friendships are strong within the Church because they have the moral and spiritual foundation of Christ's teachings.

1 . Friendships are numerous in Mormonism because from youth to maturity and old age, each has a group to join with common interests. From earliest years, and as we move along life's highway, there are others with whom to share life's joys and problems and sometimes our disappointments. Together in one or several Church organizations people meet for guidance and sharing and helping, that life may be lived at its best. And in this common effort the Church affords unlimited opportunities for giving to us life's greatest treasures, friends in many different walks of life. They are made in Primary, Sunday School, the Mutual Improvement Association, Priesthood classes, Relief Societies, Church Welfare, Sacrament meetings, etc.

Friendships within the Church are rewarding in their variety — people from different walks of life with infinite differences in personalities. The Church offers this opportunity, for unlike other organizations which specialize in their membership — all business, all politics, all social — the Church reaches out to include every individual, one by one, and each one of them would be an interesting and rewarding friend to have. Such is the gospel of friendships which is the gospel of Christ.

2 . Friendships are strong if they have the moral and spiritual foundation of Christ's teachings. Herein one finds the basis for the statement that Mormonism is a religion of friendships. Mormonism is activated by the principle of universal participation in Church work-the kingdom of God on earth. In this mutual endeavor the bonds of true friendship are made. Missionaries know the enduring strength of such friendships. Indeed, friendships grow with leaders and teachers in each Church organization, and these friendships endure long after the work is turned over to others and new assignments are taken. Above all, there is the friendship in communion of worship. In all the cities of America, and now in most of the world, members of the Church gather to worship and to find the friendship of the Saints that is unmatched in any other activity.

Concerning the art of making friends, Bennett and Hand offer this analysis:

Many studies of workers have shown that ability to get along with others is even more important than technical skill in the job. Studies of the sources of happiness have likewise emphasized the importance of social facility. There is ample evidence in research and human experience that the person who cuts himself off from contacts with his fellows is cutting himself off from a basic source of life's happiness.

What are the important factors in getting along well with others? The Golden Rule is probably the basic secret of this art. If we try to give the same thoughtful consideration to others that we would wish others to give to us, we shall respect not only their rights but their personalities. We shall be considerate of their feelings and shall have a real interest in their welfare. We shall cooperate happily with others in both work and play. We shall love instead of hate. We can hate what injures or debases human life without hating the individuals who may be responsible.

If we truly apply the Golden Rule, we shall try to see ourselves as others see us. We shall thereby become aware of ways in which we are likely to annoy, antagonize, or hurt others. We shall also discover ways in which we may attract others and add to their pleasure and happiness. To develop this ability, we need to learn how to interpret the evidences of others' real attitudes, emotions, and thoughts, as expressed in their behavior. This is more of an art than a science. It is a skill which grows through experience in living with others. Do you enter wholeheartedly into activities for which you make personal sacrifices?

If we believe in following the Golden Rule, we must also believe in the resulting rule or corollary: Sharing wholeheartedly in the experiences of others is the experience of all truly happy, effective personalities. [^8]

Service

"Whosoever will be great among youÂ�shall be servant of all."

Perhaps the highest goodness attainable is a life of service to all mankind. Such an ideal is supported in nearly every page in the Gospels — the parables, the sermons, and the countless acts of service by our Lord Himself.

The ideal is not limited to any particular kind of service, nor a given quantity of service. The ideal is accepting life itself as a trust to be used in the welfare of mankind. It is a life that is glad for the chance to be of any help, an attitude that "service is the rent we pay for our own room on earth." [^13]

The opposite to a serviceable life would be one of negative uselessness, or worse, one marked by selfishness and cruelty and hatred.

Two cautions to a life of positive outgoing service need careful attention. One is the necessity that a youth think enough of himself to obtain a training, so that later he will have the capacity to render a finer service. This failure of a young man or woman to organize his life reminds one of Tolstoi's comment, quoted elsewhere, that before one can sweat about any service to the world, he should sweat about himself first. Our first duty is to ourselves. Jesus said to "love thy neighbor as thyself." Youth must think of the future, their part in it, and of helping with a trained mind and hand, ready when their turn comes.

The second limitation to a serviceable life is allowing our religion to become the hearing of the word only without the doing of it. There is reason for great joy in the "good news" of the Gospel, and to hear it preached has many rewards — it brings inward peace and calm to a life distracted by the many claims made upon us. In worship one gains immeasurable values, such as getting one's self together, finding harmony, new hope, and a sense of forgiveness. But this is all that many receive. In fact, they allow their religion to be essentially a receiving matter. The Gospel has two sides, however — the hearing and the doing. Jesus put the matter thus: "Therefore whosoever heareth these sayings of mine, and doeth them, I will liken him unto a wise man, which built his house upon a rock:Â�

And every one that heareth these sayings of mine, and doeth them not, shall be likened unto a foolish man, which built his house upon the sand:" ( Matt. 7:24, 26) Paul said, "For not the hearers of the law are just before God, but the doers of the lawÂ�" ( Rom. 2:13) James put a touch of warning in his words, "Be ye doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving your own selves." ( James 1:22) "Not every one that saith unto me, Lord, Lord, shall enter into the kingdom of heaven; but he that doeth the will of my Father which is in heaven. ( Matt. 7:21)

The life of service is not easily defined. One way to describe it would be to understand that for every right we claim there is a corresponding duty, and for every privilege a responsibility. Yet it is more; it is a heart filled with love and gratitude, such that one feels he never could repay the debt he owes to those whose centuries of toiling labor and costly sacrifices have made a world with its margins of good over evil, and truth over error, and beauty everywhere. Einstein properly said of our present century: "It's high time that the ideal of success should be replaced by the ideal of service."

The following inscriptions on the tombstones of two different men help to point up the ideal of service:

Here lies a miser who lived for himself, And cared for nothing but gathering pelf, Now, where he is, or how he fares, Nobody knows and nobody cares. [^14]

The other reads:

"The truest help we can render an afflicted man," writes Phillips Brooks, "is not to take his burden from him, but to call out his best strength that he may be able to bear the burden." [^16]

Virtue for its own sake, regardless of reward, is always a higher pathway than doing one's duty for a reward to follow. Sometimes people read the Gospels and feel that the lower standard of motive for reward is the dominant note — the humble shall be exalted (see Matt. 23:12), the rewards offered are sometimes a hundred fold. Another singular emphasis Christ made about the ideal of service is this: He was deeply concerned lest our lives fall into the deadly sin of negative uselessness — doing nothing when service is needed, being useless instead of useful.

Here is one of the hardest tests of discipleship to Christ, reaching out as volunteers in service even though there is no legal demand upon us, no social pressure or when there may even be good justification to "walk by on the other side." This was the terrible wrong of the Priest and Levite in the parable of the Good Samaritan — they did nothing, they were useless. They neither helped nor hurt the man. They did not want to be mixed up in the affair. They stood aloof from both robbery and service. Their plans and schedules were not upset by a rather messy situation. Like many of us today, they missed one more opportunity to be of real service.
