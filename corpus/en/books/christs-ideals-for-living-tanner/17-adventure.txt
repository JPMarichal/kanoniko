Obert C. Tanner, Christ's Ideals for Living ( 1955)

Adventure

"Endure hardness, as a good soldier of Jesus Christ."

In contrast to serenity, but not in contradiction to it, we come to another side of the gospel of Jesus Christ — adventure. Besides our need for inner peace and tranquility, human nature is such that we also need adventure. A measure of uncertainty is said to be a necessary condition for zest and progress. "It's faith in something," wrote Oliver Wendell Holmes, "and enthusiasm for something that makes a life worth looking at."

Of all the descriptions written of life as an adventure, the following from Dr. Fosdick would rank as one of the most vivid:

It is an amazing adventure to be born upon this wandering island in the sky and it is an adventure to leave it when death calls. To go to school, to make friends, to marry, to rear children, to face through life the swift changes of circumstance that no man can certainly predict an hour ahead, these are all adventures. Each new day is an hitherto unvisited country, which we enter, like Abraham leaving Ur for a strange land, "not knowing whither he went" (Heb. 11:8), and every New Year we begin a tour of exploration into a twelve month where no man's foot has ever walked before. If we all love tales of pioneers, it is because from the time we are weaned to the time we die, life is pioneering. [^1]

Religion to Latter-day Saints is anything but a stuffy affair. The Gospel was restored through young men. It was cradled in their hardships. It was nurtured amid persecutions; it grew with opposition and came to maturity with the death of the prophet. Latter-day Saints are all in the tradition of forced marches, new country, battles, Indians, suffering, hunger, wounds, disappointments and deaths.

But we are also in the tradition of missionaries, imprisonments and banishments; of songs and prayers and voyages; of Zion and labor and farms and cities; of learning and beauty and culture; of service and leadership and spiritual blessings to the world.

Such a tradition of adventure belongs in the religion of Christ. The Savior said:

Blessed are ye, when men shall revile you, and persecute you, and shall say all manner of evil against you falsely, for my sake.

Rejoice, and be exceeding glad; for great is your reward in heaven; for so persecuted they the prophets which were before you.

- Matt. 5:11-12

This is a chapter on the ideal of adventure — as faith in God, faith in man, and in a life lived as a great adventure of sacrificial devotion to great causes.

One gains some insight into the adventurous spirit of Christ from these words He spoke to His contemporaries:

For I say unto you, That except your righteousness shall exceed the righteousness of the scribes and the Pharisees, ye shall in no case enter into the kingdom of heaven.

Ye have heard that it was said by them of old time, Thou shalt not kill; and whosoever shall bill shall be in danger of the judgment:

But I say unto you, That whosoever is angry with his brother without a cause shall be in danger of the judgment: and whosoever shall say to his Brother, Raca, shall be in danger of the council; but whosoever shall say, Thou fool, shall be in danger of hell fire. ( Matt. 5:20-22)

Here was a challenge to present leaders, to past tradition, and a demand for a new righteousness from His followers. We come to the great divide in religious history with these words: "But I say unto you." Here was a new law, a new age, and a new life in God.

Jesus has been referred to as an ascetic — one who would turn his back on the simple pleasures of life and seek to save his soul apart from human relationships. He was anything but that. He enjoyed flowers and attended wedding feasts, and loved little children and the home life of His friends.

Then there are students who think of Christ as a poet. But Christ was neither an ascetic nor a poet. He was rather one who called men to a dangerous campaign. And a skeptical Thomas spoke the fear and faith of the Twelve when he said: "Â�Let us also go, that we may die with him." ( John 11:16)

Such a life did turn the world upside down. It was the greatest adventure ever launched in history. So far from being a view of life that was safe and secure, it was a view that His own life must pass away and that His disciples should be loyal to His cause and help to bring it about. There are many moods connected with the Gospel: serenity, joy, certainty, trust, etc. One mood would consider the Gospel as an adventure. The mood of adventure finds expression in the attitude we take toward (1) God, (2) Man, and (3) Ourselves.

The important part of the argument is that which reads: "not to wager that God is, is to wager that he is not." This is the issue for youth. Life goes on. It will not stop while we delay our decision. And "not to wager that God is, is to wager that he is not." How few realize that by delayed indecision of whether to wager at all, they have already made their wager — "that he is not."

The great definition of religion given by Professor Montague, that "what is highest in man is deepest in nature," is an expression of adventurous faith; that is, that our world will eventually make real what Christ has revealed.

It is true that the idea of God may be considered merely as a theoretical hypothesis, but real faith in God is an experience, not a belief with various arguments; it is the experience of a Divine Friendship, an Unseen Companion of the way, and such an experience is positive and living; it is significant and controlling; it is a positive assurance that God lives and hears prayer and helps us.

Such a venture of faith is never a half-way matter. It is all or nothing. On the positive side it is fellowship with the Highest and a feeling of responsibility to Him. It is a great adventure to test all we do by the principles of Jesus Christ. It is a great adventure to say, with Ralph Waldo Emerson: "All I have seen teaches me to trust the Creator for all I have not seen."

One of America's great pioneers in social work, Jane Addams, was asked, "What is your working hypothesis in Hull House?" She answered, "I believe in the innate fineness of people."

All the richest values, love and friendships, have with them the element of uncertainty.

So writes Professor Luccock:

Lloyds of London will insure almost anything under the sun — including the weather! — but even Lloyds will not insure the success of a marriage. They will insure a ship on a twenty-five thousand mile trip around the world. They will not insure a marriage over a twelve-month journey. The richest values of life are never mathematical, they are moral ventures. [^2]

There is no adventure comparable, perhaps, to that of forgiving an enemy. It takes great love and strong faith to try it, but the testimony of all who succeed is unanimous: "Love never faileth."

3 . There is the mood of adventure in the faith we have in ourselves. Following is the modest yet courageous and adventurous hope Lincoln held out for his own life:

Every man is said to have his peculiar ambition. Whether it be true or not, I can say, for one, that I have no other so great as that of being truly esteemed of my fellow-men, by rendering myself worthy of their esteem. How far I shall succeed in gratifying this ambition is yet to be developed. I am young and unknown to many of you. I was born, and have ever remained, in the most humble walks of life. I have no wealthy or popular relations or friends to recommend me. My case is thrown exclusively upon the independent voters of the country; and, if elected, they will have conferred a favor upon me for which I shall be unremitting in my labors to compensate.

But, if the good people in their wisdom shall see fit to keep me in the background, I have been too familiar with disappointments to be very much chagrined. [^3]

The ideal of losing one's life in a sacrificial adventure of service and kindness is told by Edwin H. Hughes with this analogy:

Two pools high up in the mountain talked together. One said, "I'm going down." "You'll lose your life. Don't go." But over the precipice and down the mountainside it went, across the plain and out into the sea. Caught up by the sun, carried by the clouds, it came down in rain to its place beside the other pool. "It's great, I'm going again." As it left it looked over at the other pool, which was covered with a green slime, and a stench was rising from its stagnant water. This time it found trees growing along its banks. Green grass and grain fields, fragrant flowers and beautiful gardens had sprung up, bringing happiness and prosperity to the people all along the way. [^4]

Then H. S. Putnam concludes:

And so the stream of life that flows out from the altar of sacrifice in the temple of the human soul will bring life and joy and beauty to human hearts all along the way. [^5]

Nor is adventure a value for young people only. General Douglas MacArthur kept the following quotation from Samuel Ullman on his desk between the pictures of Washington and Lincoln:

Youth is not a time of life; it is a state of mind; it is not a matter of rosy cheeks, red lips and supple knees; it is a matter of the will, a quality of the imagination, a vigor of the emotions; it is the freshness of the deep springs of life.

Youth means the predominance of courage over timidity, of adventure over the love of ease. This often exists in a man of sixty more than a boy of twenty. Nobody grows old merely by a number of years. We grow old by deserting our ideals.

Years may wrinkle the skin, but to give up enthusiasm wrinkles the soul. Worry, doubt, self-distrust, fear and despair — these bow the heart and turn the spirit back to dust.

Whether sixty or sixteen, there is in every human being's heart the love of wonder, the sweet amazement at the stars and the starlike things, the undaunted challenge of events, the unfailing child-like appetite for what-next, and the joy of the game of living.

You are as young as your faith; as old as your doubt; as young as your self-confidence, as old as your fear; as young as your hope, as old as your despair. [^6]

There is a faith that God has a special plan for each particular life. It is pure venture that we so live as to make His will possible through all our years on earth. Such a life will generally take the path of sacrificial devotion to a cause — a devotion of calculated risks, yet full of service, which Louis Pasteur summarized:

This ideal of having an adventurous faith in ourselves and the way we decide to live, is expressed by Henry C. Link:

Psychology now confirms and elaborates, by the methods of modern science, what Christ proclaimed through prophetic insight, namely:

That sportsmanship is more important than winning the game. That short-cuts to the abundant life destroy personality.

That the principle of expediency, namely, the end justifies the means, leads to mental and moral anarchy.

That playing the games of life according to the rules is more important than pre-occupation with the rules themselves.

That is to say, personality is the result of faith rather than of reason, of action more than of thought, of observing the laws rather than of interpreting them into new laws.

The faith of the individual in himself, in his fellow men, and in a higher moral order, we are discovering, is more important than all the knowledge which the natural sciences have given us. [^8]
