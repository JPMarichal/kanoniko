Magnanimity is defined as "a loftiness of spirit enabling one to bear trouble calmly, to disdain meanness and revenge."

This ideal is severely tested by (1) the injustices we sometimes suffer from others, (2) the jealousy we feel in their superiority, and (3) the prejudice that grows in our hearts from lack of knowledge and comprehension of other people and their problems.

Men pass many other tests and fail on this one. To "bear trouble calmly" — the injustices inflicted upon us by the meanness of others, our failures against their superiority, our prejudices — few surmount these obstacles with a "loftiness of spirit" that "disdains meanness and revenge."

The sum of the matter is, that more than the hurts we suffer, we wrong ourselves by nursing our resentments, destroying our good will, and losing our serenity and poise.

The test of true religion, as Christ portrays it again and again, is genuine brotherhood. And in magnanimity we have the severest test of brotherhood's meaning: to bear injury without seeking vengeance, to be slandered without returning in kind, to be wronged and refuse to "get even." Rather, to have a spirit so magnanimous that one strives to help the injurer, the vilifier, and the wrongdoer. This is what Christ meant when He said, "Love your enemies, do good to them which hate you."

Perhaps the greatest obstacle to achieving a magnanimous spirit is prejudice — plain ordinary prejudice against some group or race or nation or religion. One is impressed with how common this is in our lives, and yet how completely free from it was the life of Christ. The following incident in His life illustrates how He overcame customary propriety to take in all sorts of people

And it came to pass, that, as Jesus sat at meat in his house, many publicans and sinners sat also together with Jesus and his disciples: for there were many, and they followed him.

And when the scribes and Pharisees saw him eat with publicans and sinners, they said unto his disciples, How is it that he eateth and drinketh with publicans and sinners? ( Mark 2: 15-16)

Consider again the magnanimous spirit of the Savior in overcoming the misunderstandings and willful insults He suffered. He who exalted spirit over body was called a winebibber; He who prayed with such reverence, "Hallowed be Thy name," was charged with blasphemy; He who paid truth its greatest tribute, "the truth shall make you free," had said of Him, "he deceiveth the people;" he who was without ill will toward any man was called a servant of the devil.

Another example of the Master's magnanimous spirit is found in the total record of His life — that He never was angry at the wrongs done to himself. His indignation always arose over injuries done to others. When they spat on Him, mocked Him, scourged Him, and crucified Him, He did not show anger. Moreover, a life that collects and cherishes resentments is one of purest selfish egotism. "The plain fact is," writes Harry Emerson Fosdick, "that grudge-bearing sensitiveness is one of the meanest and most subtle forms of egotism. We may be selfish in doing positively unkind deeds, cherishing anti-social ambitions, indulging in financial niggardliness and greed, but we are just as likely to be selfish in displaying easily wounded vanity and pride. The supersensitiveness that is continually being hurt and, once hurt, irascibly cherishes a grudge; the bare nerve of self that waits only to be touched to writhe and, writhing, tingles with rancor toward the annoyer; the evil eye that watches with morbid fascination for slight and insult and, once insulted, finds happiness only in thoughts of getting even — all this is sheer egotism in its barest and most repulsive form.'' [^2]

One of the enemies of a magnanimous spirit is jealousy. Again we are indebted to Dr. Fosdick for his ability to hold a looking glass up to human nature. He writes:

Men commonly fail in magnanimity, not only in relation to their enemies, but to their rivals. Jealousy is the twin brother of vindictiveness. All of us deal with three types of people: folk less prosperous than we are, less able, less influential; equals, whom we easily meet upon a common level; superiors, who overtop and surpass us. These last constitute a critical moral problem. They are more learned than we are, more fortunate, more highly endowed, more charming, more influential; they get what we aspire to but miss; they are promoted more swiftly in the business office, are rated higher in the school, and are praised more in the market-place. How many folk there are who can live kindly with inferiors and amiably with equals, but who grow hard and envious as soon as they deal with folk who surpass them! [^3]

Wrath is cruel, and anger is outrageous; but who is able to stand before envy? ( Prov. 27:4)

Because youth is the time when the world begins to inflict iniquities and some cruelties upon all of us, the value of winning a magnanimous spirit early in life is imperative. Charles F. Wishart writes this about grudges:

I have known people who seemed to have their memories so filled with the petty and the inconsequential and the unworthy experiences of the past that they had entirely missed the great, beautiful, and enriching memories. Let me illustrate from my own college experiences. I knew a man who, every time I met him for years after his graduation, told me of how a misguided professor had mixed up his grades with the grades of another man with a similar name and had thereby unjustly, wickedly, and wantonly deprived him of certain college honors. He had filled his mind with that grievance until all the fine and inspiring college memories were blotted out. I knew another man who carried a bitter grudge through his entire life because he had once failed a Greek examination. It was doubtless very unfortunate, and perhaps the professor was blameworthy, but was it worth while for a grown man to load his memory with that lifelong grudge? In contrast with this I happen to know that a trustee of one of our Ohio colleges, a man of national and international reputation, was expelled from that institution — "kicked out," to use the euphonious campus phraseology — and proved a big enough man to realize that this discipline had been the making of him, and came back to put his time and money into the building up of his alma mater. [^4]

When Robert E. Lee was a cadet at West Point, a classmate took a violent and irrational dislike to him. The animosity persisted into later life. For many years this fellow officer made malicious attacks on Lee. One day a mutual acquaintance asked Lee what he thought of this individual. To the questioner's surprise, Lee spoke in the highest terms of him. Then his questioner said slyly, "I guess you don't know what he's been saying about you for years." "You have not asked me," Lee replied, "for his opinion of me. You have asked me for my opinion of him.'' [^5]

Dr. Fosdick writes this story:

In the course of the Armenian atrocities a young woman and her brother were pursued down the street by a Turkish soldier, cornered in an angle of the wall, and the brother was slain before his sister's eyes. She dodged down an alley, leaped a wall, and escaped. Later, being a nurse, she was forced by the Turkish authorities to work in the military hospital. Into her ward was brought, one day, the same Turkish soldier who had slain her brother. He was very ill. A slight inattention would insure his death. The young woman, now safe in America, confesses to the bitter struggle that took place in her mind. The old Adam cried "Vengeance," and the new Christ cried, "Love." And, equal to the man's good and to her own, the better side of her conquered, and she nursed him as carefully as any other patient in the ward. The recognition had been mutual, and one day, unable to restrain his curiosity, the Turk asked his nurse why she had not let him die, and when she replied, "I am a follower of him who said, 'Love your enemies and do them good,'" he was silent for a long time. At last he spoke, "I never knew that there was such a religion. If that is your religion, tell me more about it, for I want it." [^6]

His perfected philosophy teaches also that it is better to suffer wrong than to do wrong, and pray for our enemies and for those who despitefully use us. There are no other gospels or systems of philosophy that bear these marks of divinity and immortality. You may hunt the philosophies of the world in vain for any code of ethics that insures the peace and rest that may be found in His comprehensive, yet simple, gospel. [^7]
