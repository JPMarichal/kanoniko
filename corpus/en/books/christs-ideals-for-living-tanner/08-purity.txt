Purity

"Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God."

The ideal of the pure in heart means freedom from improper motive. The word "heart" means man's interior life, his whole personality. Jesus used the word in this meaning when He said, "For where your treasure is, there will your heart be also." ( Matt. 6:21) And again, He said: "Â�Out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaketh." ( Matt. 12:34) Paul defines the pure in heart as "those who are right with God." In other words, the pure in heart are those who with singleness of mind try to do God's will.

This broad and fuller meaning of "the pure in heart" includes the virtue of sexual purity, but it includes much more, as well illustrated from a recent book by Elder Albert E. Bowen:

Jesus extolled the pure in heart, declaring it is they who shall see God. No one can challenge their right to that supremacy. As an ideal for a human society it is not subject to question. If men's hearts were pure we should not have the reports of bestiality which come from the battlefields respecting the treatment of helpless prisoners. Neither should we have the oppression under which the inhabitants of the world groanÂ�

If men were pure in heart there would be no war at all either among nations, between classes or individuals, for there could be no covetousness, or greed, or seeking of advantage or desire to destroy. [^1]

The beatitude of this lesson, that the pure in heart shall see God, is considered as the "bright particular star" for many students of scripture, and this is true even though such a star is the most in-accessible-a pure heart and seeing God.

To the pure in heart all things are pure. With a pure heart one can see God and without a pure heart one cannot do so. Goethe said:

"The eye sees only so much as it brings with it the power of seeing." Some people looked upon Christ with impure hearts and reported they saw a winebibber, a glutton, a prince of devils. Others looked upon Him and found healing and the power to live a new life, one of them saying, "Thou art the Christ, the son of the living God."

The vision to see God can be one of life's great blessings. One commentary has enlarged upon the possibility of this ideal with these thoughts: "More likely he (Christ) meant that the pure in heart see God in the world about them when others are blind; that the pure in heart are aware of the moments of the Divine Will in their lives even in the midst of pain, when others are rebellious or despairing; that the pure in heart have by intuition the leading of God's spirit when others feel bereft; that the pure in heart have times of vision when earth and flesh fall away-Â�" [^2]

'Out of the mist into the light, O blessed gift of inner sight!' [^3]

"Galahad saw the Holy Grail, though others failed, because his heart was pure." [^4]

Blessed are they that have eyes to see. They shall find God everywhere. They shall see Him where others see stones.

-John Oxenham

This ideal may be considered with three thoughts: (1) Peace of mind, (2) the happiness of others, and (3) freedom.

1 . Peace of mind, a clear conscience, is a priceless possession and often quite unappreciated until too late. Henry Wadsworth Longfellow wrote:

Every man has a Paradise around him until he sins, and the angel of an accusing conscience drives him from his Eden. And even then there are holy hours, when this angel sleeps, and man comes back, and with the innocent eyes of a child looks into his lost Paradise again-into the broad gates and rural solitudes of nature.

Centuries ago Thomas a Kempis wrote what might be considered as contemporary words of all men everywhere:

The glory of a good man is the testimony of a good conscience. Therefore, keep your conscience good and you will always enjoy happiness, for a good conscience can bear a great deal and can bring joy even in the midst of adversity. But an evil conscience is ever restive and fearful.

Sweet shall be your rest if your heart does not reproach you. [^5]

Even so, it is important to understand that in the economy of nature our human impulses are not wrong of themselves. But they can easily be made so by the context of improper circumstances. The physical side of our nature is not evil, but may become so for lack of self-control. So, the fleeting thoughts that enter our minds, but not our hearts, are not blameworthy, nor should we grow morbid about them. But there is cause for concern when we give generous hospitality to evil thoughts, calling them back, sharing them with pleasure in our hearts. "For as he thinketh in his heart, so is he." ( Prov. 23:7)

Tennyson, in his poem "Guinevere," wrote these lines as a charge of loyalty to the conscience of the pure in heart:

I made them lay their hands in mine and swear To reverence the King, as if he were Their conscience, and their conscience as their King, To break the heathen and uphold the Christ To ride abroad redressing human wrongs, To speak no slander, no, nor listen to it, To honor his own word as if his God's, To lead sweet lives of purest chastity, To love one maiden only, cleave to her, And worship her by years of noble deeds, Until they won her; for indeed I knew Of no more subtle master under heaven Than is the maiden passion for a maid, Not only to keep down the base in man, But teach high thought, and amiable words And courtliness, and the desire of fame, And love of truth, and all that makes a man. [^6]

Phillips Brooks draws this following word picture, contrasting a lovely exterior with an unlovely interior:

What a painful contrast we often see where whatever can be done by the architect's hand, well paid, is done to rear the mansion, the palace; where whatever taste can do is done to collect all books and all pictures; where whatever the upholsterer can do is done to cover the floor with picture carpets; or where whatever the artist can do, is done to the ceiling to make it, if possible, as beautiful as the floor of heaven! But if the man who walks in the midst of all this treasure is himself selfish, carnal, grasping, hating, jealous, unlovely, the outward form of physical beauty stands in an eternal contrast to the walking homeliness of a man that has developed his animal life but not the spiritual, efflorescent higher life. [^7]

-Phillips Brooks

Sometimes people speak cavalierly of "puritanism," but impuritanism will never do. Ralph W. Sockman draws a realistic description of the problem:

There is something in the world which can take a lad as clean as yours or mine and harden his nature until, cruel and criminal, he goes forth a gangster. That something, call it by whatever name we please, is the old fact of sinÂ�Sin has social roots whose removal requires organized religious effort. Sin has cosmic implications which require right re- adjustment with the "Power not ourselves that makes for righteousness." Sin has a grip which can be loosened only by him who is "able to do exceeding abundantly above all that we ask or think, according to the power that worketh in us."

This is the point at which the religion of Christ supplements psychology. It puts us in touch with the "Power not ourselves which makes for righteousness." It seems that our baffled world is coming to recognize that some such power is essential. For this reason our society is open to see Jesus the Savior. [^8]

Peace of mind comes when one strikes bedrock on a few fundamentals: To say with Goethe: "One cannot always be a hero, but one can always be a man." Or with Amiel: "It is not what he has, or even what he does, which expresses the worth of a man, but what he is."

When Charles E. Hughes was running for political office on his anti-race-track gambling bills, his friends warned him: "The opposition will stop at nothing; they will defame your character; they will ruin you politically."

"Gentlemen," he replied, "there is only one man in this world who can harm Charles E. Hughes, and that man is Charles E. Hughes."

2 . The happiness of others. A second thought about the ideal of the pure in heart is more sobering: No man lives unto himself. The consequences of his mistakes and sins always run over the bounds of his own life to hurt others, especially those most loved and who love best-grieved parents, disappointed friends, or one betrayed.

Dr. Fosdick illustrates this terrible truth from George Eliot's book, Adam Bede: "Hetty Sorrel, pretty, vain, and superficial: Adam Bede, the stalwart carpenter; Arthur Donnithorne, careless, impulsive, well meaning, rich. You remember Adam Bede's honest love for Hetty and his wish to marry her, Hetty's ruin at the hands of Donnithorne, her hapless child, her frenzied wanderings. You remember the scene where Donnithorne, having tried desperately to make amends for what never could be mended, goes to Adam Bede and asks forgiveness. Well, Adam gives it, but it is not easy. 'There's a sort o' damage, sir,' says Adam, 'that can't be made up forÂ�' [^9]This is what youth rarely understands until too late-'a sort o' damage that can't be made up for.' Why? Because our wrongdoing always hurts another, sometimes far more than we ourselves are hurt."

The happiness of others hangs in the balance when a youth is pulled by two forces: the one is headlong impetuosity, without time to think, and without fear of consequences. The other force, the one that loved one's parents and friends hope may win, is a sense of honor, the powerful influence of good friends, home, good books, great music, church, and inward prayers.

Freedom is the reward achieved by those who are pure in heart-free from the binding power of habit, free from the tolling remorse of memories, free from a blindness that cannot see the divine in life, free from a callousness that leaves us dead to the benedictions of a pure heart. Such a freedom, like other freedoms, is never appreciated until too late.

So, many youths begin life with unspoiled characters and unsmirched reputations, but failing to appreciate them, throw them away. Then come the years of slavery. Sometimes the binding power of habit begins with headlong impetuosity, and sometimes with a careless "just once." It is hardly the wisdom of youth to fully appreciate what John Taylor once said. "No sin is small. No grain of sand is small in the mechanism of a watch."

"There can be no fulness of life where there is slavery," wrote President David O. McKay, "and a man who is subject to his passions is the most abject slave." And then he added this great eulogy: "He who can rule his passions is greater than a king."

It is so much easier to keep character than recover it when it is lost. Recovering lost character and lost reputation is a terrific struggle. We preach many sermons about the joy in heaven over one sinner who repents, one prodigal who comes back from the far country of sin, but perhaps we should preach more about the glorious chances and the freedoms of the boy and girl who never went away at all.

A final word on this high and difficult ideal might be this: "Meet the dawning day with determination to leave the past behind. Each dawn is a new world. Forget the past. Life begins tomorrow, as it always will-tomorrow after tomorrow." [^10]

My grandfather used to say to his family, "There is a line of demarkation, well defined, between the Lord's territory and the devil's. If you will stay on the Lord's side of the line you will be under his influence and will have no desire to do wrong; but if you cross to the devil's side of the line one inch, you are in the tempter's power, and if he is successful, you will not be able to think or even reason properly, because you will have lost the spirit of the Lord."

When I have been tempted sometimes to do a certain thing, I have asked myself, "Which side of the line am I on?" If I determined to be on the safe side, the Lord's side, I would do the right thing every time. So when temptation comes, think prayerfully about your problem, and the influence of the spirit of the Lord will enable you to decide wisely. There is safety for us only on the Lord's side of the line.

If you want to be happy, remember that all happiness worthy of the name is on the Lord's side of the line and all sorrow and disappointment is on the devil's side of the line. [^13]

-George Albert Smith
