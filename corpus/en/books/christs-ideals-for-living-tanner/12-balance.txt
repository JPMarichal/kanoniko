Obert C. Tanner, Christ's Ideals for Living ( 1955)

Balance

"I am come that they might have life, and that they might have it more abundantly."

There is a many-sidedness to the life and teachings of Christ. His ideals and values offer a fullness to life, an abundance, that gives joy and zest to living. Not all Christians have found this. Many have stressed one teaching and omitted or neglected others. Like a diamond with one facet ground too large, many Christians have been extremists, as the history of Christianity amply testifies. But Jesus would have us fulfill the purpose of our lives by giving attention to all the facets of our being. Far more than is generally supposed by Christian writers and Christian Churches, Jesus lived and taught the ideal of balance, right proportion, harmony, etc. True, He did not practice moderation in His love of God and man, as stated in the preceding chapter. This unbounded love is, however, the basis for a life that orders all other things in harmony and good balance. He recognized the many sides of life and the danger of giving attention to one while neglecting the other. Charles Edward Jefferson writes this characterization of Christ's life:

But when we come to Jesus we find ourselves in the presence of a man without a flaw. He was enthusiastic, blazing with enthusiasm, but he never became fanatical. He was emotional, men could feel the throbbing of his heart, but he never became hysterical. He was imaginative, full of poetry and music, seeing pictures everywhere, throwing upon everything he touched a light that never was on land or sea, the inspiration and the poet's dream — but he was never flighty. He was practical, hard-headed, matter of fact, but he was never prosaic, never dull. His life always had in it the glamour of romance. He was courageous but never reckless, prudent but never a coward, unique but not eccentric, sympathetic but never sentimental. Great streams of sympathy flowed from his tender heart toward those who needed sympathy, but at the same time streams of lava flowed from the same heart to scorch and overwhelm the workers of iniquity. He was pious, but there is not a trace about him of sanctimoniousness. [^1]

One of the qualities in the religion of the Latter-day Saints is this ideal of balance. In the Gospel plan all of life's legitimate claims are harmonized into the wholeness of a good life. There are no extremes. Religions throughout the world commonly fail at this point. They stress one point excessively, or deny another, so that the ideals of right proportion and balance are lost. One reason Latter-day Saints have achieved success here is in the nature of the Church organization. From all walks of life and with every possible experience, each member is called to a position of responsibility — both leading and serving, and everyone with the goal of Christ's life to follow.

Christ's teaching can be made to appear inconsistent by the precise and systematic mind. Actually these seeming inconsistencies are but two facets of a well-balanced or many-sided life.

On one occasion He said, "Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you restÂ�For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light." ( Matt. 11:28-30) Yet on another occasion He said, "Â�If any man will come after me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross, and follow me." (See Matt. 16:24) Jesus is making clear that discipleship to Him can give peace and be refreshing and exhilarating, yet it will also be costly and require self-denial.

He said, "Take therefore no thought for the morrow; for the morrow shall take thought for the things of itself. Sufficient unto the day is the evil thereof." ( Matt. 6:34) But He also told the parable of the foolish virgins who did not take thought about tomorrow, and concluded the story with these words: "watch, thereforeÂ�" (See Matt. 25:1-13) He is saying that we can be too much concerned about tomorrow and crowd out the things of today, yet we can care too little about tomorrow, and that, too, is dangerous.

He warned us, "Take heed that ye do not your alms before men, to be seen of themÂ�" ( Matt. 6:1) But He also admonished us, "Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good worksÂ�" ( Matt. 5:16) It is a warning to some men who like to show off their good works in pious exhibitionism. Others try to hide their talent for fear, perhaps, they may be asked for some sacrifice. And all of us may be guilty of both; now of one, later of the other.

In one sermon He said, "Blessed are the peacemakers:" ( Matt. 5:9) In another, "Suppose ye that I am come to give peace on earth? I tell you, nay, but rather division:" ( Luke 12:51) This appears as a frank recognition that in the clash of good and evil forces, peace will be impossible, and that peacemakers who work quietly for peace and those who may fight against unrighteousness are both blessed.

One is impressed with the clash of interests in His life, His wrath against those who injure others: the hypocrites who hide their indulgences, the dishonest who make money from religious services in the temple, the cruel who rob widows, the proud who thank God they are not as other men. Yet in another mood Christ is tender and helpful: "O Jerusalem, Jerusalem, thou that killest the prophets, and stoneth them that are sent unto thee; how often would I have gathered thy children together, even as a hen gathereth her chickens under her wings, and ye would not!" ( Matt. 23:37)

There is one foundation on which all Christ's teachings turn: that is His spirit. Here is a quality of life through which these ideals are made to harmonize with each other. This spirit of the Savior is more than one or all the virtues together. By such a spirit Jesus was able to hold all virtues together in perfect harmony.

He is a path, if any be misled; He is a robe, if any naked be; If any chance to hunger, he is bread; If any be a bondman, he is free; If any be but weak, how strong is he!

To dead men life he is, to sick men, health; To blind men, sight, and to the needy, wealth; A pleasure without loss, a treasure without stealth. [^2]

-Giles Fletcher

One catches some idea of the many-sidedness to Christ's ministry in His first sermon, delivered in the synagogue of His home town of Nazareth, when He told the people what He was going to do with His life. "The spirit of the Lord is upon me," He said, "because he hath anointed me to preach the gospel to the poor; he hath sent me to heal the broken-hearted, to preach deliverance to the captives, and recovering of sight to the blind, to set at liberty them that are bruised." ( Luke 4:18) Here is a gospel to the poor, to the rights of labor, sympathy for poverty, and regard for the natural claims of brotherhood. Here is a healing gospel. Here is an emancipating gospel, deliverance-sight to the blind, to "see the will of God upon the scroll of destiny" and so find peace. Here is a liberating gospel, "to set at liberty them that are bruised."

Make me a captive, Lord And then I shall be free, [^3]

-George Matheson

The ideal of balance, like all ideals, leads us on yet ever eludes our grasp. Who but the father of his country could merit a life summarized with such a delicate balance as this:

Washington, the brave, the wise, the good, Supreme in war, in council, and in peace. Valiant without ambition, discreet without fear, confident without presumption. In disaster calm; in success moderate; in all, himself. The hero, the patriot, the Christian. The father of nations, the friend of mankind, Who, when he had won all, renounced all, and sought in the bosom of his family and of nature, retirement, and in the hope of religion, immortality. [^4]

-Inscription at Mount Vernon

Yet who is not made sober by Alexander Pope's description of man's plight in the struggle to live well:

Know then thyself, presume not God to scan, The proper study of mankind is man. Placed on this isthmus of a middle state, A being darkly wise, and rudely great: With too much knowledge for the skeptic side, With too much weakness for the stoic's pride, He hangs between, in doubt to act or rest: In doubt to deem himself a god or beast; In doubt his mind or body to prefer; Born but to die, and reasoning but to err; Alike in ignorance, his reason such, Whether he thinks too little or too much: Chaos of thought and passion, all confused; Still by himself abused or disabused; Created half to rise, and half to fall; Great Lord of all things, yet a prey to all; Sole judge of truth, in endless error hurled; The glory, jest and riddle of the world! [^5]

-Alexander Pope

There is a two-fold opposition to a well-balanced life: (1) One is simply our own failure to determine the values we want in life, and how much effort each should receive. (2) The second is the social pressure that favors one or more of these values to the exclusion or neglect of others.

1 . The first, to determine what we want, should not be too difficult, yet few take time or effort to draw up a scale of values. Often a person has not given the careful thought necessary to decide distinctly just what he wants from life.

Most human beings would agree on these: Health, wealth, friends, knowledge, pleasure, beauty, and worship. To have these is to live abundantly, and to have a religion that contributes to them is to have a "more abundant" religion. Herein Latter-day Saints have done well. Theirs is a well-balanced religion in belief and practice. None of the above values are neglected. Nor is one value stressed out of all proportion to the others. "Whatever affects human welfare," said Elder Ezra Taft Benson, "has always been and ever will be the concern of the Church." This statement is a repetition of the essence and heart of the Gospel of Jesus Christ. That is, whatever affects human welfare — Jesus spent His life on this point — whatever helps people, human personality, is good, and whatever injures or hurts human life and its possibilities for happiness is evil.

It is not an easy matter to live a well-balanced life. Our trouble comes when our desires war within themselves and we allow one — sometimes the wrong one — to get control and dictate to the others.

For Latter-day Saints there is a priority of values. One always comes first: Advancing the Gospel of Jesus Christ at home and abroad. This means that when we see clearly the difference between a clear conscience and evil pleasures, between the Word of Wisdom and poor health, between independence and dependence, between selflessness and unselfishness, between understanding and prejudice, between helping and getting — we know which we ought to do and with God's help we propose to try.

2 . The second opposition to a well-balanced life is the social pressure to do one or a few things and let the others go. There is the one-sidedness of our vocation, our politics, and even our education. Oliver Wendell Holmes has written the following description of our disturbing situation:

The longer I live, the more I am satisfied of two things: first, that the truest lives are those that are cut rose-diamond fashion, with many faces answering to the many-planed aspects of the world about them; secondly, that society is always trying in some way or other to grind us down to a single flat surface. It is hard work to resist this grinding-down action. [^6]

Indeed it is hard work "to resist this grinding-down action." Yet that is the ideal of Christ's disciples. Jesus was no eccentric. His Gospel denies none of our righteous desires.

Yet there must always be two values of major concern, the health of our spiritual life, and the cause of God on earth.

His life was gentle; and the elements So mix'd in him, that Nature might stand up, And say to all the world, "This was a man!" [^7]

-William Shakespeare
