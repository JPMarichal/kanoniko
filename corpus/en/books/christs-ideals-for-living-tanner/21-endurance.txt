The bright ideal of patient endurance is seen only by the dark background of life's inevitable discouragements, disappointments, failures and suffering. Paul has expressed the ideal with immortal words: "I have fought a good fight, I have finished my course, I have kept the faith." Of all the virtues, none is more needed than unwearying patience and steady endurance. For it is true of us all, that "some night in his lifetime everyone comes home to find a new guest there-disappointment." Adversity has an appointment with all of us and she seems never to forget her appointments. And when discouragements come, and sometimes pile up before there is time to absorb them, it takes inner resources of considerable depth to overcome resentments and ward off bitterness.

It would make a long list to add up all the discouragements in Christ's life. He was accused of being a deceiver ( John 7:12); of being beside himself by His family ( Mark 3:21); of being a servant of the devil by His enemies ( Matt. 12:24); of being a blasphemer by religious leaders ( Matt. 9:3); of having ambitions for political power which He was compelled to deny ( John 6:15); of being a winebibber and a glutton. ( Luke 7:34)

Such was the continual slander and open hostility He met. Even though He devoted His life to the truth, preached and lived the gospel of love, defended the family, and stood for God's sovereignty-yet He had to endure continual misunderstanding and open abuse for His friendliness with others, and His life with God.

But even more, His disciples were slow to comprehend, two of them asking on one occasion, "Master,Â�Grant unto us that we may sit, one on thy right hand, and one on thy left hand, in thy gloryÂ�And when the ten heard it, they began to be much displeased with James and John." ( Mark 10:35-41)

It takes a lot of pluck to win a victory against odds. But it takes even more to sustain one disappointing failure after another over a prolonged period of time, and still keep faith and hope for the future. This is the test of life: not alone how much one can achieve, succeed, win-but how much one can endure of defeat, discouragement, outright failure-and still keep a fine spirit, come up smiling, and bear the losses with equanimity and poise and faith. This is the test of a man.

The Duke of Wellington, in a discussion of British victories over the French, told some French officers: "British soldiers are not braver than French soldiers, they are only brave five minutes longer." This was later born out at Dunkerque, which was described in a New York Times editorial:

So long as the English tongue survives, the word Dunkerque will be spoken with reverence. For in that harbor, in such a hell as never blazed on earth before, at the end of a lost battle, the rags and blemishes that have hidden the soul of democracy fell away. There, beaten but unconquered, in shining splendor, she faced the enemy.

They sent away the wounded first. Men died so that others could escape. It was not so simple a thing as courage, which the Nazis had in plenty. It was not so simple a thing as discipline, which can be hammered into men by a drill sergeant. It was not the result of careful planning, for there could have been little. It was the common man of the free countries, rising in all his glory out of the mill, office, factory, mine, farm and ship, applying to war the lessons learned when he went down the shaft to bring out trapped comrades, when he hurled the lifeboat through the surf, when he endured poverty and hard work for his children's sake.

This shining thing in the souls of free men Hitler cannot command or attain or conquer. He has crushed it, where he could, from German hearts.

It is the great tradition of democracy. It is the future. It is victory. [^2]

Of the many ways a man's endurance is tested, three are particularly severe: (1) When fortune is adverse, (2) When fortune smiles, and (3) When temper is lost.

1 . It is a common observation that "good starters and good stayers are not necessarily the same people." One has only to look at old high school and college yearbooks to be impressed with this fact. Some of the slow starters, after many years, are now out in front, while those promising ones with high expectations in earlier years are now doing nothing important. One hundred men start out in business at the age of twenty-five. After forty years the statistics are:

36 dead 54 dependent on family or charity 5 barely making a living 5 fairly well-to-do

But there are no statistics on the moral fibre of men and women who succeed or fail in personal character-no way to tell from pictures and outer circumstances whether one has made a success of life by those important inner circumstances of patience and magnanimity and quiet courage.

In his diary, Robert Louis Stevenson wrote:

For fourteen years I have not had a day of real health. I have wakened sick and gone to bed weary, yet I have done my work unflinchingly. I have written in bed and out of bed, written in hemorrhages, written in sickness, written torn by coughing, written when my head swam for weakness-and I have done it all for so long that it seems to me I have won my wager and recovered my glove. Yet the battle still goes on: ill or well is a trifle so long as it goes. I was made for contest, and the Powers-That-Be have willed that my battlefield shall be the dingy, inglorious one of the bed and the medicine bottle. [^3]

Then there is the need of enough endurance for a life of much routine and some monotony. Always the poet tells the story best:

Lord, of all pots and pans and things, since I've no time to be A Saint by doing lovely things, or watching late with Thee, Or dreaming in the dawnlight, or storming Heaven's gates, Make me a saint by getting meals, and washing up the plates. Although I must have Martha's hands, I have a Mary mind; And when I black the boots and shoes, Thy sandals, Lord, I find. I think of how they trod the earth, what time I scrub the floor; Accept this mediation, Lord, I haven't time for more. Warm all the kitchen with Thy love, and light it with thy peace; Forgive me all my worrying, and make all grumbling cease. Thou who didst love to give men food, in room, or by the sea, Accept this service that I do-I do it unto Thee. [^4]

-M.K.H.

Finally, every one comes to the time when one must walk in the absence of a loved one. Elizabeth Barrett Browning speaks for us all:

When some beloved voice that was to you Both sound and sweetness, faileth suddenly, And silence, against which you dare not cry, Aches round you like a strong disease and new- What hope? What help? What music will undo That silence to your sense? Nor friendship's sight, Not reason's subtle count; not melody Of viols, nor of pipes that Faunus blew: Not songs of poets, nor of nightingales Whose hearts leap upward through the cypress-trees To the clear moon; nor yet the spheric laws Self-chanted, nor the angels' sweet "All-hails," Met in the smile of God; nay, none of these. Speak thou, availing Christ!-and fill this pause. [^5]

2 . Many can withstand the frown of fortune, but who can stand before its smile? It is easier to win wealth and recognition than it is to be worthy of it. Many are fit to win success and unfit to use it. With learning and wealth and standing, there follows an irresistible train of evils-a proud spirit, the temptation of pleasures-drink and debauchery-loss of sympathy for the poor, and excuses to the calls of service.

A running commentary on success today is the parable of the builder who was not able to finish. Only "Â�he that endureth to the end shall be saved." ( Matt. 10:22)

Against such lack of endurance, consider this tribute by T. V. Smith of Abraham Lincoln, a man whom success did not ruin:

No man made great by death offers more hope to lowly pride than does Abraham Lincoln; for, while living, he was himself so simple as often to be dubbed a fool. Foolish he was, they said, in losing his youthful heart to a grave and living his life on married patience; foolish, in pitting his homely ignorance against Douglas, brilliant, courtly, and urbane; foolish in setting hemself to do the right in a world where the day goes mostly to the strong; foolish in dreaming of freedom for a long-suffering folk whom the North was as anxious to keep out as the South was to keep down; foolish in choosing the silent Grant to lead to victory the hesitant armies of the North; foolish, finally, in presuming that government for the people must be government of and by the people.

Foolish, many said; foolish, many, many believed.

This Lincoln whom so many living friends and foes alike deemed foolish hid his bitterness in laughter; fed his sympathy on solitude, and met recurring disaster with whimsicality to muffle the murmur of a bleeding heart. Out of the tragic sense of life he pitied where others blamed; bowed his own shoulders with the woes of the weak; endured humanely his little day of chance power, and won through death what life disdains to bestow upon such simple souls-lasting peace and everlasting glory.

How prudently-to echo Wendell Phillips-we proud men compete for nameless graves, while now and then some starveling of Fate forgets himself into immortality. [^6]

3 . Another test of endurance is evidenced by control of temper. It is said there may be an excuse to get angry-clear through-but there is never an excuse to lose one's temper. Said Wm. Fleming French:

Some of us feel it is a virtue to lose our tempers-to flare up and give battle. Somehow they feel it would be cowardly to be quiet and sensible about the thing, in spite of the fact that it takes more grit to sit tight and hold one's temper than it does to work up a hysteria.

But there is no virtue in fury-no sense and profit in it. An unrestrained temper can never be an asset. It is always a liability-a weakness.

So learn to sit tight, to smile when you want to rave. And you will be surprised at the self-confidence you will gain, and the victories you will win. [^7]

Many Americans have been mauled by some vile mob, but few, if any; have had the physical strength and the unconquerable will to face their enemies immediately following such indignities. One night as the Prophet lay beside a sick infant which he had adopted to help heal the sorrow from losing his first three children, a band of fanatics dragged him from his bed and tore his clothing from him. While some of the ruffians held him, one who was apparently more animal than human, spring upon him like a cat, lacerating his body with sharp finger nails; another of the band broke a bottle (perhaps filled with poison) upon his teeth. After that he was beaten, his bruised and scarred body was covered with a coat of tar and feathers, and the drunken mob fled from the scene. It is a sad but true commentary on the story of America that many such scenes can be duplicated in our history, but not its sequel: the remainder of the night was spent by the Prophet and his friends in cleaning the tar from his body, and in administering first aid to his scratched and acid-burned skin. But the next morning was Sunday and he was scheduled to preach. And preach the gospel of Christ he did, without sleep and in great pain; and his congregation was made up in part of members of the very mob which a few hours earlier had so abused him. Human beings the world over admire that type of physical and spiritual strength; they will be attracted to such a man as steel filings are pulled to a powerful magnet. [^8]

-Daryl Chase
