Freedom

"Ye shall know the truth, and the truth shall make you free."

Latter-day revelations contain a great deal more about the ideal of freedom than is found in ancient scripture. This is understandable to Latter-day Saints who accept the doctrine of continuous revelation, for one of the greatest issues of our civilization today is freedom. And nowhere in literature is the principle of free agency made so forcefully clear as in Mormon doctrine and teaching. President McKay said:

Next to the bestowal of life itself, the right to direct that life is God's greatest gift to manÂ�Freedom of choice is more to be treasured than any possession earth can giveÂ�Whether born in abject poverty, or shackled at birth by inherited riches, everyone has this most precious of all life's endowments — the gift of free agency; man's inherited and inalienable right.

Free agency is the impelling source of the soul's progress. It is the purpose of the Lord that man become like Him. In order for man to achieve this it was necessary for the Creator first to make him freeÂ� [^1]

One of the great insights on the ideal of freedom is found in the 58th section and 27th verse of the Doctrine and Covenants; "Verily I say, men should be anxiously engaged in a good cause, and do many things of their own free will, and bring to pass much righteousness." Indeed, this is the key to freedom-to be "anxiously engaged in a good cause." It is a paradox that true freedom is partly enslavement — men "anxiously engaged," devoted, loyal, engrossed, lost "in a good cause" — these are the ones who are truly free. For freedom of itself is dispersive, divisive, scattering, unorganized, this way or that, irresponsible, and undirected. Freedom and liberty by themselves will produce a selfish and irresponsible individual, doing what he pleases and trying for all he can get. Such a person thinks of his liberty as the right to be free from something — not for something. Freedom for something organizes a life with purpose. It puts things together. It liberates our latent powers and gives us direction and integration.

Patrick Henry said, "Give me liberty or give me death," but this means both for all too many. A young man grows restless with restraints and prohibitions and denials. He finally decides he is going to break loose from codes and rules and repressions. Then, with liberty to do as he pleases, he makes a mess of his life. Once free to choose, he becomes a slave to habit. Someone has expressed it: "There are only two stages in the life of a drinker: (1) when he could stop if he would, and (2) when he would stop if he could." This applies to all forms of emotional license. Discipline, control, resolve — these are the demands of loyalty and the keys to freedom. A youth may take the freedom of self-expression and with this alone find himself in "a far countryÂ�with riotous livingÂ�and the husks that the swine did eat." But with the other half of liberty, loyalty, he can become devoted to an ideal, a vocation, a family, the Gospel, and find the joy of true liberation. The final test is the loyalty that masters our life and gives it meaning. Our best use of freedom is to choose this loyalty; it is being free for and not from something. To be sure, we want to be free from the will and enslavement of others. But that is only half of the matter; we then must find some loyalty that makes us truly free — the cause of truth, the enlargement of mental horizons, the creation of beauty, and a community made better for all because we are living there. Someone else has defined freedom and liberty: "Liberty is the substitution of inner voluntary loyalty for outward constraint." Outward constraints fall away before the willing spirit of devotion to worthwhile goals.

The key to democracy is found in voluntary groups solving social problems instead of waiting for compulsory governmental controls. Democracy is cooperative groups — doctors voluntarily solving the medical needs of a community without the force of legislation; businessmen freely submitting to fair trade standards; lawyers keeping their profession on a high level by ethical standards voluntarily adopted — such is the society of free men, matching every freedom with a loyalty, every right with a responsibility.

A people who accept every freedom and liberty with a loyalty and a responsibility to match it, will be what Jefferson called "a safe depository of the ultimate powers." He wrote:

I know no safe depository of the ultimate powers of society but the people themselves; and if we think them not enlightened enough to exercise their control with a wholesome discretion, the remedy is not to take it from them, but to inform their discretion by education. [^3]

This statement by Thomas Jefferson is the reason taxes must be raised for education — good education — if our democracy is to survive in a world threatened by totalitarianism.

The ideal of freedom in Christ's life is of a spiritual freedom; the "inner voluntary loyalty" explained above. One catches something of this in the first word of Christ to His disciples. He said, "Come," not "go." He relied upon the inner spirit that is liberated with a testimony of the Gospel. He said, "Ye shall know the truth, and the truth shall make you free." Truth does free the human spirit, frees it from the bondage of superstition, from the misery of unwarranted fears, from the thraldom of life's many outward constraints. But more, truth frees our spirits to see new visions and new goals ahead. Truth lightens the step and brightens the eye of all who believe in the Gospel. They are free from a purposeless universe, and a life void of meaning; they are free from a wretched selfishness — they freely walk "in paths of lowly service," and divine companionship, which are the principles man knows at his best to be true and eternal.

A man is free when he faces life's responsibilities — not begrudgingly as compulsions and coercions and dire necessities — but with the gladness of a rare privilege and as a joyful opportunity. This spirit the Master summed up in these famous words: "And whosoever shall compel thee to go a mile, go with him twain." ( Matt. 5:41)

Freedoms have been classified into three types: economic, political, and intellectual. Consider each in turn, together with the way democracy, socialism, and communism would handle each.

Economic freedom is the right to own private property and use it to make a private profit. American democracy places one restriction on this freedom, that it be subject to government regulation as special need arises. Socialism and communism would take away the freedom to our own property, and give to the state the ownership of the means of production and distribution.

Political freedom is the right to vote for representatives who meet and pass such laws as they can agree upon and as the people are willing to support. Democracy and socialism grant this freedom. Communism denies it for the present but promises at some indefinite future time to give this right back to the people.

Intellectual freedoms are free speech, free press, freedom to assemble, freedom of worship, freedom of teaching and learning, etc. Democracy and socialism grant these, but communism denies them until some distant time when it is claimed they will be restored as the state "withers away."

Our concern here is religious freedom. However, we must be concerned with all the other freedoms, for if we lose our economic freedom to the state, there is a high probability that the men who have such great powers in their hands (economic and political) will be human enough to reach into the intellectual freedoms in order to assure their own power. And so our spiritual and intellectual freedoms rest back on the maintenance of our economic freedoms. Much more is at stake than the security of those with property, namely, all the freedoms that would eventually go, if economic freedom is lost. Human nature, and therefore, human history, is a commentary on Lord Acton's statement: "Power tends to corrupt, and absolute power corrupts absolutely."

This human frailty of love of power gives us pause whenever we see government grow and people become increasingly dependent upon it. Henry C. Link describes a typical decision that more and more Americans will have to make. He quotes a letter written by a young man:

Recently I learned that it would be possible for my mother to go on relief and for me to get aid enough to go to an engineering school full time. Some of my friends have told me I would be foolish not to take it. Even my mother favors it for my sake. What would you advise? I am very ambitious and want to succeed.

His reply to the young man was:

You have the most priceless ingredient of success in the world, namely, self-dependence. In an age when children are all too willing to depend on their families and families to depend on others, you have courageously struck out for yourself. You have the stuff that makes men great. As soon as you accept help from an outside source you sacrifice your intellectual and moral independence unless you can do it through a business-like loan or a scholarship based on merit. An engineering education obtained in this way, in my opinion, will not do nearly so much for your success as will the attempt to obtain such an education independently. I congratulate you on your difficult situation and your independence thus far. As you value your personality, your happiness, your success, hold fast to this priceless ingredient. Do not sell it for a mess of pottage. [^4]

One of the great statements on religious freedom is found in the 134th Section , Verse 4, of the Doctrine and Covenants:

We believe that religion is instituted of God, and that men are amenable to him, and to him only, for the exercise of it, unless their religious opinions prompt them to infringe upon the rights and liberties of others; but we do not believe that human law has a right to interfere in prescribing rules of worship to bind the consciences of men, nor dictate forms for public or private devotion; that the civil magistrate should restrain crime, but never control conscience; should punish guilt, but never suppress the freedom of the soul.

The importance of this revelation is the distinction it makes of the temporal and spiritual jurisdiction. The state is not to interfere in worship and matters of conscience, and "never suppress the freedom of the soul." God's sovereignty comes before the state, and if the state infringes upon God's laws, man's duty to God and His commandments should control.

Elder Adam S. Bennion has been one of America's great crusaders for freedom, having spoken to the leaders of this country in nearly every walk of life. The following quotation from him is worthy of much thought and discussion and action:

In the history of civilization, freedom has been enjoyed by relatively few men. That fact ought to make us, as Americans, prize it the more. But the mere having of it is no guarantee that we shall always keep it. It may be lost — as it has been throughout history — through:

Conquest or Bankruptcy or Complacency or Indifference or Moral Disintegration

The best way to keep it is to exercise it.
