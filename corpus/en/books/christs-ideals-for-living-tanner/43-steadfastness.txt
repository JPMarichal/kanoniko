Steadfastness

"Â�he steadfastly set his face to go to Jerusalem."

Gethsemane is a word of great meaning. It has come to be a symbol of trouble — deep trouble. And everyone, regardless of favorable circumstances, some day will know a Gethsemane. Sooner or later, to good and bad, unavoidable adversity comes to everyone. Thomas Carlyle wrote: "Every noble crown is, and on earth will ever be, a crown of thorns.'' [^1]

All those who journey, soon or late, Must pass within the garden's gate; Must kneel alone in darkness there, And battle with some fierce despair. God pity those who cannot say: "Not mine but thine"; who only pray: "Let this cup pass," and cannot see The purpose in Gethsemane. [^2]

-Ella Wheeler Wilcox

The problem is not whether we might escape adversity; the problem is how we might face it. Some meet calamity and come out of the experience irreconciled and embittered the remainder of their days. Others are numbed by tragedy and live through the experience and the years that follow in a mood of silent neutrality. Still others use adversity, and from it they emerge with vaster sympathies and stronger faiths. Trouble leaves them with greater trust in God and deeper spiritual insight. While it is true that "the deeper the sorrow, the less tongue it hath," yet some people, unable to explain their tragedy, accept it and use it in a way that leaves them kinder and vastly more understanding.

Jesus did not say, "I have explained the world." He said, "Â�I have overcome the world." ( John 16:33) One overcomes the world in a prayer like that spoken in Gethsemane, "Â�not what I will, but what thou wilt." ( Mark 14:36) That is, one overcomes the world when free from the hurts of men and adverse circumstances. One overcomes the world with the sincere desire that God's purposes be worked out in our lives. All our petitions in prayer must have such an ending. Jesus prayed earnestly, "Father all things are possible unto thee; take away this cup from me: neverthelessÂ�" It is when this last word is spoken, "nevertheless," that we overcome the world — praying for the things we want, asking God to do special favors, and then adding, "nevertheless." Such a prayer is not to "move the arm that moves the world," but a prayer that "the arm that moves the world can move us." It is not difficult to have faith and trust in God when all goes well, but it is altogether another matter to be steadfast in the face of mysterious tragedy. Countless people have been lifted up by the steadfastness of the Savior in that dark hour of utter discouragement and defeat, when hate was stronger than love, injustice stronger than justice, and unrighteousness stronger than righteousness-yet able in all its mystery to pray that if this be the will of God He would endure to the end.

Blind Milton, writing "Paradise Lost," had gained such a spiritual triumph over adversity.

I argue not Against Heaven's hand or will, nor bate a jot Of heart or hope; but still bear up and right onward.

Christ was realistic about the world's injustices and cruelties. Here are some of the estimates He gave His followers on the cost of discipleship, the price they would have to pay for living and teaching His principles of righteousness in an unrighteous world:

1 . Behold, I send you forth as sheep in the midst of wolves:Â�( Matthew 10: 16)

2 . Then shall they deliver you up to be afflicted, and shall kill you: and ye shall be hated of all nations for my name's sake. ( Matt. 24:9)

3 . They shall put you out of the synagogues: yea, the time cometh, that whosoever killeth you will think that he doeth God service. ( John 16:2)

4 . Then said Jesus unto his disciples, If any man will come after me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross, and follow me. ( Matt. 16:24)

Lord, must I bear the whole of it, or none? "Even as I was crucified, My son." Will it suffice if I the thorn-crown wear? "To take the scourge, My shoulders were made bare." My hands, O Lord, must I be pierced in both? "Twain give I to the hammer, nothing loth." But surely, Lord, my feet need not be nailed? "Had Mine not been, then love had not prevailed." What need I more, O Lord, to fill my part? "Only the spear-point in thy broken heart." [^3]

-Frederick George Scott

The ideal of steadfastness must be seen against the background of life's immeasurable tragedies. Here is the most difficult obstacle faith has to meet, this vast total of human misery. Besides hunger and suffering and fear, there is a staggering amount of tyranny and iniquity and inhumanity. The first thing to be said is that suffering is something to be faced frankly. It is an integral part of our world, hardly exaggerated by Paul when he wrote, "The whole creation groaneth and travaileth in pain together until now." ( Rom. 8:22)

Suffering is never understood on such a cosmic scale, however, as it can be where reduced to a single life. So Katherine Mansfield, a gifted author who died at the age of thirty-four, wrote in her journal: "I should like this to be accepted as my confession. There is no limit to human suffering. When one thinks: 'Now I have touched the bottom of the sea — now I can go no deeper, one goes deeper.'"

Edmund Burke wrote, "Never despair. But if you do, work on in despair." In his later years when he wrote that, he believed that the civilization he had worked so hard to build, was crumbling to pieces. He had just lost his adored son. And then he wrote: "Never despair. But if you do, work on in despair."

Who never broke with tears, his bread, Who never watched through anguished hours With weeping eyes, upon his bed, He knows ye not, O heavenly Powers. [^4]

-Johann Wolfgang von Goethe

"When Robert Louis Stevenson was a child," writes William F. Kosman, "already suffering with the illness which kept him confined to bed for so much of his life, there were times when he could not sleep at night. At such times his nurse would take him in her arms, carry him to the window and show him the lights burning in the other houses here and there across the darkness, and tell him that in all probability in every house where a light could be seen another boy was sick and unable to sleep, just as he. The lad understood and was comforted. If we but know it, all around us are others passing through the same experiences, wrestling with the same problems, striving for the same goalsÂ�" [^5]

Later in life, Robert Louis Stevenson summed up life's sorrows and then added: "I still hope; I still believe; I still see the good in the inch and cling to it." He put this courageous faith into these words:

To go on forever and fail and go on again, And be mauled to the earth and arise, And contend for the shade of a word and a thing not seen with the eyes, With the half of a broken hope for a pillow at night, That somehow the right is the right And the smooth shall bloom from the roughÂ� [^6]

Charles Clayton Morrison writes of noble men and women who bear their suffering after the spirit of our Master. "If it were not that we are all bound together in a human fellowship of suffering, I suppose no one of us would be able to face the griefs, the disappointments, the failures, the frustrations, the denials of hope which life brings. You suffer, yes. And I suffer, too. And if I see you gallantly bearing your cross, I am reproached for my weakness, my whining, my stubborn irreconcilability. It is your way of meeting your grief that gives me heart to meet mine. It is the fact that some men and women walk so bravely into the shadow and come out with radiant faces — it is this fact which gives the rest of us courage when our turn comes. This is the deepest cure for despair, of rebellion — this awakening of the moral chivalry that is in every soul. If I were a pastor I should study how I might stress this in my personal contact with sorrowing people and in my preaching, seeking to evoke that gallantry of spirit which initiates every sufferer into the glorious company of those who by bearing their own cross bravely have contributed to the salvation of us all.'' [^7]

What explanation may there be for a world of disease, drunkenness, poverty, lust, and war?

In all religious literature there are few theoretical explanations. The Latter-day Saint religion offers one that is satisfactory to an objective and inquiring mind. The Gospel reveals that man's mission in this life is to live through experiences of development and progress — to achieve a noble and God-like character. Could there be a development of great character in a world without struggle, without conflicts to wage for worthwhile ends, a world where courage and sacrifice are not required, a soft and easy life — would it produce the great characters like those who endure great suffering yet refuse self-pity, refuse to curse fate; who would not allow trouble to crush them and make them bitter and cynical, but rather face their troubles and bear them nobly until their lives are sweetened and hallowed — is this not to carry out our mission of achieving a nobler character? So our forebears around the campfires — not of picnics — but for loss of homes, with only their souls, their courage and integrity, and the stars above, gathered near to each other, trying to sing away their fears and suffering.

Why should we mourn or think our lot is hard? 'Tis not so; all is right! Why should we think to earn a great reward, If we now shun the fight? Gird up your loins, fresh courage take; Our God will never us forsake; And soon we'll have this tale to tell- All is well! all is well! [^8]

The Son of Man hath descended below them all. Art thou greater than he? (Doctrine & Covenants 122:6-8)

To the ideal of steadfastness in adversity, this must be added: Much of what we have to endure can and ought to be eliminated. There is much needless poverty, unnecessary disease, and may not wars be abolished by agreements of law binding on all nations? One must stand steadfastly before unavoidable calamities, but also stand against calamities that are intolerable and can be eliminated by those who steadfastly oppose them.

Therefore, dearly beloved brethren, let us cheerfully do all things that lie in our power; and then may we stand still, with the utmost assurance, to see the salvation of God, and for his arm to be revealed. (Doctrine & Covenants 123: 17)

So it is in life: when the winds of temptation and the waves of despair beat upon us, the eye of faith is turned more upon these raging elements than upon the Light of Life; the power of faith is thus weakened, and as Peter, we begin to sink. Too many, ah, too many go down beneath the billows; only a few cry out as he, "Lord, save us!"

And immediately Jesus stretched forth His hand, and caught him, and said unto him, "O, thou of little faith, wherefore didst thou doubt?" [^9]

-David O. McKay

Preparations were made by which we could pass through this mortal probation, receiving tabernacles of flesh, that we might go on to perfection. To bring about this change and to give to us the additional blessings, it was necessary that we also come in contact with pain, temptation and sin. In the world of spirits we could not suffer physical pain and understand the various conditions which are peculiar to mortal life. That we might have the knowledge of these things we had to experience them. The Father, therefore, through His Son, made known to us the plan of salvation. The full purpose of existence was explained to us in a grand council held in the heavens. We were informed that when we came to this present world we would have to suffer the ills of mortality. Among these ills we would be tried and tempted and only those who were willing to abide in the covenants of the Lord — which would be given us here — should have the privilege of returning again to his presence. [^10]

-Joseph Fielding Smith
