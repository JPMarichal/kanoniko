Self-Regard

All of us seek the approval and respect of others. Such a standard helps us in our determination to live well. But there is a higher ideal. It is to live so that we can respect ourselves. A good public reputation is satisfying, but the private one we hold of our inward life is far more difficult and far more precious. Respect for one's self is more searching and enduring than respect from others.

To live so as to avoid the condemnation of others is one level. To live so as to win the approval of others is yet another level. But to live with a deep self-respect that is in good working order all of the time — that is yet the highest level of a thoroughly genuine life which meets the standard taught and lived by the Master.

None but one can harm you, None but yourself who are your greatest foe; He that respects himself is safe from others: He wears a coat of mail that none can pierce. [^1]

-Henry Wadsworth Longfellow

Self-regard and self-conceit are opposites. The conceited man is already lost. But the man with true self-regard and self-esteem is one who has deep convictions about life's sanctities, purities, and fidelities. Such a person possesses the decisive element in all great character. One with deep self-respect feels there are some things in life too fine to be profaned, and rather than desecrate them it is better to sacrifice any gain of pleasure, or wealth, or worldly honor; or in some cases, life itself. He possesses a deep sense of honor in business, reverence for womanhood, loyalty to truth, love of freedom, fidelity to loved ones, friendships, Church, and God. To live such a life one must have ingrained in his character a consciousness of something deep within him, which must not be desecrated. We may call it a lofty self-respect.

Self-reverence, self-knowledge, self-control, These three alone lead life to sovereign power. Yet not for power (power of herself) Would come uncall'd for, but to live by law, Acting the law we live by without fear; And, because right is right, to follow right Were wisdom in the scorn of consequence. [^2]

-Alfred Tennyson

The ideal of self regard in Christ's life is exemplified supremely in the sacrifice on the cross. But there were other occasions. He said to Peter, "Get thee behind me, Satan," when Peter would dissuade Him from going down to Jerusalem to put His case before a misled people and the hostile leaders of that time. He said to His disciples when the love of power among the gentiles was discussed, "Â�for he that would be first shall be last." (See Matt. 19:30; 20:16; Mark 10:31; Luke 13:30.) He quickly resisted the temptations He faced at the beginning of His ministry. (See Chapter 3 .)

One is certain above all other things, that Jesus lived in a way that He was never ashamed, God saying of Him, "This is my beloved son in whom I am well pleased." ( Matt. 3:17; Mark 1:11; Luke 3:22)

It is the heart of the gospel that our inward life is the test of righteousness; not just telling the truth, but being sincere; not just keeping our temper, but keeping love in our hearts; not merely continence before marriage and fidelity after, but keeping our hearts free from improper motive; not just avoiding, but being genuinely modest; such genuineness of our inward self is the condition of all self-respect. And Jesus gave the key to His life and to our own salvation when He expressed His relationship to God: "Â�I do always the things that are pleasing to Him." ( John 8:29)

Consider this ideal on the three levels mentioned in the introduction of the lesson: (1) A self-regard on the level that avoids the condemnation and prosecution of the law; (2) A self-regard that is satisfied with winning the approval of others; and (3) A self-regard that can win the approval of ourselves.

1 . Someone has said that the greatest danger of this generation is a "law morality." That is, doing that which is just inside the law. While the laws of the land are all based upon fundamental moral precepts, they are far behind the ideals of Christ. One might say the laws of the land are last-ditch denials to those who would abuse their freedoms. And many live barely within this boundary and narrowly miss its penalties. They hazard other lives with speed on the highways if detection is unlikely. They intimidate to advantage where legal prosecution is difficult. They write contracts with weasel words, should later profit require their adoption. In summary, they live by a code of selfishness which the law will permit, and they are deaf to the call of service upon which the welfare of the country depends, and the character that Christ requires. They barely miss the condemnation and prosecution of the law. Their self-regard is not very high.

2 . Others keep a self-regard that depends upon the respect of others. Not just avoiding the condemnation of the law, they seek actively their approval. This is a much higher level. To live with public acclaim and favorable reputation is no mean achievement. To lose the regard of others is a terrible experience. Yet there is one more awful to lose self-regard. So a man may live with one's high social respectability as a success in business, yet be restive about injustices and inequities in the business world. A man may win applause in artistic achievement, yet know he is mediocre for lack of better effort. A man may win elections by promising abundance to all. A man may talk fluently of Christ to others and feel keenly the double edge of a knife that cuts back to himself. It is not the highest order of self-regard to live by the approval of others.

3 . To live with a high standard of self-regard that can win an honest approval of ourselves-that is another test altogether.

Who does not hear the ring of an inward bell with the prayer of an old Scotch weaver: "O God, help me to hold a high opinion of myself"; a high opinion of one's self that is born of a self-regard sense of the sacredness in every son and daughter of God; a self-regard that he would not hurt nor harm "even the least" and never rest so peacefully thereafter. Or to see truth lost for expediency, or justice buried with money, or might determining the right — to fight such battles against these, and in their defense to pledge with our forefathers "our lives, our property, and our sacred honor" — this is to be a true follower of Jesus Christ.

Jesus said we were to love our neighbor as ourselves. This is a wide grant of privilege to think about the health of our own souls. Tolstoy expressed a caution to those over-anxious about others to their own neglect, in these words: "Young man, you sweat too much blood for the world; sweat some for yourself firstÂ�If you want to make the world better you have to be the best you canÂ�You cannot bring the kingdom of God into the world until you bring it into your own heart first."

To hold such a high opinion of ourselves will require at least four steps: Self-knowledge, self-acceptance, self-organization, and relation of the self to the highest we know.

1 . Self-knowledge is important because by knowing our basic and primitive impulses such as vanity, jealousy, sex, anger, hunger, etc., we can detect that beneath the surface, reasons for our actions are sometimes these deeper and more elemental drives. It is important in harnessing the emotions to know about these tendencies so we can manage them better.

The vast claims of success through scientific management need to be matched by more success in self-management. We need to know more about our mental machinery, rationalizations, etc., if we are to control ourselves better and to help others. It is eternally true that good words and deeds, as Jesus said, do not come from a heart that is not genuinely good. "A good tree cannot bring forth evil fruit." We must everlastingly purify our motives, again and again-never taking them for granted, but humbly and frankly search them out and keep them loyal to the highest.

2 . Self-acceptance is necessary so that we do not lose our faith through disappointed dreams of the impossible. A man cannot hold a balanced self-esteem if he feels he is a failure. Unable to accept himself and his actual capacities and limitations, a man will seek various escapes — alcohol, day dreaming, wishful thinking, novels, movies, television, or even religion. Not all escape is bad. The test is whether, from our various escapes, we return to the realities of life with body and mind and spirit refreshed and ready for the common tasks that lie ahead. A person can hold a deep inner self-regard if he is able to achieve some measure of success of realistic goals within his abilities.

3 . The next condition of a high regard of self is to have a goal and to organize one's life to achieve it. Indecisive procrastination, putting off indefinitely the choice of vocation, suspending judgment on religious matters, waiting another year — all this lack of organization can eventually make a tramp, that is: one who has freedom without direction.

Every young man and woman should ponder carefully these famous words by David Starr Jordan:

Your first duty in life is toward your afterself. So live that the man you ought to be may, in his time, be actual. Far away in the years he is waiting his turn. His body, his brain, his soul are in your boyish hands. He cannot help himself. What will you have for him? Will it be a brain unspoiled by lust or dissipation; a mind trained to think and act; a nervous system true as a dial in its response to the truth about you? Will you, Boy, let him come as a man among men in his time? Or will you throw away his inheritance before he has had the chance to touch it? Will you turn over to him a brain distorted, a mind diseased, a will untrained to action, a spinal cord grown through and through with "devil-grass," "wild oats"? Will you let him come and take your place, gaining through experience, happy in your friendship, hallowed through your joys, building on them as his own? Or will you fling it all away, decreeing, wanton-like, that the man you might have been shall never be? This is your problem in life-the problem which is vastly more to you than any or all others. How will you meet it, as a man or as a fool? It comes before you today and every day. And the hour of your choice is the crisis in your destiny. [^3]

4 . Finally, one who would hold a high opinion of himself must relate himself to the highest — to a life of sacrificial service carried on by the standards of Christ and the help of God.

When the high heart we magnify, And the clear vision celebrate, And worship greatness passing by, Ourselves are great. [^4]

-John Drinkwater

It must not be said of this or any generation: "Every man has his price." It is still better to die standing than to live on one's knees as a slave. It is still better to live with a clear conscience than see strange faces in restless sleep. It is still better to speak the truth than to live in a honeycomb of lies.

But-and this is the sum of the matter — even if it did not appear as better, and the right for right's sake were losing ground on every side, we should still choose our loyalty to the standard expressed by Luther, who said: "Here I stand. I can do no other. God help me. Amen."

There is an incident told by Voltaire of a writer who excused his scurrilous work by saying: "My Lord, I must live," and received this icy reply: "I do not see the necessity."

A poem was written to answer this common excuse that a man must live:

"A man must live!" We justify Low shift and trick, to treason high; A little vote for a little gold, Or a whole Senate bought and sold, With this self-evident reply — "A man must live!"

But is it so? Pray tell me why Life at such cost you have to buy. In what religion were you told A man must live?

There are times when a man must die! There are times when a man will die! Imagine for a battle-cry From soldiers with a sword to hold, From soldiers with a flag unfurled, This coward's whine, this liar's lie, "A man must live."

The Savior did not "live." He died. But in his death was life — Life for himself and all mankind! He found his life by losing it! And we, being crucified Afresh with him, may find Life in the cup of death, And, drinking it, Win life forever more. [^5]

-Charlotte Stetson Gigman

It will not do to have a standard that keeps us barely within the bounds of social respectability; nor to play the game as close to foul play as we care. Nor is our standard the approval and plaudits of others. Our test is the approval of "The Father who seeth in secret," and our own self-respect. To gain this we must learn not to dally with temptations, but to repel them on every side with a fierce indignation born of a high self-regard.
