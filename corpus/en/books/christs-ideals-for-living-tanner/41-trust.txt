The ideal of trust is one that comes near to the heart of religion. It is important to understand the differences between religious faith as a belief, and religious faith as a trust. Belief is of the intellect, while trust is of the heart. Belief is not so profound a matter. It involves giving intellectual assent to some proposition, or affirming belief in certain ideas. Trust is another matter altogether. The inconsequence of mere belief is expressed in the scripture, "Thou believest that there is one God; thou doest well: the devils also believe, and tremble." ( James 2: 19) In other words, belief is not so difficult. True belief, of course, is very important, but one can have a shallow religion with true beliefs. Trust goes much deeper. Trust is that confidence and reliance on the creative power in the universe as one that is working towards a worthy end, and guiding our course of events in wisdom and goodness. One can believe in God, as Voltaire confessed his faith; "We salute," he said, "but we do not speak." Then there is a trust in God which says, "The Lord is my shepherd, I shall not want."

A lecture can be clear on religious beliefs, and clarity is important. But even if the class and the lecturer believed all the doctrines explained, there is yet a long way to go before the fringes of religion's deeper meaning are reached. That comes when one trusts a person in spite of adversities, and in spite of intellectual difficulties. Trust is able to say, "Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil: for thou art with me; thy rod and thy staff they comfort me."

The following hymn, perhaps the most popular in all Christendom, expresses the ideal of trust:

Abide with me! fast falls the eventide, The darkness deepens; Lord, with me abide; When other helpers fail, and comforts flee, Help of the helpless, O abide with me!

Swift to its close ebbs out life's little day; Earth's joys grow dim, its glories pass away; Change and decay in all around I see; O Thou, who changest not, abide with me!

I need Thy presence every passing hour; What but Thy grace can foil the tempter's power? Who, like Thyself, my guide and stay can be? Through cloud and sunshine, Lord abide with me!

I fear no foe, with Thee at hand to bless; Ills have no weight, and tears no bitterness; Where is death's sting? Where, grave, thy victory? I triumph still, if Thou abide with me.

Hold Thou Thy cross before my closing eyes; Shine through the gloom, and point me to the skies; Heaven's morning breaks, and earth's vain shadows flee; In life, in death, O Lord, abide with me! [^1]

-Henry Francis Lyte

One may have beliefs without much trust, as Voltaire had. But beliefs are important because they precede our trust. We could have no trust without some idea of the doctrine in which to place our trust. Yet our trust may far outreach our knowledge of doctrine. Indeed, many are not able to give clear explanations of the deep trust that is within them. In our sacrament and testimony meetings one experiences how inadequate are words to express our true and deepest meanings of praise and thanksgiving. Missionaries explain the Gospel well, not always from careful training and eloquence, but with a modesty and love that is shared-their trust that God lives, that He has ordained a new dispensation of the Gospel that will bring peace and joy and confidence to those who are willing to believe — and trust. It is one thing to believe in correct doctrine, but quite another thing to walk daily in a life of trust with God which these doctrines would explain.

One may be, as someone has expressed it, "as orthodox as the devil and just as mean." The test finally is whether one lives the gospel of love and trust in God and man. A favorite hymn sung by the Latter-day Saints reveals this ideal of trust:

Fear not, I am with thee, O, be not dismayed, For I am thy God, and will still give thee aid; I'll strengthen thee, help thee, and cause thee to stand, Upheld by my righteous omnipotent hand.

When through the deep waters I call thee to go, The rivers of sorrow shall not thee o'erflow, For I will be with thee, thy troubles to bless, And sanctify to thee thy deepest distress.

When through fiery trials thy pathway shall lie; My grace, all sufficient, shall be thy supply. The flame shall not hurt thee, I only design Thy cross to consume and thy gold to refine. [^2]

-Robert Kean

Christ lived the ideal of trust beyond all that words can express. At the bitter end He could say, "Father, into Thy hands I commend my spirit." And each day that He lived was one with God, departing frequently from His labors to seek quiet communion in prayer.

His promise that if we "Ask: it shall be given unto you; seek, and ye shall find; knock, and it shall be opened unto you;" means that we are to be receptive to God's blessings. Prayer is a mood of receptivity for God's will in our lives. It is also our power to achieve and endure.

One of the great passages in sacred scripture is the analysis of religious faith that gets to the heart of this whole matter of trust. The prophet Alma said, "Faith is not to have a perfect knowledge of things; therefore if ye have faith ye hope for things which are not seen, which are true." ( Alma 32:21)

Here the issue is stated on which all religion depends, namely, our hope and trust in "things which are not seen, which are true." Are they true? Should we believe only that which our senses can verify? Some reply affirmatively on this and are known as materialists. For some, no answer is possible. These are agnostics. And some reply with Alma's trust, "if ye have faith, ye hope for the things which are not seen, which are true."

We often say that seeing is believing; yet the unseen part of our lives adds up to a considerable total.

All our ideas are invisible. No one has seen an idea. And yet they are so potent in our lives that one person put it: "The greatest day in a man's life comes when he turns a corner and runs into a new idea." A single idea has been known to transform a man's life. Our western civilization is said to be based on three ideas: The Greek idea of balance, Christ's idea of love, and now the modern idea of scientific method. We Latter-day Saints would add a fourth idea: A Restored Christianity. The great difference between Plato and Aristotle was on the analysis of ideas, and the nature of ideas has formed the great divide in philosophy to the present. But all ideas are of the unseen world.

Like ideas, love is invisible — we see expressions of love, but no one has seen love itself. The poet tells of it best (on the lips of Marpessa):

And though the first sweet sting of love be past, The sweet that almost venom is; through youth, With tender and extravagant delight, The first and secret kiss by twilight hedge, The insane farewell repeated o'er and o'er, Pass off; there shall succeed a faithful peace; Beautiful friendship tried by sun and wind, Durable from the daily dust of life. And though with sadder, still with kinder eyes, We shall behold all frailties, we shall haste To pardon, and with mellowing minds to bless. Then though we must grow old, we shall grow old Together. [^3]

-Stephen Phillips

Faith is invisible; yet our faith is the star which guides us and gives us the strength to endure. Browning writes of one possessed by a noble faith:

One who never turned his back but marched breast forward; Never doubted clouds would break; Never dreamed, though right were worsted, wrong would triumph; Held we fall to rise, are baffled to fight better, Sleep to wake.

No, at noonday in the bustle of man's work-time Greet the unseen with a cheer! Bid him forward, breast and back as either should be, "Strive and thrive!" cry "Speed,-fight on, fare ever There as here!" [^4]

-Robert Browning

Hope is invisible. Yet it is so important as to be the one thing, perhaps, that has kept man from giving up the fight when failures were overwhelming.

[^5]

Personality is invisible. Besides our physical body which can be seen, there is our personality with its reasoning powers, memories, and imaginations; these are not seen. No one can see our goals in life, our purposes, our mind. And when our loved ones are no longer visible to us we do not think they are less real, for their personalities were always of the unseen world. True, we are accustomed to think that the test of what is real is what is seen. Yet all our cherished values in life are unseen — love, faith, hope, ideas, purpose, and personality itself.

We are trying to abide the celestial law of God; we are preaching the gospel of Jesus Christ, and endeavoring to carry out its principles. Now the question is, will it pay us to do so? Will it pay us to be faithful? Will it pay us to pass through whatever trials or afflictions, or persecutions, or even death itself, for the kingdom of God, for salvation and eternal life, the greatest of all gifts which God can bestow on the children of men? I say it will, and I hope that the Latter-day Saints, that all men in authority — that we will all be faithful before the Lord, that we will remember our prayers, labor for the Holy Spirit, labor to know the mind and will of God, that we may know the path to walk in, that we may obtain the spirit of the Lord and the Holy Ghost, and that we may overcome the world and magnify our calling till we get through this probation. [^8]

-Wilford Woodruff
