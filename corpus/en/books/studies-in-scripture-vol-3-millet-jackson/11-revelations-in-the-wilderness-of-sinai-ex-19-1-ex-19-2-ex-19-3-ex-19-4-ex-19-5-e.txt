REVELATIONS IN THE WILDERNESS OF SINAI (Exodus 19-40)

DAVID P. WRIGHT

Chapters 19-40 of Exodus are without doubt some of the most important in the whole of the Old Testament. They recount God's revelation of several very important units of legal and ritual prescription: the Decalogue (i.e., the Ten Commandments), 20:1-17; [^1] the "Covenant Code," 21:1-23:19; the prescriptions for the building of the portable sanctuary, chapters 25-31; and the so-called "Ritual Decalogue," 34:5-26. But chapters 19-40 consist of more than just a listing of revelations, one after the other, such as we find in the Doctrine and Covenants. The various units of prescription are set within a narrative frame which explains the events surrounding the several revelations. The main purpose of this article is to give an explication of this narrative frame. In doing so the focus will be on the message of the biblical text as it has come to us. Joseph Smith taught: "I believe the Bible as it read when it came from the pen of the original writers. Ignorant translators, careless transcribers, or designing and corrupt priests have committed many errors." [^2] Like Joseph Smith, I admit that the text of Exodus as we now have it has had a complex development, and diverse historical traditions have been incorporated into it. However, it is necessary to read it, as part of the exegetical endeavor, as the final editor appears to have intended it; that is, as a unified piece. [^3] This I will do here. Following this study of the narrative is a brief discussion of the laws presented in Ex. 19-40 and the layout of the portable sanctuary and its furniture. [^4]

The Revelations at Sinai and the Ten Commandments

19:1-3. The setting of chapters 19-40 (and also of Leviticus, and Numbers 1:1-10:12) is the wilderness of Sinai. The Israelites arrived in this area and set up camp at the base of Mt. Sinai (location unknown) on the first day of the third month after the exodus from Egypt (Ex. 19:1-2). As soon as they arrived there, Moses began a series of treks up the mountain to receive revelations. That Moses wasted no time in seeking God's revelations is indicated in vv. 2-3, which seem to demand the reading: "Israel camped there opposite the mountain, but Moses went up to God." [^5]

19:3-8. The first revelation Moses received at Sinai looked back to the exodus from Egypt and prepared the people for the revelations of chapters 19-23, which culminate in the covenant-making ceremony of 24:3-11 (19:3-7). God told his prophet to remind the Israelites of the divine deliverance from Egypt (vv. 4-5). This act of salvation is one of the main bases of God's covenant relationship with Israel, which he now sought to initiate in full. He told Moses to inform the people that if they obeyed his voice and kept his covenant, they would be God's own special treasure, a "kingdom of priests," a holy (i.e., set apart) nation, chosen from among all other nations (cf. Deut. 14:2). After this revelation, Moses descended and laid before the people what God had told him. The people responded that they would do whatever God commanded (vv. 7-8; cf. similar responses in 20:19; 24:3, 7).

19:8-15. Moses ascended the mountain a second time and gave God the people's answer (19:8). God then announced that he would reveal himself to Moses in a cloud so that the people would hear him speaking with Moses and so that they would trust in Moses' prophetic call (v. 9; cf. v. 19; 20:18-21).

At this point there is some difficulty in the text. The last half of v. 9 repeats essentially the last half of v. 8. Are we to understand that Moses went down after the revelation in the beginning of v. 9, declared it to the people, obtained their response, went up again and reported this second response to God (end of v. 9)? Or is the repetition at the end of v. 9 just to reiterate what already occurred (at the end of v. 8) so that further revelation could be given in v. 10, as if it were saying: "and what is more, after Moses returned the response of the people (referring back to v. 8), God spoke to him again" (v. 10)? Whatever the solution, in v. 10 God continued to speak to Moses to prepare the people for the coming theophany, the appearance of God to the people. [^6] Moses was to have them purify themselves for two days preceding the event and to have them launder their clothes (v. 10). When Moses actually instructed the people regarding these rules in vv. 14-15, he specified that sexual contact was also to be avoided in preparation for the theophany. Elsewhere in the legislation of the Pentateuch we learn that those with sexual fluxes are impure and in this state cannot have contact with things that are holy (i.e., the sanctuary and sacrificial meat; see Lev. 12; 15; 22:4-7; Num. 5:2-4; Deut. 23:10-12). [^7] God continued the instructions, saying that the people and animals were to be restricted so that they did not ascend the mountain at the time of the theophany. Anyone who did so was to be executed (vv. 12-13; on the danger of contacting the divine sphere, see below on 20:18-21). After receiving these instructions Moses descended and imparted them to the people (vv. 14-15).

19:16-19. On the third day after the Israelites began their ritual preparations, the theophany began (v. 16). A heavy cloud appeared on the mountain and the sound of a ram's horn was heard. This caused the people to tremble. Moses stationed the people at the base of the mountain (v. 17). The statement that "Moses would speak and God would answer him with a voice (or, thunder)" in v. 19 seems to be anticipating the revelation in 20:1-17. At any rate, that God and Moses spoke is a fulfillment of v. 9, which says that the people were to hear the two speak.

19:20-23. In v. 20, the statement that God descended merely reiterates the fact that God had already descended (cf. vv. 16, 18); that is, this was not a separate or second descent of God. It is repeated to set up the scene for summoning Moses to the top of the mountain to receive further revelation. The revelation he received at that point was still preparatory to the major revelation of chapter 20. God wanted Moses to be sure to warn the people so they would not break through to see God. If such were to happen, many would die (v. 21; see below on 20:18-21). God also wanted to make sure that the priests purified themselves (v. 22). The mention of the priests at this point is admittedly difficult. Priests had not yet been consecrated (cf. Ex. 28-29; Lev. 8-9), and according to the flow of the narrative, the Levites were not singled out as a priestly tribe until 32:25-29. To whom, then, is the text referring in its present context? One might think that it refers to Aaron's sons, who were potential priests. We note that Aaron's sons, Nadab and Abihu, were allowed with Moses, Aaron, and the elders to ascend the mountain in 24:1, 9. This indicates that before the bestowal of priesthood upon them they had a special position. Alternatively, one can identify the priests with the "servants" (or "young men") of the Israelites who offered offerings, a priestly duty (cf. Lev. 1-7), in 24:5. Jewish tradition understood the priests in 19:22 and the servants in 24:5 to be the firstborn males of the Israelites. [^8]

19:24-25. The command in 19:24 that Moses go down and bring Aaron up with him is obscure. It may be a command that Moses and Aaron appear on the mountain for the revelation that was about to take place in 20:1-17. But this seems somewhat difficult for several reasons: (a) After Moses went down in 19:25 there is no report of his going up to the mountain with Aaron for the revelation in 20:1-17. (b) After the revelation in 20:1-17 there is no statement that Moses (or Aaron) descended from the mountain. And (c) it seems that Moses was already among the people when the revelation of chapter 20 was over (cf. 20:18-19). Consequently, it may be better to understand the command to come up to the mountain with Aaron in 19:24 as anticipatory of the command to go up to the mountain with Aaron, his sons, and the elders in 24:1, 9. This means that Moses was among the people when the Decalogue, the Ten Commandments, were proclaimed.

20:1-20. With all the preparatory instructions in place, the revelation of the Decalogue (20:1-17) proceeded. [^9] This was a special revelation, because God spoke not only to Moses, but to the people as well. Deuteronomy makes this more explicit (Deut. 4:36; 5:4-5; 9:10). The natural reaction to hearing the voice of God was fear. [^10] At the end of the revelation, the people requested Moses to be the mediator of God's word. They asked him in the future to speak with God and then inform them of God's instruction so that they would not have to hear God speak, lest they die (20:18-19). This response of the people is considered a positive thing in the biblical context (cf. v. 20). [^11] Deuteronomy makes this more explicit. God responded to their plea (Deut. 5:24-27): "I have heard this people's request which they have made of you; everything they said is good. May their mind always be such, to fear me and to keep all my commandments all the time" (Deut. 5:28-29). The people's experience of hearing God's voice, it was hoped, would lead them to believe more firmly in God and in Moses' prophetic call (cf. Ex. 19:9, 19). It was also hoped that it would lead them to continual obedience (cf. 20:20; Deut. 5:29). But these expectations were disappointed. Israel fell into apostasy (Ex. 32; Num. 25:1-14), complained against God (Num. 11; 14:11, 27), and complained and rebelled against Moses' authority (Num. 12; 14:2-4; 16:3).

20:21-23:33. With Moses' role as the mediator of God's word decided, Moses alone ascended the mountain to receive further revelation while the people stood afar off (19:21). The revelation which he received covers 20:22-23:33 and has been termed by modern scholars the "Covenant Book" or "Covenant Code," from the term in 24:7 (in its strictest sense, the term Covenant Book refers to the laws in 21:1-23:19). [^12] Attached to the end of the laws proper is a passage delineating God's promises to and his expectations of the Israelites in connection with the conquest of Canaan (23:20-33). God would send his angel before the Israelites (vv. 20, 28). If the Israelites would be obedient to God's word through his messenger, he would wage war with the inhabitants of the land so that Israel could inherit it (vv. 21-23, 27-31; cf. Deut. 7:1-2, 17-24). The Israelites were to be careful, however, not to become involved in the idolatrous practices of the natives of Canaan. They were to obliterate their cult places so that they would not be a temptation (v. 24; cf. Deut. 7:5, 25-26). Moreover, foreigners were not to remain at all in the land lest they lead the Israelites into idolatry (vv. 32-33; cf. Deut. 7:3-4, 16-20; 20:15-18). As the Israelites served God, they would be blessed with food; they would not suffer sickness; no one would be barren and no one would miscarry; everyone would live a full amount of days (vv. 25-26; cf. Deut. 7:12-15; for a passage with a tenor similar to Ex. 23:20-33, see Ex. 34:10-16).

24:3-8. When Moses descended from the mountain after this revelation, the covenant ceremony anticipated in 19:5 and throughout the intervening chapters began. Moses came to the people and recounted to them all the "words" of the Lord and all the "laws" (KJV, "judgments") (24:3). The term "words" appears to be referring to the Decalogue, which is prefaced by the statement: "God spoke all these words" (20:1). The term "laws" appears to refer to the main body of the Covenant Code, which is introduced with: "These are the laws" (KJV, "judgments") (21:1). Hence what Moses recited to the people, according to the present context, was the Decalogue and Covenant Code. The people responded to this, as they did to the revelation preparatory to the covenant (19:8), that they would observe all that God commanded (24:3). After this, Moses wrote down all the "words of the Lord" (v. 4). According to the context, this must refer to everything he had just announced to the people, i.e., the Decalogue and the Covenant Code. Note that later God would also give Moses a copy of the Decalogue inscribed on stone (cf. 24:12; 31:18; 32:16, 19; 34:1, 4, 28; Deut. 4:13; 5:22; 9:8-18; 10:1-5).

Though the revelations were recited and the people pledged their obedience, the covenant ceremony was not yet complete. The next day, Moses built an altar at the base of the mountain and set up twelve stone pillars, representing the different tribes (24:4). He had some servants (or young men; see above on 19:20-23) of the Israelites offer burnt offerings and well-being offerings (also known as peace offerings; see Lev. 1 and 6:9-13 for the priestly prescriptions about burnt offerings and see Lev. 3 and 7:11-18 for the prescriptions concerning well-being offerings) (Ex. 24:5). Half of the blood of these offerings he threw on the altar (as was the rule with these sacrifices; cf. Lev. 1:5; 3:2; etc.) and the other half he saved in bowls so that he could throw it on the people after reading the book of the covenant (and here in the context this refers to the document he wrote in 24:4 which, as was determined, contained the Decalogue and Covenant Code) (24:6-8). If the altar is recognized as representing God, then this ceremony (which is unique in the Bible) symbolized the binding of the divine and human parties in covenant obligations.

24:1-2, 9-11. At this point the covenant ceremony was almost complete, but one important feature of the ceremony remained to be fulfilled. At the end of the revelation in which the Covenant Code was given, God told Moses to ascend the mountain with Aaron, his sons Nadab and Abihu, and seventy of the people's elders (24:1-2). This Moses and the others did after the blood ceremony (v. 9). Here, as representatives of the people, the group saw God (v. 10). As we know from our examination of the Exodus narrative already, coming in contact with the divine is risky business. It is therefore understandable that the text should remark that though these men saw God, he did not harm them (24:11).

As we examine vv. 9-11, we observe that their focus is the theophany. But the full significance of these verses appears only in the last words of v. 11: "And they ate and drank." This brief description seems to be that of a covenant meal (cf. Gen. 31:46, 54; Ex. 18:12). If so, this appears to be the final sealing of the covenant between God and the people's leaders.

24:12-16. After the theophany and meal, Moses was called farther up the mountain (v. 12; cf. v. 2). God said to Moses: "I will give to you the stone tablets, and the instruction and the commandment." The difficulty in this verse lies in discovering what was written on the tablets. One might think that the sanctuary prescriptions of chapters 25-31 were inscribed on them, since the statements about giving the tablets to Moses (24:12 and 31:18) enclose these prescriptions. But this is probably not the intention of our passage. According to the larger context of Ex. 34:1, 28 and Deut. 4:13; 5:22; 9:9-21; 10:1-5, the content of the tablets was the Decalogue. Furthermore, the phrase in Ex. 24:12, "and the instruction and the commandment," seems to refer to revelation beyond or separate from the content of the tablets. It is possible that this phrase refers to the sanctuary prescriptions. Thus v. 12 can be understood as making a distinction between the tablets and the sanctuary prescriptions. We must admit that by following this interpretation a problem remains in v. 12, which at the end says: "which I have written to instruct them." If the tablets and "the instruction and the commandment" are two different things, the verse as it now reads understands both of them to have been written by God. The problem is that there is no reference that God wrote the sanctuary prescriptions. One could understand the phrase "and the instruction and the commandment" as supplemental and, hence, parenthetical, not intended to be modified by the relative clause that follows. Otherwise, one can reject the foregoing interpretation of the phrase "and the instruction and the commandment" and say that it is an explanation of the tablets: "I will give to you the stone tablets, that is, the instruction and the commandment, which I have written to instruct you." However the phrase in v. 12 be understood, it is rather certain that the fuller context intends us to understand that the Decalogue was inscribed on the stone tablets.

The Forty-Day Revelation and the Golden Calf

24:13-31:17. Moses complied with God's command and ascended the mountain. Joshua, his servant (cf. 17:9; Num. 11:28; cf. Ex. 33:11), accompanied him part way up the mountain (24:13; cf. v. 18, where Moses alone entered the cloud). This mention of Joshua looks forward to 32:17, which mentions him again upon Moses' descent. The rest of the elders which were with Moses part way up on the mountain for the theophany were commanded to stay and wait there for Moses and Joshua to return from the mountain revelation. Aaron and Hur were to fill in for Moses in answering legal disputes while he was gone (v. 14). There is problem in the narrative at this point. At the end of this revelation, we find Aaron among the people in the camp making the golden calf (chapter 32), not part way up the mountain where Moses told the elders to wait. No mention is made of Aaron and the others going back down to the people after 24:14. Are we to understand that Aaron and the others disobeyed Moses' command to wait there for them and went back to the camp? Or are we to understand, taking a cue from Cassuto, that after the theophany and meal in v. 11 everybody went back to the camp, including Moses, and therefore when Moses said to wait "here" (v. 14), it meant to wait at the camp? Another solution is more likely because it is less speculative: "here" means at the bottom of the mountain, i.e., in the camp. That is, Moses did not mean that they were to wait on the mountain side, rather he was telling them in so many words not to follow him up the mountain.

Moses was to remain on the mountain for forty days (cf. 34:28; Deut. 9:9, 11, 25). While he was up there, the cloud of God was visible to all the people below (24:17). This comment is important for part of the significance of the story of the golden calf in chapter 32. It underscores the rebelliousness of the people's sin; though God's presence was visible to them—they knew he was watching and had a witness that he had not forgotten them—they apostatized! [^13]

31:18-32:6. At the end of the revelation of the sanctuary prescriptions and the giving of the stone tablets (31:18), but just before Moses descended, the scene of the narrative turns to the camp. The people had grown impatient in Moses' long absence. Therefore, they gathered against Aaron and demanded that he make them a god which would lead them on their way (32:1). Aaron's complicity and willingness in the making of the golden calf are hard to judge. Some have viewed Aaron as a rather unwilling partner in the request of the people. They say that v. 5, for example, where Aaron observed what the people were saying about the calf and consequently built an altar and proclaimed a feast to God, indicates that Aaron was trying to correct, to a certain degree, their apostate behavior. It seems, however, that the story in its present form wants to fully indict Aaron with the people. This understanding emerges from his explanation to Moses, in which it appears that he realized his guilt and tried to disassociate himself from the sin. When Aaron talked of the people's error, he cited their words verbatim from v. 1. But when he spoke about his own activity, he did not cite the description in vv. 2-4 but substituted a version so different that it sounds like the calf made itself: "I said to them: 'Whoever has gold, let them take it off.' They gave it to me. I threw it in the fire and out came this calf" (v. 24). Another indication of Aaron's full complicity is v. 25, which says that "Aaron had caused the people to go out of control" (cf. v. 6, 18). But despite this evidence which suggests that Aaron be judged guilty, it must be admitted that the text is not totally lucid on the intentions of Aaron.

In the context, the story of the golden calf can be viewed as a near complete reversal of God's expectations of the people: (a) They had received commandments not to worship any other gods but the Lord (20:3; 22:20; 23:13, 24, 32) and not to fashion images (20:4-5); but this they did in chapter 32. (b) While Moses was on the mountain receiving commands about the building of the sanctuary, part of which included asking the people to make a contribution of precious materials for the building of the portable shrine (25:2-8; cf. 35:4-9, 20-29), Aaron was asking for a contribution of precious materials to build the calf (32:2-4). (c) God was the one who brought the people out of Egypt (19:4; 20:2), but the people declared of the calf "Behold, your god, who brought you from the land of Egypt" (32:4). (d) The people had just celebrated a covenant ceremony where burnt and well-being offerings were offered at an altar Moses built, and where the leaders "ate and drank" (24:4-11). Here the people celebrated a festival to the Lord represented by the calf, offered the same type of offerings at an altar built by Aaron, and ate and drank. One feature they added was licentious revelry (32:5-6, 25; the verb translated in KJV as "to play" is found with a sexual connotation in Gen. 26:8; 39:14).

32:7-14. After the description of the people's sin in 32:1-6, the narrative returns to Moses on the mountain (vv. 7-14). God told him about the behavior of the people and that he planned to destroy them and make a mighty and numerous nation out of Moses' posterity (vv. 7-10). Moses, as intercessor, pleaded with God that he be lenient and not destroy them. Moses' intercession succeeded, and God changed his mind (vv. 11-14). A similar situation is found in Num. 14:11-25, where God was about to destroy the Israelites for rejecting the opportunity to conquer the land, and to make Moses a mighty nation in their place. Moses interceded and convinced God to change his plan. [^14]

32:15-24. Moses then descended the mountain and recognized the festive singing in the camp (vv. 15-18). When he saw the calf and the dancing, he cast the tablets to the ground and they shattered. This act represented the annulment of the covenant made previously (24:3-11). In the future, after God had forgiven his people, it would be necessary to rewrite the contents of the tablets on new tablets and reestablish the covenant (chapter 34).

32:25-29. Though God had changed his mind about destroying the people (v. 14), punishment was still necessary for those specifically involved in the sin. Part of the punishment was to be carried out by man. Moses rallied the Levites around him, told them to arm themselves and slay (presumably) the guilty persons without respect for their personal relation. Because of this act of valiancy, Moses declared that that day they had become dedicated to God, i.e., they had shown themselves worthy to serve God as priests (v. 29; cf. the same idiom in Ex. 28:41; 29:29, 33, 35; Lev. 8:33; 16:32; 21:10; Num. 3:3; Judg. 17:5, 12; etc.).

32:30-35. The next day Moses went up on the mountain to intercede further for the people. So set on obtaining forgiveness, Moses went even as far as to ask God to destroy him if he did not forgive the people (vv. 31-32). God responded that he would only destroy the persons who had sinned (v. 33; on individual responsibility for sin, see Deut. 24:16; Ezek. 18), and added a command that Moses now lead the people to the place he would show him (v. 34). The last two verses of the chapter (vv. 34-35) indicate that sometime later God sent a plague among the people as a punishment for their sin. [^15]

The Renewal of the Covenant

33:1-4. Chapter 33 begins with a repetition of the command for Moses and the people to be on their way to the land of promise (v. 1; cf. 32:34). Here is repeated, too, the idea that God would send an angel before them (33:2; cf. 32:34 and 23:20-23). But appended to this is the statement that God would not be going in the midst of the people lest he destroy them (33:3). This statement is important for understanding what will follow in chapters 33 and 34. These chapters largely deal with Moses' attempts to get God to agree to come back into the midst of his people as they traveled. God finally and fully conceded to this wish in 34:9-10. The people's response to the news that God would not go in their midst was one of sorrowful repentance: they mourned and did not wear their ornaments (33:4).

33:5-6. The message of the foregoing verses is repeated in a slightly different way in vv. 5-6. Moses was to tell the people that if God were to go in their midst, they would be destroyed. And here God commanded the people to take off their ornaments. This command seems to be in contradiction to the statement in v. 4: how can the people obey God's command of taking off their ornaments if they had already done so? The KJV sensed the contradiction and therefore added the conjunction "for" at the beginning of v. 5 (which is not in the Hebrew) and read the verb as a pluperfect ("had said") in order to remove the contradiction. Read this way, God's command to remove the ornaments in v. 5 temporally preceded the actual removal in v. 4. In the context of the Hebrew perhaps the command to take off the ornaments in v. 5 is to be understood as: "leave off your ornaments," meaning the people had taken them off on their own at first (v. 4), and now God was commanding them to leave them off. [^16]

33:7-11. The passage about the Tent of Meeting and its being set up outside the camp (vv. 7-11) is somewhat parenthetical, but it is of great importance in the context of chapters 32-34. God had just announced that he would not go in the midst of the people (vv. 3, 5). It is not until 34:9-10 that God finally agreed to come back in the midst of his people. Therefore, between these two points in the narrative God is conceived of as being outside the community. Now we learn elsewhere that God's presence was connected with the location of the sanctuary. In the sanctuary prescriptions God said that he would dwell in the midst of the Israelites (25:8; 29:45-46; cf. Num. 5:3); Num. 2:2, 17 place the location of the Tent in the middle of the camp (see also the location of the sanctuary and its appurtenances in the wilderness travels in Num. 10:13-28). Therefore, the story in Ex. 33:7-11 of the Tent of Meeting being set up outside the camp makes perfect sense here. Since God refused to dwell among the people, the Tent needed to be set up outside the camp. Later, after God's forgiveness was obtained and the covenant was renewed, it was set up inside the camp (Num. 2:2).

Admittedly, there is a major problem with the location of the passage about the Tent in vv. 7-11. In chapters 25-31 Moses received the plans for the Tent and its operation. But it was not constructed and set up until chapters 35-40. The story about the Tent outside the camp in 33:7-11 is therefore chronologically out of place. Perhaps in our contextual reading we are to understand that this was a separate Tent of Meeting, a precursor to the apparently more elaborate Tent of Meeting of chapters 25-31, 35-40. [^17]

33:12-17. Ex. 33:12 continues the issues raised in vv. 1-6 concerning God's presence among the people. Moses asked God whom he planned to send with him on the journey to the land of promise. God responded that his presence would go with them (v. 14). It is not clear if this means that God had now decided to come back into the midst of his people (34:9 may indicate that he had not) or if God was identifying himself as the angel who would go before the people. In any case, Moses responded that if God's presence did not go with them, they would not travel on (v. 15).

33:18-23. Moses had another request (v. 18). He asked God to show him his glory. Why he would ask this at this point of the narrative is unclear. Perhaps Moses wanted to be reassured of God's favor toward him (cf. vv. 12, 13, 16, 17, 19; 34:6). In any case, God agreed to Moses' request in principle; he would cause his attributes to pass by and would call out his name (v. 19). But Moses would only see the back of God at this time, since no one could see his face and live (vv. 20-23). This statement can be understood figuratively to mean that Moses would only see part of God's glory (i.e., his "back"), because he would not be able to endure his full glory (i.e., his "face"). God fulfilled Moses' request to see his glory in 34:6-9. [^18]

34:1-28. The scene was now set for the reestablishment of the covenant. This God initiated by telling Moses to prepare two stone tablets, like the first ones, and to bring them up to the mountain (vv. 1-4; note that here Moses prepared the tablets, whereas it was God who prepared the first ones; cf. 32:16). This Moses did. While on the mountain, God revealed part of his glory to Moses (vv. 6-8), and Moses made a final request that God go in the midst of his people (v. 9). That God agreed to this request is implied in v. 10, in which God announced that he was reestablishing his covenant with the people. Vv. 11-16, which have a content similar to 23:20-33 and Deut. 7, form a preface to the covenant stipulations that follow in vv. 17-26. [^19] At the end of the commandments, Moses was told to write these words (v. 27). The last half of v. 28 appears to say that he wrote these things on the tablets of stone. This is in contradiction with v. 1 on two counts: (a) The content of the former tablets (24:12; 31:18; 32:15-16), most probably the Decalogue of 20:1-17 (see above on 24:12), was to be written on the second tablets, but according to the context of 34:27-28, the version of laws in 17-26, different in many respects from chapter 20, were written on them. (b) According to v. 1, God was to write the content of the second tablets, but in v. 28, Moses apparently did. The solution of these problems on a contextual level is difficult but will be attempted. The last half of v. 28 is probably to be separated contextually from v. 27. If such is done, one can read the verb "he wrote" as referring to God. The "words of the covenant" which he wrote, though seeming to be the same as those of v. 27, would actually be different; that is, they would be the Ten Words (— the Decalogue of 20:1-17), as the very end of v. 28 says. Thus Moses himself wrote the one set of covenant words revealed in 34:17-26 on some other medium than the stone tablets (v. 27), while God wrote the Decalogue on the stone tablets (v. 28b). [^20]

34:29-35. Chapter 34 ends (vv. 29-35) with a story about how Moses' face glowed after the mountain revelation. Perhaps in the context we are to connect this phenomenon with the revelation of God's glory in vv. 6-9 (cf. 33:18-23); this glory had, so to speak, rubbed off on him. This glow, like other divine phenomena, frightened the people to the extent that they would not approach Moses (v. 30). Moses summoned them to return, but at first only Aaron and the leaders came back. After this the people gained courage and returned. Then Moses imparted to them the commandments he received on the mountain. This event parallels the imparting of commandments in 24:3. After he instructed the people, he put on a veil to hide his facial glow (v. 34). The account ends by saying that from then on Moses would wear a veil to hide his face. He would remove it only when speaking with God and when imparting God's word to the people (vv. 34-35).

The Construction of the Tabernacle

35:1-39:43. With the covenant renewed, the people could start to work on the Tabernacle, whose plans were previously revealed to Moses (chapters 25-31). Moses began the instructions to the people by warning them to observe the Sabbath day requirements (35:2-3; cf. 31:12-17). The position of this instruction indicates that the building of the Tabernacle, God's sanctuary, was not to take precedence over the Sabbath requirements; work on the sanctuary must not be done on the seventh day. The instructions continued with a call for contributions of materials for the construction (35:4-9; cf. vv. 20-29; 36:2-7; 38:24-31) and a call for those who had artistic abilities to offer their talents (35:10-19; cf. vv. 30-36:1). The various parts and pieces of furniture for the Tabernacle were then constructed (36:8-39:43) and Moses could proceed with erecting it.

40:1-38. God told Moses that the Tent was to be set up on the first day of the first month in the second year after the exodus (40:2, 17). Hence, the erection of the Tabernacle took place about three-quarters of a year after the arrival in the Wilderness of Sinai (cf. 19:1). After Moses set up the Tabernacle (40:2-8, 17-33), it was ready for dedication (40:9-11; the dedication is recounted in Lev. 8:10-11), and Aaron and his sons could be installed as priests (40:12-15; cf. Lev. 8; see also Ex. 30:22-33).

When the Tent was set up, God's cloud covered the structure (vv. 34-35). The end of chapter 40 (vv. 36-38) anticipates the departure of the Israelites from the Wilderness of Sinai (cf. Num. 10:11) by describing how God signaled when they were to travel on by the position of his cloud (cf. Num. 9:15-23). Before this departure could take place (according to the narrative at Num. 10:11, on the twentieth day of the second month of the second year), all the events and revelations in Leviticus and Numbers 1:1-10:10 occurred.
