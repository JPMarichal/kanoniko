James R. Clark, Messages of the First Presidency, vol. 6 ( 1975)

Introduction

Experience with the first five volumes in this series has shown that despite careful research and the cooperation of colleagues and archives staffs, additional Messages of the First Presidency have become available after a volume in the series has come from the press. In that sense the series has never contained a complete set of messages, although the goal of the compiler and the publisher was to reproduce in the series all available messages. In earlier volumes it was also necessary to acknowledge the existence of certain messages by date, title and source only.

In this volume, due to highly inflated printing costs, it has been agreed by the compiler and the publisher that certain messages will again be acknowledged by date, source and title or brief resume rather than reproducing the full message. Those documents, however, which in the judgment of the compiler are of major and vital importance have been reproduced in full as in past volumes.

The messages not reproduced deal in general with rather routine matters such as, for example, the notice announcing the regularly scheduled General Conference of the Church when those announcements contain little more than time and place. Annual announcements of the American Red Cross fund drive have been noted only by date, source and title when these messages were prepared by the American Red Cross, furnished to the First Presidency, and the First Presidency requested that the notice be called to the attention of the membership of the Church. In each case of omission, however, the message is listed in its chronological order by date, source of publication, and title or subject.

To serve the needs of scholars who may wish to examine the full set of messages as assembled by the compiler, copies of all messages so found for the period covered by this volume, 1935-1951, have been deposited in the Library of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints in Salt Lake City, Utah, and in the Harold B. Lee Library on the campus of Brigham Young University in Provo, Utah. There they may be consulted by the general public as well as by scholars with particular interests in the Messages.

This procedure enabled the compiler and the publisher to maintain the integrity of those messages fully reproduced without any editing and still allow the volume to cover the remainder of the administration of President Heber J. Grant and the administration of President George Albert Smith within a volume comparable with the other volumes in the series in size.

This volume covers a period of a little over sixteen years. During that time only four men served in the calling of a member of the First Presidency. President J. Reuben Clark, Jr., served as First Counselor and President David O. McKay served as Second Counselor to both President Heber J. Grant and President George Albert Smith.

As one reads the Messages in this volume and becomes aware of their style of expression, the immense and devoted support of these two counselors to the President of the Church becomes apparent. President J. Reuben Clark, Jr., drafted many of the messages of the First Presidency for this period, as his papers deposited in the archives in Salt lake City and Provo, Utah, attest. Of those messages delivered orally, many were read by President Clark or President McKay, especially during the last five years of the life of President Heber J. Grant when his health, after he had suffered a stroke, kept him from public address on many occasions.

President George Q. Cannon, who served as a counselor to three Presidents of the Church, wrote of this close relationship and harmony between the President of the Church and his counselors which was also evident during the period covered by this volume.

There are at the head of this Church, chosen by the Lord, three men, who constitute what is called the First Presidency of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. One is the President, the other two are his counselors. But all three are Presidents according to the revelationsÂ�. Can President Woodruff do without his counselors? I do not know what he can do, or what he might do; but I know that he does not do it. I know that President Young did not, nor President Taylor. I know that President Smith did not. He sought the counsel of his counselors. They acted in concert. And when the First Presidency act in power, they are a power. (Millennia! Star, September 5, 1895.)

Another of the great men of the Church with half a century of experience in sitting daily in the council meetings of the First Presidency, beginning with President Heber J. Grant, and recording their proceedings has written of that experience:

It has been an inspiring experience to listen to the First Presidency discuss the many weighty problems that have arisen over the years. It is a rare privilege to hear the wise discussions and witness the inspired decisions where questions and problems are presented, and to be touched by the spirit of each as his feelings on the matter are expressed. It is thrilling to know his Spirit is directing the minds of his servants, and to have the Spirit bear witness to my own soul that the decision arrived at is in accordance with the will of the Lord. These men, over the years, have been and are men with independent minds, men of faith, men with opinions of their own, men whose minds are not easily swayed. If Mormonism, so-called, were a false philosophy or church, it could not produce a group of men such as the General Authorities are and have been so far as my acquaintance with them over 50 years has shown.

These men are making scripture today. (Joseph Anderson, Prophets I Have Known, Deseret Book Company, 1973, p. 11.)

In the period covered by this volume the problems for decision by the First Presidency were numerous and many were weighty.

In 1935 the repercussions of the Great Depression had hit the United States and the world with full force. This had an effect on the Church and its membership. In 1936 the First Presidency, under the inspiration and revelation of God, launched the Church Security Program later to be known as the L.D.S. Church Welfare Program.

In 1938 there was deep concern in the councils of the First Presidency over the impact of educational philosophies alien to the philosophy and teachings of the Church. These alien philosophies were having their impact within the educational system of the Church, and the First Presidency spoke out at Aspen Grove in Provo Canyon to the leaders of the Church educational system.

Even before the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor in 1941 the First Presidency had begun to wrestle with the problems of a war-torn world, and this continued throughout the remainder of the period covered by this volume. In 1945 they spoke out strongly against "Universal Compulsory Military Training" for youth, listing sixteen main objections.

Restrictions on travel and fuel allocations during the war years caused internal procedural changes in the programs of the Church, including cancellation of attendance of the general membership of the Church at the annual and semi-annual conferences in Salt Lake City and the holding of restricted sessions for certain designated priesthood officers only in the Salt Lake Temple rather than in the Salt Lake Tabernacle.

The General Conference reports have been invaluable in providing insights in writing the historical notes for this volume. Archival papers of key General Authorities of the Church as they exist and are made available for research at Brigham Young University and in Salt Lake City have been invaluable for providing background for the messages in this volume.

One of the most helpful sources in giving background and interpretation to events in the lives of Presidents Grant, Smith, Clark and McKay is the book Prophets I Have Known (Deseret Book Company, publisher) by Joseph Anderson, secretary to the First Presidency and official clerk of the general conferences. His chapters on "President Grant-A Man of Strength and Faith" (pp. 15-56) and "President Smith, A Man Who Loved All Men" (pp. 97-116) should not be missed by anyone seeking to understand the messages contained in the present volume, Presidents Clark and McKay are also seen briefly but insightfully through the eyes of the secretary to the First Presidency.

On the question as to whether certain messages, especially those delivered orally by a member of the First Presidency, were the views of the First Presidency or those of the individual members of that council, Elder Joseph Anderson, who sat in their meetings for fifty years and heard all of their discussions, has written:

The question has occasionally been asked by latter-day Saints when the First Presidency has issued a statement to the Church whether they were setting forth their opinion or whether the Latter-day Saints should accept the pronouncement as the word of the Lord. In other words, would the members be out of harmony with the Lord's will if they acted contrary thereto.

Members of the Church, and others, have their free agency to accept or reject the advice of the First Presidency. There is no coercionÂ�. When these brethren unitedly give advice and counsel to the Church, whether it is over their signatures or by oral expression, they are speaking for the Lord. Those who think their understanding is better are at liberty to act as they see fit, but they must accept full accountability for their actions. (Prophets I Have Known, p. 10.)

As to my own reactions to these Messages of the First Presidency issued during these sixteen war-torn crucial and trying years, they leave me with utmost admiration and awe at the First Presidency's grasp not only of Church problems but of world-wide problems affecting the Church as well. They leave me with humble respect and valid testimony of the Spirit that the guidance offered was from the Lord, even though at times even some members of the Church, as Elder Anderson indicated, were slow to recognize the divinity of the message and give allegiance to it. The fact that certain instructions were repeatedly given over the years is indicative of the necessity for this constant educative process on the part of the Lord through the First Presidency.

Truly these were four great prophets, as great as any, perhaps, who have lived upon the earth. They were spiritual giants. They were shepherds of the flock who were thoroughly aware of the dangers to the flock both from without and from within. They were watchmen on the towers of Zion who failed not to raise the warning voice or give comforting advice and counsel to meet the needs of the sheep of the Master who had been placed under their care. Truly they fulfilled the injunction of the Master-"Feed My Sheep!" JAMES R. CLARK
