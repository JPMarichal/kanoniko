An Empire in the Desert-review of Church history, October 15, 1911

1911-October 15-Deseret News, November 4, 1911; also Oakland Tribune, October 15, 1911.

AN EMPIRE IN THE DESERT

At a time when the entire nation if not the whole civilized world, has its gaze fixed upon the west, and is about to witness another great exposition showing, among other marvels, the resources and attractions of the vast domain spreading from the Missouri river to the Pacific ocean, and from Mexico to the frozen Klondike, it seems eminently fitting that a leading newspaper of the Sunset Land should devote space in a special mammoth edition to the publication of articles reminiscent of the men by whom and the methods by which this mighty empire was added to the material possessions of the colossal and still growing republic floated over by the Stars and Stripes. Some of these men were "Mormons," or, as they termed themselves, Latter-day Saints; the founders of Utah, the pioneers and first settlers of the great American desert, redeemers and developers of the once barren region wailed in by the giant barriers of the Rockies and the Sierra Nevadas. The privilege of telling their story and our own in the columns of the Oakland Tribune, especially at a time like the present, is one that we greatly appreciate.

JOSEPH SMITH

The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, otherwise known as the "Mormon" Church, came into existence in the early part of the nineteenth century. Its founder was Joseph Smith, whose claim to divine direction and continuous revelation from God in the great work he set out to accomplish soon made him one of the most widely known and at the same time one of the most unpopular men in America. He was only a boy, however, a lad of 14-years, when his remarkable career began. A native of Vermont, born at Sharon, Windsor county, on the 23rd, of December, 1805, much of his boyhood was passed among the farming districts of Western New York, where he was living with his parents, humble, honest tillers of the soil, when he was called to play the part of a religious reformer, the prophet of a new dispensation.

THE "MORMON" RELIGION

The religion of the Latter-day Saints which the world calls "Mormonism," affirms, as the reason for its existence the departure of the present-day Christian churches from the principles of the original Christian faith, thus necessitating a restoration of the true gospel, with its spiritual gifts, and the powers of the priesthood, as enjoyed anciently. Mormonism's other and true name is the Gospel of Jesus Christ, now being preached in all the world as a witness in fulfillment of prophecy, before "the end" comes the end of wickedness, to be followed by a reign of right.

Joseph Smith's first vision for he was a seer, as well as a prophet and revelator was in the spring of 1820, while he was living at Manchester, a small village in Ontario (now Wayne) county, New York. He declared that, in answer to prayer, two glorious beings, in the form of man, appeared to him, and one, pointing to the other said, "This is My Beloved Son, hear Him." The boy's purpose in seeking the Lord was to inquire which of all the existing churches was the true Church of Christ, and he was amazed when told by the heavenly Oracle that the true church was not then upon the earth, but was about to be re-established, and that he was the human instrument divinely chosen to bring about its restoration. Belief in a personal God, who made man in his own image, as taught in the first chapter of Genesis, had long since disappeared from the creeds of Christendom, and was fast fading from the minds of both clergy and laity, when Joseph Smith declared that he had looked upon Deity, and that the Father and the Son were distinct personages, each in human form, thus restoring the lost knowledge of the true and living God.

AN ANCIENT RECORD

Three years later was revealed to him, in a similar way, an ancient record, a book of metallic plates having the appearance of gold, and containing a history of America ages prior to its discovery by Columbus. The revealer of these plates, who proclaimed himself a messenger from God, gave his name as Moroni, and stated that he was one of many prophets who, when in mortality, had ministered to a people called Nephites, a branch of the house of Israel, formerly inhabiting this land. The Nephites were the civilized ancestors of the degenerate Lamanites, or American Indians. The writings of these prophets, compiled and abridged by Mormon, the father of Moroni, had been buried in a hill anciently called Cumorah, in which place of deposit the youthful prophet, directed by the angel, discovered them. By means of interpreters Urim and Thummim found with the plates, he translated the hieroglyphic characters of the Nephite record, and the result was the Book of Mormon, published to the world early in 1830. The title page of the volume contains the testimony of three witnesses, Oliver Cowdery, David Whitmer, and Martin Harris, who solemnly aver that an angel showed them the plates after Joseph Smith had translated them, and that they heard a voice from heaven declare that the translation was by the gift and power of God. Eight other witnesses testify that they saw the plates and handled them while they were in the prophet's possession, prior to being reclaimed by the angel.

BOOK OF MORMON

The Book of Mormon may be termed the Bible of the western hemisphere. It is not regarded by the Latter-day Saints as a substitute for the Hebrew scriptures, but as a companion volume to that sacred book. They believe it to be the Stick of Joseph, and the Bible the Stick of Judah, referred to by the Prophet Ezekiel. The Nephite record relates how the Savior, after his resurrection, appeared to the inhabitants of this land and organized his church among them, after the pattern of the church founded by the apostles at Jerusalem, teaching the same doctrines here that he had taught there. The Book of Mormon is replete with predictions of the glorious future of America. "The Land of Zion," where the New Jerusalem is to be built by a gathering of scattered Israel, preparatory to the second coming of the Messiah, and the beginning of the Millennial Reign. It shows how such men as Columbus, the Pilgrim fathers, the patriots of the revolution, and the "Gentiles" of today-meaning the people of the United States and of European nations-have been and are being used by the Almighty to bring about the great consummation.

The Latter-day Saints-called "Mormons" for their belief in the Book of Mormon-are taught by that record and by other divine revelations to regard themselves as literally of the seed of Israel, children of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, lineally descended from Joseph of old, through the tribe or half tribe of Ephraim, who became mixed with the Gentiles, or children of Japheth, in the great dispersion, Ephraim is the first of the tribal families of Israel to be recalled and gathered out from Babylon in the last days, the dispensation of the fullness of times, the era of universal restitution spoken of by the ancient prophets.

THE WORD "GENTILE"

The word "Mormon," like the word "Christian," was originally a term of reproach, but the word "Gentile," applied by the Book of Mormon to Americans and Europeans in general, has no opprobrious intent. The "Mormons," who have come out from the Gentiles, and are of the same mixed lineage with many of them, look upon them as their co-partners in the work of the Lord. They hold that the Prophet Isaiah, when he declared that "in the last days the mountain of the Lord's house" should be established in the tops of the mountains," had in view, among other things, the present position of the Latter-day Saints, in the elevated valleys of the Rocky mountains; and when he described the chosen people as "flying upon the shoulders of the Philistines toward the west," he was foretelling Israel's gathering to "the land of Zion" by means of the steamship and railroads of the Gentiles.

LOYAL AMERICANS

The "Mormon" people have always been true and loyal Americans. The first Latter-day Saints, including Joseph Smith and his immediate associates, were descended from the Pilgrims and Patriots who founded this nation, and were nursed from infancy in the love of liberty, justice and equal rights, the basic principles upon which this government rests. All Latter-day Saints, whatever their nativity, are taught by their leaders and by their sacred books, to revere the Constitution of the United States as a divinely inspired instrument, which they and the friends of freedom everywhere are under obligations to maintain and defend, in order to perpetuate the principles upon which this republic was founded.

The "Mormons" term themselves Latter-day Saints, in contradistinction to the former-day Saints, or those composing the Church of Christ in ancient times. The modern church was organized on the 6th of April, 1830, at the little town of Fayette, Seneca county, New York. At that time "Mormonism" had a score or more of disciples, men and women who had accepted the teachings of Joseph Smith and Oliver Cowdery, the first and second elders of the church. They were its organizers, and the authority under which they acted was that of the Aaronic and Melchizedek priesthoods, conferred upon them by heavenly messengers. Six men participated in the organization, the law of the land requiring at least that number in any religious society. PREJUDICE AND PERSECUTION

Prejudice and persecution followed the preaching of the new religion-new yet old, for its tenets were those of the Bible, and had been held and practiced by the ancient Saints, though many of them had become obsolete in modern Christendom. The anger and hatred excited among the opponents of "Mormonism" kept pace with but failed to dampen the enthusiasm or destroy the faith of its adherents. From the hour of its birth the infant Church was harassed and oppressed by those jealous of its progress and under the strangest misconceptions as to its true teachings and real purposes. As might be expected from the character of its opening proclamation that affecting the status of other churches, which were held to be without divine authority, and lacking in knowledge of the true God and the fulness of the everlasting gospel "Mormonism's" earliest and bitterest opponents were professed ministers of Christ. Thundering from the pulpit their angry protests and fierce denunciations against "Old Joe Smith" and his followers, they misrepresented them in every conceivable way, poisoning the minds of their parishioners against them, slandering them in public and in private, publishing defamatory and hate-inflaming articles, instituting vexatious law suits against the leaders and members of the new religious body, and even instigating and leading lawless mobs in their violent assaults upon the persons and properties of the despised and execrated "Mormons."

RANCOROUS APOSTATES

Later persecutors were rancorous apostates, severed from the Church for their iniquities: unscrupulous, political demagogues, disappointed and revengeful from failing to control the "Mormon" vote to their own interests and aggrandizement; venal editors and publishers, cravenly subservient to popular clamor, pandering to prejudice, or intent upon exploiting the pecuniary profit an unpopular people and their religion; and finally the riff-raff of rascality in general, with special reference to that designing, hypocritical element so aptly described by Dr. Johnson in his trenchant and telling epigram. "Patriotism is the last refuge of a scoundrel." Such characters have sought from the beginning to make it appear that the Latter-day Saints are disloyal, that they hate the government of the United States, that they are continually plotting for its overthrow, and that they are an ignorant, degraded, vice-besotted community, reeking in corruption and crime.

IN OHIO AND MISSOURI

The pressure brought to bear upon the newly organized Church by some of these hostile agencies, compelled it, during the early months of 1831, to move bodily from Western New York and Northern Pennsylvania, where it had made its first proselytes, to Northern Ohio, which was then almost a wilderness. At Kirtland, on the shores of Lake Erie, "Mormonism" established its headquarters, and soon had two thousand zealous workers enlisted in its cause. A temple was built, and a more thorough organization of the priesthood effected. Schools were instituted for the study of theology and ancient and modern languages, and papers published in the interests of the rapidly growing community. Missionaries were sent to the various states of the Union, to the adjoining British provinces, and to England, where the first "Mormon" converts were made at Preston, Lancashire, in the summer of 1837.

Very early in "Mormon" history the state of Missouri, which was then on the frontier of the nation, was indicated by the prophet as the predestined site for the City of Zion, New Jerusalem, the precise place being at Independence, Jackson county, then neighboring on Indian Territory, the latter including the present state of Kansas. To Jackson county, therefore, in the summer of 1831, proceeded a "Mormon" colony for the purpose of "building up Zion."

A COMMUNAL ORDER

Preliminary to this undertaking, the Latter-day Saints, both at Kirtland and at Independence, entered into a communal order similar to the one instituted by the Savior's disciples at Jerusalem, as briefly mentioned in the Acts of the Apostles. "They had all things common" meaning their material possessions. The United Order, as it was termed, and as a basis the divine law of consecration; all private properties belonging to members of the Church were legally conveyed to the Church, which, in return, deeded an individual stewardship to each head of a family, who was expected to manage the same for the general good, all his gains reverting to a common fund, from which each steward derived his or her support, "every man seeking the interest of his neighbor, and doing all things with an eye single to the glory of God." This was the keynote of the United Order. The prophet taught his people that such a system had sanctified the City of Enoch in antediluvian days, and it was shown by the Book of Mormon that the same system had flourished among the Nephites for two centuries after the coming of Christ.

Misunderstanding the acts and motives of the new settlers, and instigated by sectarian priests and scheming politicians, the Missourians, at first friendly, began to look with distrust upon the "Mormon" neighbors. They were suspected of being Abolitionists, which was sufficient in itself to damn them in the eyes of slave-holding Missouri. It was also charged that their purpose was to join with the Indians across the border and drive the Gentiles from the land. Denial of these false accusations availed nothing, and in the autumn of 1833, by which time the "Mormon" colony in Jackson county had increased to about twelve hundred persons, mobs arose and drove them from their homes, pulling down or burning their homes, and despoiling them on the lands they had purchased from the government. Some of the leaders were tarred and feathered and many of the people whipped and otherwise maltreated. Out upon the bleak prairie, along Missouri's banks, chilled by November winds and drenched by pouring rains, hungry and shelterless, weeping and heartbroken, wandered forth the exiles. Most of them, after much suffering from exposure, found a refuge in Clay county on the opposite shore.

TO THEM STILL ZION

Seventy-eight years have passed since then, and though the dispossessed and driven people have never returned to Jackson county, yet that spot is still to them Zion, the place to which they or their children will eventually wend their way to rear upon its consecrated soil the city and temple of God. All other gathering places of the Saints, including their present homes in the region of the Rocky mountains, are merely "stakes" of Zion.

Following the Jackson county expulsion came a general migration of the "Mormon" people from Ohio to Missouri, where they settled mostly in Caldwell and Daviess counties, a new and unsettled region, purchasing lands, founding cities, establishing industries and improving and beautifying the country. Good order, sobriety and industry were manifest, and peace and prosperity prevailed. But all this must soon change. The old fires were still smouldering. The volcano only slept.

In the summer and autumn of 1838 political and religious animosity re-enacted on a larger scale the cruel tragedy of Jackson county. A war of extermination authorized and proclaimed as such by Lilburn W. Boggs, governor of Missouri, was waged against the Latter-day Saints. The strife did not cease until blood had been shed both in battle and in massacre, until cities had been sacked and pillaged; houses burned, fields laid waste and property of all kinds confiscated or destroyed. The "Mormon" leaders, condemned to death, were torn from the arms of their wives and children and thrust into prison. Finally the entire community, twelve thousand in number, after suffering the most terrible insults and indignities, were driven in the dead of winter from the confines of the state.

DRIVEN INTO ILLINOIS

The friendless refugees, fleeing by the light of their burning homes, leaving their crimson footprints upon the snow, were hospitably received by the state of Illinois. There they founded, on the site of a small village named Commerce, the beautiful city of Nauvoo, in Hancock county, on the eastern shore of the Mississippi. The place when purchased and settled by them was swampy and sickly, but it soon became, under effective drainage and other sanitary regulations, healthy and wholesome, a joy to all dwellers, a delight to all beholders. The population increasing, a large city arose, the largest in the state. Compared with it, Chicago was then a mere fishing village.

During their sojourn of seven years in Illinois the Latter-day Saints in Nauvoo and adjacent towns increased in number to twenty thousand. Many of these came in the tide of foreign immigration, which, in June, 1840, began to pour into Illinois, the first results of "Mormon" proselyting in Great Britain, Israel was beginning to gather from the nations. While the prophet, with the main body of the people, was building up Nauvoo and other towns founded by them in Illinois and Iowa, the apostles, with Brigham Young at their head, were laying broad and deep the foundations of the British mission. There, in 1840-1841, they baptized seven or eight thousand persons, and raised up branches of the Church in almost every noted city and town throughout the United Kingdom. They also established a periodical, "The Millennial Star," and published a new edition of the Book of Mormon, besides sending a thousand people to Nauvoo, and founding a permanent emigration agency.

HAD CORDIAL WELCOME

The kind-hearted people of Illinois, pitying the woes of the exiled "Mormons," gave them a cordial welcome. The legislature granted to Nauvoo a most liberal charter, under which temperance and peace prevailed, and toleration for all religious sects was secured. The Nauvoo Legion, mostly "Mormons," was organized as part of the state militia, with Joseph Smith as lieutenant-general. The Legion was specially designed as a protection against mob violence. The prophet was elected mayor of Nauvoo. There another temple was built, a university and other public buildings were projected, papers published, industries fostered, and the city beautified and extended on all sides.

EXODUS AND CIVIL WAR FORETOLD

In the midst of the prosperity that seemed assured, despite some mutterings of a coming storm, the prophet, on the 6th of August, 1842, uttered this prediction:

"I prophesy that the Saints will continue to suffer much affliction, and will be driven to the Rocky mountains; many will apostatize; others will be put to death by our persecutors, or lose their lives in consequence of exposure or disease; and some will live to go and assist in making settlements and building cities and see the Saints become a mighty people in the midst of the Rocky mountains."

As early as December, 1832, Joseph Smith had predicted the Civil war between the north and the south designating the cause-slavery -and the place-South Carolina-where the great conflict would begin. In 1844 he offered himself as a candidate for the presidency of the United States, including in his declaration or principles a proposition to abolish slavery through the purchase of the slaves by the general government with money realized from the sale of public lands. This was eleven years before Ralph Waldo Emerson proposed the same measure and received the plaudits of the civilized world for his wise and humane suggestion.

PROPHET AND PATRIARCH SLAIN

The year of his presidential candidacy was the year of the prophet's martyrdom. Much of the ill-feeling then manifested towards him and his followers by the once friendly people of Illinois was undoubtedly owing to religious rancor, but the greater part of it was the offspring of political jealousy. The "Mormons" in Hancock county held the balance of power between Whigs and Democrats, the two great national parties of that period. They generally voted with the Democrats, but sometimes supported the Whigs, when they deemed themselves mistreated by the other party. Disgruntled over this condition of affairs certain politicians organized "The Anti-Mormon Party," which proclaimed "a war to the knife" policy against the Latter-day Saints. The Anti-Mormons reinforced by apostates from Nauvoo, caused the murder of the prophet and his brother, the patriarch, and brought about the expulsion of the Latter-day Saints from Illinois.

SHOT BY A MOB

Joseph and Hyrum Smith were shot to death by a mob while in prison at Carthage, the county seat of Hancock county, awaiting trial on trumped up charges of "riot" and "treason." The basis of the riot charge was the abatement, as a public nuisance, by order of the mayor and city council of Nauvoo, of a vile and libelous paper called "The Expositor," established by apostates for the avowed purpose of bringing fresh troubles upon the "Mormon" community. The alleged treason of the "Mormon" leaders was the placing of Nauvoo under martial law, in order to defend the city against armed mobs that were threatening the destruction of the town and the massacre of its inhabitants. Willing to answer for their acts in the courts of their county, Joseph and Hyrum, with others, surrendered for trial, and while under the pledged protection of the executive of the state, Governor Thomas Ford the two brothers were murdered by a band of militiamen from Warsaw, who with the connivance of the guards, broke into the jail and accomplished their fell purpose. John Taylor, another "Mormon" leader, a fellow prisoner with the prophet and the patriarch, was savagely wounded at the same time. The mob was led by Levi Williams, a Baptist priest and colonel of militia. The murders, though well known, were never brought to justice.

BRIGHAM YOUNG AND WEST

Brigham Young now came to the front as the successor to Joseph Smith and under his masterly leadership and that of the stalwarts by whom he was surrounded, the "Mormon" people made their famous exodus into the all but unknown west. Their advance companies left Nauvoo in February, 1846, some of them crossing the Mississippi river on the ice. Dragging their heavily loaded wagon trains across the snow-covered and rain-soaked prairies of Iowa, the vanguard about the middle of June, encamped upon the Missouri river, the threshold of the wilderness, past the extreme western fringe of civilization. Beyond lay the broad plains where the savage red man roamed, and farther west the sunburnt valley and dry plateaus of the great American desert, mountain-wailed, desolate and forbidding. A few thousand Americans had made their homes along the fertile slopes of the Pacific in Oregon and California, but none had settled permanently in the arid region surrounding and including the valley of the Great Salt Lake. The only white inhabitants of this waste were a few hunters and trappers, living in lonely log forts with their Indian wives and half-breed children, acting as guides to the occasional emigrant trail or chance traveler to or from the western ocean. Into this desert and, coveted by none, shunned by all and denounced by Daniel Webster and other American statesmen as "a vast worthless area," uninhabitable, and unfit for cultivation came the expatriated "Mormons," the pioneers of inter-mountain civilization, the builders of an empire in the barren heart of the west.

THE "MORMON" VOLUNTEERS

First the "Mormon" battalion, five hundred volunteers, enlisting on the Missouri river, in the summer of 1846 as part of the invading force of the United States, which was then at war with Mexico, marched from Fort Leavenworth, by way of Santa Fe, to the Pacific coast. "History may he searched in vain for an equal march of infantry," was the comment of their commanding officer, Colonel Philip St. George Cooke, a Gentile, regarding this achievement of the "Mormon" volunteers. They helped to conquer and acquire for this nation, not only California, then comprising Utah and Nevada, but New Mexico, which also belonged to our sister republic, and included Arizona and other parts. Oregon at that time was claimed and jointly occupied by Great Britain and the United States. Many of the Battalion men, honorably discharged at Los Angeles at the expiration of their year's term of enlistment, immediately re-enlisted, at the urgent solicitation of Governor Mason, the United States military commandant, and remained to garrison San Diego. Their comrades set out to rejoin their families, left on the far away frontier. Some of these discharged soldiers, obtaining employment at Sutter's Fort, near the present city of Sacramento, were instrumental, while constructing a mill race, in digging up the first gold of California. It was their foreman James W. Marshall, a non-"Mormon," who pronounced the metal gold, but it was the picks and shovels of his "Mormon" workmen that brought the gold to the surface. It was also a "Mormon" who made the first record of the world-renowned discovery.

About the same time that the Latter-day Saints began to leave Nauvoo destined to be sacked and its temple desecrated and burned by mob force a "Mormon" company of two or three hundred souls, in charge of Samuel Brannan, sailed from New York on the ship Brooklyn for the bay of San Francisco. Doubling Cape Horn, they reached their destination in July, 1846. They carried with them farming implements, a printing press and all the paraphernalia for the founding of a new settlement. Landing at Yerba Buena (now San Francisco,) they settled in San Joaquin valley, putting in crops, building houses and establishing a newspaper The California Star, the second journal published in the Golden state.

ORGANIZING FOR JOURNEY

Meanwhile on the Missouri river, where the "Mormon" people had built Winter Quarters (now Florence, Nebraska,) Brigham Young and his associates were busy organizing the main body of their followers for the journey across plains and mountains to the shores of the Great Salt Lake. The wagons, drawn by ox and mule teams, and driven, in many instances, by women and children, were divided into companies of "hundreds," "fifties" and "tens," with a captain over each.

President Young, with several of the apostles, set out in advance with a picked company numbering 143 men, three women and two children. These pioneers, well armed and as well equipped as possible in those days of general privation, left Winter Quarters about the middle of April, 1847. Traveling up the north bank of the Platte, they broke a new road over the plains, a road that became known as "The Old Mormon Trail," and is now covered for several hundred miles by the roadbed of the Union Pacific railway. Their leader pointed out, as they journeyed along, the probable route of a great transcontinental railroad, from the Missouri river westward. It may be added that as early as 1852 the Utah Legislature petitioned Congress for the construction of both railroad and telegraph over the plains and mountains to the Pacific.

SALT LAKE VALLEY

The pioneers crossed the Platte at Fort Laramie, and the Rocky mountains at South Pass. The date of their arrival in Salt Lake valley was Saturday, the 24th of July. They had brought with them, in their covered wagons, plows, seed grain and a year's supply of provisions, and their first care, after thanking God for bringing them safe to a land of peace, was to plant the potatoes and garden seeds upon which they based their hope of a harvest. The ground was parched and burning, and more than one plowshare was broken in the hard, sunbaked soil. To make plowing easier, they flooded the earth with waters diverted from the mountain streams, and thus inaugurated the vast system of irrigation which has since done so much to redeem arid America.

President Young had been warned by Colonel Bridger and other mountaineers, met on the way, that it would be impossible to mature a crop in Salt Lake valley, and had been urged by Samuel Brannan, who came from the Pacific coast for that purpose, to pass by this barren land and join him and his colony in California. But Brigham Young knew better. His eye was upon the future, and his decision, "This is the place; here we will build the City and Temple of God," has been vindicated a thousand fold.

BEGINNINGS OF GOVERNMENT

Flinging to the breeze the Stars and Stripes, these colonizers took possession of a land lately conquered from Mexico, and after the treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, by which, in February, 1848, this region was ceded to the United States, they organized, pending the action of Congress upon their petition for a state government, the Provisional state of Deseret, electing Brigham Young governor, March 12, 1849. Congress denied the prayer for statehood, but organized, on the 9th of September, 1850 the Territory of Utah, with Brigham Young as governor, by appointment of President Millard Fillmore. He was reappointed in 1854 by President Franklin Pierce, and at the close of his second term was succeeded by Governor Alfred Gumming, Utah's first non-"Mormon" executive.

Just prior to Governor Gumming's installation occurred the exciting, but bloodless conflict known in Utah history as "The Echo Canyon War." President James Buchanan, misled by false reports, sent General Albert Sydney Johnston, at the head of twenty-five hundred troops, to put down an alleged "Mormon" uprising.

Knowing the reports to be untrue, and that investigation would prove them so, governor Young determined that the investigation should be had; in other words, that trial should precede execution.

MARTIAL LAW

He therefore put the territory under martial law and forbade the troops to cross the border. The date of his proclamation was September 15, 1857. The governor's purpose was not to defy the national authorities, but to hold in check Johnston's army, and thus prevent a possible repetition of the anti-"Mormon" atrocities of Missouri and Illinois. To enforce the proclamation he ordered the militia to fortify Echo canyon, the principal mountain pass leading to Salt Lake valley. The citizen soldiers were commanded to shed no blood, but to impede in every possible way the advance of the troops, and compel them to go into winter quarters east of the Wasatch mountains. The plan succeeded. Peace commissioners were sent by President Buchanan, all overt acts were pardoned and the affair was amiably adjusted.

Though no longer governor of Utah, Brigham Young remained the most influential man in the territory, and to the end of his days was the most conspicuous personality between the Missouri river and the Pacific coast. Under his wise and able leadership and that of his compeers, men of strong character and exceptional ability, the Latter-day Saints, gathering from all nations, penetrated by the preaching of their missionaries, redeemed the desert and made the wilderness to blossom. They were not, as their enemies asserted, "the off-scourings of civilization," but men and women of worth and respectability, representing all vocations and professions. They explored the country, placated the savage tribes, battled with crickets, grasshoppers and drouth plagues threatening the destruction of their early crops instituted and extended irrigation, reclaimed arid lands, built cities, established newspapers, schools and factories, and made the whole land hum with industry. The settlements founded by the "Mormon" people on the shores of the Great Salt Lake formed a nucleus for western civilization, greatly facilitating the colonization of the entire inter-mountain region.

TELEGRAM AND RAILROAD The Civil war, predicted by Joseph Smith, had been raging for six months, with the tide of victory running at first in favor of the southern confederacy, when the Overland telegraph line was completed to Salt Lake City. The first message sent over the wire-October 18, 1861, was signed by Brigham Young and contained these words: "Utah has not seceded, but is firm for the Constitution and laws of our once happy country." At the same time the "Mormon" leader offered to the head of the nation the services of a picked body of cavalry to protect the mail route from Indians and other enemies of the government, an offer graciously accepted by President Lincoln. Early in 1862, Utah applied for admission into the Union, an application repeated many times during the next quarter of a century.

SHRIEK OF LOCOMOTIVE

It was twenty-two years after the settlement of Salt Lake valley when the shriek of locomotive broke the stillness of the mountain solitudes, and the peaceful settlements of the Saints were thrown open to the encroachments of modern civilization. The change was welcomed, President Young himself, with other leading citizens, taking contracts and helping to construct the great iron highway. The welding point of the Union Pacific and Central Pacific lines for the road was built from both east and west was at Promontory, on the northern shore of the Great Salt Lake (May, 1869.) Utah's days of isolation were ended. Population increased, commerce expanded and a thousand and one improvements were planned and executed. Telegraphs and railroad threw a network of steel and electricity over a region formerly traversed by the slow-going ox team and lumbering stage coach. The mines, previously opened, but hitherto unprohibited, now began to pay; properties of all kinds advanced in value, and industry on every hand felt the thrill of a general reawakening. Tourists from east and west flocked to the "Mormon" country, to see for themselves the "peculiar people" and their institutions, trusting no more to the wild tales told by sensational traducers.

The founder of Utah lived to shake hands and exchange cordial greetings with President Ulysses S. Grant, the first president of the United States to visit the territory. This meeting occurred in October, 1875. About two years after, on the 29th of August, 1877, President Young died at his home in Salt Lake City.

THE TRUTH ABOUT POLYGAMY

He was succeeded by John Taylor, during whose presidency Mormonism celebrated its jubilee, with twenty-five nationalities represented in the great gathering commemorative of fifty years of earnest proselyting in various countries of the globe. It was during President Taylor's administration that the federal government under laws enacted by Congress in 1862, 1882 and 1887, began a rigorous prosecution of plural marriage, commonly called polygamy, which had been a tenet of "Mormonism" since July, 1843, when Joseph Smith, at Nauvoo, authorized it and entered into plural marriage relations, as did a number of his followers. The practice begun in Illinois was continued in Utah, though never at any time did more than 3 per cent of the Latter-day Saints engage in it. All, however, looked upon it as a divine institution, as the restored marriage system of the Hebrew patriarchs, and some of the best men and women in the community assumed its obligations and reared large families thereunder. But the government set its face against the institution and finally, after much suffering, during which many men and some women went to prison for the sake of their convictions, such proceedings being supplemented by the confiscation of the Church property, the "Mormon" people bowed in submission to the laws enacted against this feature of their religion and agreed in general conference that no more plural marriage should be solemnized under the sanction of the church. This, however, was not until the anti-polygamy legislation had been thoroughly tested in the courts and the highest tribunal in the land had declared it constitutional. President Taylor, who had gone into exile at the beginning of the time of trouble, died July 25, 1887.

AN ERA OF GOOD FEELING

The two most important events connected with the succeeding administration-that of President Wilford Woodruff were the issuance of the "manifesto" withdrawing Church sanction from the further solemnization of plural marriages, and the dedication of the Salt Lake temple. The manifesto, issued in September and refined by the general conference in October, 1890, was followed by an era of good feeling between "Mormons" and Gentiles. Utah had long been a battle ground, torn by dissension and ill will between the two classes of the commonwealth. Now they began to affiliate and were friendly as never before. Old political lines were obliterated; the People's party (Mormon) and the Liberal party (Gentile) were dissolved, and the citizens generally, regardless of past prejudices and affiliations, divided on national party lines as Democrats and Republicans. As an appropriate capstone to the temple of peace and patriotism thus reared, Utah, on the 4th of January, 1896, was admitted into the Union as a state. Further acts of grace on the part of the federal government were the pardonings of Presidents Harrison and Cleveland, in successive proclamations, of all polygamists and the return, by act of Congress, of the church property that had been confiscated.

Prior to the dedication of the Salt Lake temple, April, 1893, President Woodruff permitted and in fact invited a concourse of non-"Mormon" citizens, including federal officers and anti-"Mormon" editors, to pass through the sacred building and inspect its furniture and equipment; a privilege unprecedented and a courtesy much appreciated by the whole Gentile community. The temple, which had been forty years in course of erection and had cost nearly four million dollars, was dedicated with solemn and impressive rites-continued from day to day until thirty-one thousand Latter-day Saints had witnessed the ceremonies.

UTAH PIONEER JUBILEE

President Woodruff's final act of conspicuous note was the unveiling of the pioneer monument, the initial event of the Utah Pioneer Jubilee, a festival of five days' duration commemorative of the fiftieth anniversary of the arrival of the first white settlers upon the shores of the Great Salt Lake. He died in September, 1898.

Lorenzo Snow, who succeeded President Woodruff at the head of the Church, zealously devoted himself to the task of freeing it from the bondage of debt, a condition largely the result of the confiscation of its property under the operations of the antipolygamy statutes. President Snow was a natural financier and his efforts were crowned with a goodly degree of success. Not the least among important happenings of that period was the gratifying spectacle of "Mormon" and Gentile youths, sons of sires who had built up the state, eagerly responding to the call of the government of volunteers and standing shoulder to shoulder, fighting the battles of their country, or otherwise serving it, during the war with Spain. The record made by the Utah batteries in the Philippines under the command of Major Richard W. Young, and Major Frank A. Grant, the latter a non-"Mormon," is a page of history of which the American army has reason to be proud.

THE PRESENT STATUS

The present administration of the Church dates from the death of President Snow in October, 1901. The Latter-day Saints in all the world now number about half a million souls. According to the government census report of May, 1910, more than half of them, or 256,647, are in the United States, and mostly in the stakes of Zion, sixty-two in number, extending through the Rocky mountain region. A majority of the stakes are in Utah, the others in Idaho, Arizona, Wyoming, Colorado, Oregon, Canada and Mexico. A stake is a division of the church, organized in such a way as to constitute almost a church in itself, and each stake is subdivided into wards, also fully organized. A stake is usually the size of a county, though population and other conditions create exceptions. Each stake is presided over by three high priests, who, with twelve high councilors, constitute a tribunal for the adjudication of differences among Church members within their jurisdiction. Each ward has a bishopric of three, constituting a lower tribunal, from whose decisions appeals may be taken to the high council. The extreme penalty inflicted by these courts is excommunication.

Presiding over the entire church is a council called the first presidency, consisting of a president and two counselors, next to whom are the twelve apostles, equal in authority to the first presidency, though subject to and acting under their direction. Whenever the first presidency is dissolved, which occurs at the death of the president, the apostles take the government and reorganize the supreme council always, however, with the consent of the Church, whose members are called to vote for or against this or any other proposition submitted to them. The manner of voting is with uplifted right hand, women voting as well as men.

CONSENT OF THE PEOPLE

In addition to the general conference, held semi-annually, and the usual Sabbath meeting, there are stake and ward conferences, at which the consent of the people is obtained before any important action is taken. The special function of the apostles is to preach the gospel, or have it preached in all nations, and to set in order, when necessary, the affairs of the entire Church. They are assisted by the seventies, a great body of missionaries, presided over by a first council of seven. Among the general authorities is also a presiding patriarch, who, with his subordinates, comforts the people with sacred ministrations. Upon a presiding bishopric of three devolves the duty of receiving and disbursing the revenues of the church and otherwise managing its business, under the general direction of the first presidency.

The Church is supported by the tithes and offerings of its members; the united order, previously described, being in abeyance. The tithes are used for the building of temples and other places of worship, for the work of the ministry, the furtherance of education, the support of the sick and the indigent and for other charitable and philanthropic purposes. About two thousand missionaries are kept traveling abroad. They are unsalaried, and, whenever possible, travel "without purse or scrip," like the ancient apostles. While they are under the divine injunction to "preach the gospel to every creature," they have special instructions to baptize no married woman without the consent of her husband, and no child under age without the consent of its parents. The charge that "Mormon" missionaries make a special effort to convert women and bring them to Utah to become plural wives is a base slander, concocted and circulated by enemies of the Church.

BENEVOLENT SOCIETY

In every stake of Zion are institutions known as auxiliaries, such as Relief societies, Sabbath schools, Young Men's and Young Ladies' Mutual Improvement associations, Primary associations, and religion classes. The Relief society is an organization of women, having a special mission for the relief of the destitute and the care of the sick. An old folk's committee looks after the aged, entertaining them with free excursions, performances and exhibitions, regardless of sect or creed.

The Latter-day Saints, in addition to the liberal support given by them to the public school system, maintain a Church school system of their own, comprising the Brigham Young university and about thirty normal schools and stake academies. In these schools the Bible, the Book of Mormon and other sacred volumes are used as text books, and theology is taught, along with science, philosophy and the ordinary branches of learning. For the maintenance of its school system the Church makes an annual appropriation of over three hundred thousand dollars.

TEMPLES AND TABERNACLES

As already shown, the Latter-day Saints are a temple-building people. They have erected, up to the present time, six of these edifices, at an aggregate cost of between seven and eight million dollars. The first temple was built at Kirtland, Ohio; the second at Nauvoo, Ill., the third at St. George, Utah; the fourth at Logan, Utah; the fifth at Manti, Utah; and the sixth at Salt Lake City. A temple was projected at Far West and another at Independence, Mo., but they were not erected owing to the mobbings and drivings of the Saints.

The temples are not open to the public. They are for the performance of sacred ordinances, having in view the salvation of the living and the dead. The principal ceremonies are baptisms, endowments, marriages, sealings and adoptions. Much of this work, that in behalf of the dead, is of a vicarious character. With the Latter-day Saints there is hope of salvation for those who have departed this life without obeying the gospel, if they will yield obedience to its requirements in the other world, the place of departed spirits. The gospel will be preached to them by servants of the Lord who have entered into paradise, and they who manifest faith and repent there can be baptized for here, receiving in like manner other ministrations, to the end that they may be exalted and glorified. All, however, will be rewarded according to their works. The highest glory, according to our view, is attainable only by those who receive the gospel here, and are valiant for it, enduring faithful to the end; or those who would have done these things had they been upon earth when the opportunity was afforded. The Latter-day Saints marry for time and eternity, not merely until death parts husband and wife. Marriages performed under the civic law and by ministers of other denominations are regarded as honorable and effectual so far as relates to this life, but in order to be effectual in the life to come such covenants must be made for eternity, such unions must be formed according to God's law and under his authority, or they will have no force or effect hereafter. The family is the foundation of eternal glory, the nucleus of a kingdom without end. The husband will have his wife, the wife her husband, parents their children, forever, provided they secure them in the manner prescribed by him whose right it is to regulate all things pertaining to his kingdom.

THEIR SACRED CEREMONIES

Because of their Masonic characters the ceremonies of the temple are sacred and not for the public. But there is nothing disloyal in them, as so often asserted, nor in their performance is there the slightest departure from the principles of decorum and propriety. Within the building are halls, corridors, reception rooms, offices, chapels, priesthood assembly rooms, baptismal fonts, separate dressing rooms and bathrooms for women and men, sealing rooms, altars, paintings, statuary, magnificent mirrors, decorations and hangings, with such other furniture and equipment as may be found in the parlors of any palatial mansion.

Next to the temples in sacredness are the tabernacles, or places of public worship. The largest of these is the Salt Lake tabernacle, where may be seen and heard one of the great pipe organs of the world. This tabernacle, which will hold ten thousand people and is a marvel in its acoustic properties, was completed in October, 1867. The tabernacle choir, at the great choral contest in Chicago during the world's fair of 1893, bore off the second prize. The choir is now preparing to visit the east, to sing at Madison Square Garden, New York, during the National Irrigation congress.

In conclusion, let us say, as representing the body of religious worshipers over whom we have the honor to preside: We cherish none but sentiments of good will toward mankind. We regard all men as our brothers, children of the universal Father, and for their benefit and for his honor and glory we and our fellow servants are laboring, and expect to labor to the close of our mortal lives; passing hence to continue, in the world of spirits, the work of salvation inaugurated here. Peace be unto all, in the name of our Lord and Savior! JOSEPH F. SMITH, ANTHON H. LUND, JOHN HENRY SMITH, First Presidency of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints.
