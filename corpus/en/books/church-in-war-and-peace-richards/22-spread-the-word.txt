Stephen L Richards, Church in War and Peace ( 1943)

CHAPTER XX

SPREAD THE WORD

I think that most of you will concede that if the principles of true Christianity could be applied throughout the world the prospect for the settlement of all problems would be good indeed. Some there are, undoubtedly, who believe that the spiritual persuasive force of the gospel would never be sufficient to bring under control the selfish, ambitious, and perverse natures of men, but even these who doubt the potency of spiritual power to achieve the desired end will admit that the gospel plan of mutual consideration and good will is theoretically, at least, the most desirable formula for happy human relations.

There are those also who profess to believe that the Christian doctrine is basically wrong and that its fundamental philosophy, that of humility, is indicative of weakness. They find in it no provision for a master race and no tolerance for bondage and serfdom and dictatorship. They are therefore against the Christian concept and are seeking to break its influence in all nations.

We are against the people who hold these ideas and we are in a war with them largely to determine whether their egotistical, self-sufficient, superior race ideology, or the Christian concept of the equality of men in the family of God shall dominate the world.

Our opponents have carried on for a number of years an intense propaganda for their ideology, and so successful have they been that they have seemingly crushed the fine sentiments inspired by the Christian doctrine in millions of their people. This brings me to the point I would like to discuss with you. I have resolved the matter into three questions: First, to what extent is Christian apathy responsible for this counter-ideology; second, have the Christian doctrines been adequately interpreted; and, third, is there a remedial program which could be effective?

I think that the indifference of men professing Christianity has been a very important factor in contributing to the regrettable circumstances I have mentioned, and I think, too, that indifference has developed, in good measure at least, because men have come to be ashamed of the gospel of Christ. Such a condition must have prevailed in the earliest days of Christianity for Paul said to the Romans: "I am not ashamed of the gospel of Christ; for it is the power of God unto salvation to everyone that believeth." (Romans 1:16) Now I do not mean that many men are ashamed to be called Christians. Most are proud to be referred to as Christian gentlemen, but there is a disposition to appropriate Christian virtues without giving proper credit to their source. This is illustrated, as I have mentioned on a previous occasion, in the altruistic slogans of the service clubs and other organizations which have come to have an appreciation for some of the doctrines taught by the Savior as controlling principles in the relations of men. But these organizations seldom, if ever, acknowledge the divine authenticity of these principles. All too frequently do we arrogate to ourselves the credit for devising a plan or initiating a movement calculated to better society when all the fundamental virtue involved in the program is plagiarized from the gospel of our Lord.

Some may ask, what does it matter so long as Christian principles are applied? I believe it matters a great deal in this respect, that little progress is made in establishing the gospel as a controlling force in the lives of people unless people consciously acknowledge its divine source and power. Indeed, the very essence of the gospel lies largely in that acknowledgment. For the Christian gospel postulates a government of God as distinguished from the rule and wisdom of men. I am sure that the Christian cause has been retarded immeasurably by this failure to frankly and openly acknowledge the contributions which the gospel has made to the happiness of our lives.

When the gospel is so rarely spoken of by the many who admire its principles and teachings, it is not disseminated so widely as it otherwise would be. It is not made the subject of every day conversation. There is seemingly a reticence to discuss it. Leaders in business and society, although professedly Christians, seldom mention it, and yet on occasions they will admit gospel principles are indispensable to the preservation of the very institutions to which they devote their life's work.

Why should the man of business be ashamed to declare that the gospel of mutual consideration, as set forth in the doctrine of the good neighbor, and the loftiest concepts of loyalty, integrity and humility are derived from the teachings of the Master and the holy scriptures? Would it make them less effective? I am sure it would not, and in addition thereto it would advertise these principles and their Author in a way that would give vitality to both in the concept of him who heard them. So I conclude that this failure to frankly and openly acknowledge our indebtedness to the Christian gospel for all the finer things of our lives is in no small way responsible for the growth of antagonistic ideologies which so sorely afflict the world today.

Now how well has the Christian doctrine been interpreted? I do not have in mind differences of interpretation for this discussion. I am thinking only of the adequacy of the interpretations measured in terms of the adoption of gospel principles among men and nations. I have always been taught, and I believe, that the true interpretation is in terms of living. "What doth it profit, my brethren, though a man may say he has faith, and have not work? Can faith save him? . . . Faith if it hath not works is dead, being alone . . . Shew me thy faith without thy works and I will shew thee my faith by my works." (James 2:14-18) This Christian philosophy from James is the true concept. A very considerable part of the earthly ministry of the Savior was devoted to rebuking the hypocrisy of professors of religion whose lives were not consistent with the principles they espoused.

Nearly everyone will concede the logic of this position; yet many will not apply it to their own lives. In my opinion this failure to live what we teach has been a great deterrent to the advancement of the cause of Christ.

I never look upon religion as a mere abstract philosophy just to be discussed and admired and adopted in theory. Someone has made the homely statement "that a man who prays on his knees on Sunday and preys on his neighbor on Monday needs more simplicity in his religion." It is this inconsistency between profession and practice which has discouraged and dampened the faith of so many people. Men and women should be strong enough in their own devotion to a cause not to permit the weaknesses and hypocrisies of others to affect their faith. But many are not sufficiently well grounded and established in their convictions to do so and many others have not acquired an understanding of the gospel so that their conceptions are materially influenced by the lives and actions of those who profess to be Christians.

The life of a Christian is his greatest advocate for the cause,—far more potent than his words in the dissemination of the divine message. A man's living determines his personality and his personality is the greatest power he possesses to influence others. So conformity to the principles of the gospel has been and always will be indispensable to its propagation. All the outer symbols of religion,—the magnificent cathedrals, the beautiful music, the pageantry and rituals of the church will never have the lasting appeal which comes from the truly good life. How many regrets there must be on the part of those whose vision has been clarified,—often too late to make amends, to enable them to see the retardation of the work of God occasioned by their own failure to live the principles they themselves believed to be right.

Now, I come to the concluding question which I address particularly to my listeners who believe in the efficacy of the Christian doctrine as a solution for the problems of the world. What program can be adopted which will give most promise for spreading the word of Christ? I think you will very likely have anticipated my suggestion from what I have already said. It is for everyone who has received the truth to pass it on to others,—for each true Christian to be a missionary to the cause of Christ.

I have the honor to represent a missionary church,—a church founded in large part upon the revelation that its mission should be to preach the gospel "unto every nation, and kindred, and tongue, and people;" (D. C. 133:37) and a church that has maintained an almost incomparable missionary system since its very beginning. I would like to talk to you at length on our missionary work, but time does not permit.

In 1930, on the one hundredth anniversary of the organization of the Church, I made the estimate that up to that time there had been approximately 60,000 missionaries in the church. The average length of a mission is twenty months or six hundred days. Thirty-six million days of missionary service had been contributed at that time at an average expense outlay of one dollar per day. This money had been taken out of Latter-day Saint communities and sent away from home. If the reasonable value of a missionary's productive time may be set at three dollars per day, (it would run much higher now) there may be added to the expense item one hundred eight million dollars, making the total of moneys spent or the cost of the missionary service of our Church in its first one hundred years of existence something like one hundred forty-four millions of dollars,—a sum nearly equal to the cost of Boulder Dam whose construction at the time mentioned was just being projected. On the same basis of computation the last decade has added another twelve million days of missionary service at an aggregate money cost of forty-eight million dollars, or a grand total of two hundred twenty-four million dollars invested by a relatively small church in preaching the gospel of Christ.

The enormous cost of the service has been widely distributed, shared by nearly every family in the Church. Many families have sent more than one missionary and not infrequently has a home kept one or more missionaries in the field continuously for ten or a dozen years, and sometimes for a quarter of a century.

For a hundred years there have been two armies constantly on the march,—an army of missionaries outbound from Zion carrying the banner of gospel peace and liberty and an inbound army of freed and happy people, faith and hope shining in every face, seeking the shelter, the inspiration, and the glorious opportunities of a divinely appointed society. Contingents of these armies have passed on almost every highway in the world,—in the states, from the Americas North and South, up and down the devious waters of the Mississippi where missionaries of the early days went on flat boats to embark on slow-sailing vessels on their long, tedious voyages to their fields in Britain, Scandinavia and the Continent, across Europe to the lands of the Arabs and the Turks and on into far off India, over the broad expanse of the Pacific, to and from Hawaii, and the distant Isles of the South Seas. As the missionaries have passed the immigrants in these great counter-marches of the century, whether in their ships at sea or as they paused to clasp hands in their weary trek across the prairie, pagerange190 we can fancy that the missionaries have said, "We go to carry the gospel." "Thank God we have it," the converts reply, and then add, "We will follow you later." So indeed they have in one heroic round,—missionary to convert, then convert to missionary. Great has been their gift, generously have they given.

I say this much about our missionary labor because it indicates, better than anything I know, the spirit, the devotion, the zeal, and the sacrifice essential to disseminate the principles of Christian truth among the peoples of the earth. These missionaries have believed in their cause; they have counted it more precious than anything else in life, and they have demonstrated their affection for it and their love of humanity.

Can we, my friends, who entertain the fond hope that some day the peace of Christ may come to the world and that the principles of His holy gospel may control the hearts and minds of men, expect to realize the fulfillment of our aspirations with less effort, devotion, and sacrifice? I believe that on reflection you will say that we cannot, and therefore I entreat each of you to be not ashamed of the most salutary power in the earth and to give of your time, your means, and your constant endeavor to spread the glad tidings of great joy and by word and deed and true devotion be not only a disciple but a missionary of the living Christ.
