Franklin S. Harris, Book of Mormon: Message and Evidences ( 1961)

Chapter 16

The Book of Mormon and Joseph Smith

During the more than one hundred years since its publication, the Book of Mormon has had a wide distribution and great influence because of the doctrines it teaches and its spiritual power as a witness and missionary for Jesus Christ. In the history of the book there have been many, however, who have largely overlooked the message of the book in their zeal to discuss the relation of Joseph Smith to the book and the implications of his story of its production. Let us consider some aspects of Joseph Smith's relationship to the book and note discussion of this by some of the critics.

188Joseph Smith's own account of his connection with the Book of Mormon is too well known and readily accessible [^1] to warrant retelling. Suffice it to say the last of the Book of Mormon historians, Moroni, then a resurrected being, showed him the location of the Book of Mormon accounts recorded on plates having the appearance of gold. These plates, which were buried in the Hill Cumorah in western New York, he was enabled to translate "by the gift and power of God" with the aid of the Urim and Thummim. [^2] As Joseph Smith translated, the text was written mostly by Oliver Cowdery, acting as scribe, but also partly by Martin Harris and Emma Smith.

188The Book of Mormon was translated in two to three months, scribes writing rapidly as Joseph Smith dictated the translation. Oliver Cowdery tells: "Day after day I continued, uninterrupted to write from his mouth, as he translated with the Urim and ThummimÂ�." [^3]

189 "I wrote with my own pen, the entire Book of Mormon (save a few pages) as it fell from the lips of the Prophet Joseph Smith as as he translated it by the gift and power of God by means of the Urim and ThummimÂ�." [^4]

189I have carefully examined recently part of the original manuscript written by Oliver Cowdery. It is written without the corrections and changes made frequently by authors in original composition, and was apparently written just as Oliver Cowdery described. [^5]

189Later the plates were shown to groups of three and eight witnesses whose statements are found in the front of the Book of Mormon.

189Joseph Smith and his family were hard working farmers who were busy earning a living. Joseph Smith was a normal boy [^6] who was unable to get any appreciable formal education. John S. Reid, later a practicing attorney in western New York, who knew him intimately from 1823 onwards, paid tribute to his character, in a public statement: [^7]

189"I do know that his (Joseph Smith's) character was irreproachable; that he moved in the first circles of the community, and he was often spoken of as a young man of intelligence and good morals, and possessing a mind susceptible of the highest attainments." Mr. Reid further said:

189"After living in that neighborhood about three years, enjoying the good feelings of his acquaintance as a worthy youth, he told his particular friends that he had had a revelation from God to go to the west about eighty miles to his father's, in which neighborhood he should find hid in the earth an old history written on golden plates, which would give great light and knowledge concerning the will of God towards His people in this generation, unfolding the destiny of all nations, kindreds and tongues."

190On September 27, 1827, the plates were obtained and Joseph Smith began to copy some of the characters and do some translating. These events were common knowledge in the community; [^8] scores of people in Palmyra and vicinity were told. We have an extremely valuable written account given by Reverend John A. Clark, at that time a minister in Palmyra. [^9] Reverend Clark knew Martin Harris who later mortgaged his farm to finance publication of the Book of Mormon. Early in the autumn of 1827, about sunrise, Martin Harris went to him, told him of the Book of Mormon and showed him a paper with several lines of characters which were unfamiliar to Reverend Clark.

190Reverend Clark then relates: [^10]

190"He was so much in earnest on this subject, that he immediately started off with some of the manuscripts that Smith furnished him on a journey to New York and Washington to consult some learned men to ascertain the nature of the language in which this record was en-graven. After his return he came to see me again, and told me that, among others, he had consulted Professor Anthon, who thought the characters in which the book was written very remarkable, but he could not decide exactly what language they belonged to. Martin had now become a perfect believer."

190These characters were puzzling to the best scholars of that day, including Dr. Charles Anthon [^11] and Dr. Samuel L. Mitchell [^12] to whom they were shown by Martin Harris. This has been discussed by Ariel L. Crowley, [^13] who also gives a modern view of the transcript of particular interest in the light of D. H. Bays' earlier comments. [^14] Copies of the characters "were shown to people, including ministers and other gentlemen of learning and influence," reports Pomeroy Tucker, [^15] who lived in the community at that time.

191The translation was dictated in about sixty days from April 7, 1829, to about the first week in June. [^16] The Book of Mormon was offered for publication to Thurlow Weed in Rochester, [^17] who refused the book. It was then published in Palmyra by Egbert B. Grandin.

191Eleven special witnesses who were shown the plates never denied their testimony though several later left the Church. Several on their death beds reaffirmed the original statements. [^18]

191Emma Smith relates she moved the plates many times while they were covered. [^19]

191Witnesses who saw the plates maintained their testimony. I have heard Charles C. Richards, the last living man who has talked to witness David Whitmer, relate that David Whitmer answered his question concerning his testimony as follows: [^20]

191"Read the printed testimony of the three witnesses, which you will find on one of the front pages of the Book of Mormon-and I say to you that every word of it is true."

192 Many can remember, as I do, hearing William Harrison Homer tell the story of Martin Harris reaffirming his testimony both in Ohio and later on his death bed in Utah in 1875. [^21]

192The witnesses of the Book of Mormon in holding to their testimony made to the Book of Mormon felt, as did Hyrum Smith, when he said: [^22] [^23] "I had been abused and thrust into a dungeon, and confined for months on account of my faith and the testimony of Jesus Christ. However, I thank God that I felt a determination to die rather than deny the things which my eyes had seen, which my hands had handled and which I had borne testimony to wherever my lot had been cast; and I can assure my beloved brethren that I was enabled to hear as strong a testimony, when nothing but death presented itself, as ever I did in life."

192Many critics have side-stepped the testimony of these witnesses, or brushed it off with but a passing reference. The apostate John Hyde, [^24] accepted the testimony as being credible and that Joseph Smith must have had some plates. T. B. H. Stenhouse [^25] concluded:

192"That Joseph had at one time in his possession metallic plates of some kind, with engraved characters upon them, there appears no reason to doubt, if human testimony is to be accepted as evidence."

192Bays [^26] also accepts as a fact that the witnesses stuck to their original story.

192The non-Mormon Mr. Joseph Symes [^27] after quoting the testimony of the Three and the Eight Witnesses asks pertinently:

192"What would the Jew or Christian not give to have his Bible attested in the above manner?"

193 Why did opposition develop to the book? It is because of the implications of divine revelation and the claim of miraculous interpositions of divine power in its coming forth and translation: that "its adherents have discovered a most dangerous weapon against the moral world in this doctrine of 'a continuing revelation,'" as Perry Benjamin Pierce said. [^28] Because critics were unwilling to accept the consequences of the production and message of the Book of Mormon, they would look for "facts" to substantiate their own particular theory to explain away or discredit the Book of Mormon. As Joseph Jastrow [^29] remarks:

193"Create a belief in the theory and the facts will create themselves."

193There have been three general types of theories [^30] used by those who were unwilling to accept Joseph Smith's account of the production of the Book of Mormon. There are many variations in theories but the exponents of the theories have one thing in common,-they can see the weaknesses and fallacies in all other arguments but their own. [^31]

193The first theory was that Joseph Smith wrote the book himself but was a clever deceiver and liar. [^32] This was at first believed by Alexander Campbell who said: [^33]

193"If I could swear to any man's voice, face or person, assuming different names, I could swear that this book was written by one man. And as Joseph Smith is a very ignorant man and is called the author on the title page, I cannot doubt for a single moment that he is the sole author and proprietor of it."

194The critics soon began to have misgivings. The Book of Mormon was too complex and had too much valuable material in it to have been so easily produced by a man of Joseph Smith's experience. Take the matter of time of translation. A recent translation of the Book of Mormon into a foreign tongue took nine months of hard work. To write the original was certainly a much greater task. Writing is not easy or rapid work, as any author knows, particularly when it deals with past times and peoples. Margaret Mitchell spent seven years on Gone With the Wind. The author, T. B. Costain, of the Black Rose read or consulted five hundred books, hired a Chinese scholar and a research worker who could read medieval Latin and French and the book still has serious deficiencies. [^34]

194Emma Smith has related [^35] an incident which throws considerable light on the amount of education Joseph Smith had had:

194"At one time while he was translating he stopped suddenly, pale as a sheet, and said, 'Emma, did Jerusalem have walls around it?' When I answered 'yes' he replied, 'Oh! I was afraid I had been deceived! He had such a limited knowledge of history at that time that he did not even know that Jerusalem was surrounded by walls."

194Joseph Smith spent only a small fraction of the time in translation that individuals have spent in trying to disprove his work. His manuscript was not worked over to give many changes and substitutions as is standard practice in writing. Joseph Smith was not a linguist with a research staff and a large modern library to aid him, nor a theologian trained in all the commentaries. He was not a Bancroft with a corps of assistants and stenographers to read large quantities of material and digest it, nor was he a trained research worker turning out a Ph.D thesis. He lived before the day of public libraries in every town. There was not even any reliable information dealing with the peoples and times he was to describe in the Book of Mormon. The high civilizations in America such as Inca and Aztec were many centuries later than those of Book of Mormon times. He further made no attempt to fit into the Book information which was then known; suggested correlations came many years later by others. There is no trustworthy evidence that he ever had access to or read information which would have been useful, such books have not been produced. A book could not be produced from a rumor which he may have heard any more than the mere idea of an invasion of the Continent being desirable in World War II would gather the men and materials necessary and transport them to the beachheads. The contemporary speculations on the American Indian did not change the course of human action such as the Book of Mormon did. [^36]

195The theology of the Book of Mormon impressed Remy [^37] to remark:

195"JosephÂ� read much, especially the Bible and the dissertations of the theologians on the commentaries of the Bible."

195That is the impression the book makes but Joseph did not have any commentaries.

195Some would have us believe that Joseph Smith was a liar, vile and immoral in his personal life, and still write the cleanest book of scripture in the world. As Perry Benjamin Pierce [^38] expressed it:

195"The Book of Mormon is not in itself immoralÂ�.The Mormons have been slandered and traduced, unjustly and without warrant, for an immoral 'Bible.' There is nothing immoral in the book,"

195Joseph Smith was sincere and consistent.

196 Whence did Joseph Smith get his information? It was not until someone [^39] suggested that Joseph Smith may have pirated a partly completed manuscript written by Reverend Solomon Spaulding (died 1816) which tells a story of a Roman ship blown off course to America at the time of Constantine. The Spaulding manuscript, it was supposed, had the theological material added by Sidney Rigdon. But this Spaulding theory which had become commonly accepted by anti-Mormons received a fatal blow when Spaulding's manuscript was discovered in 1884 by President James H. Fairchield of Oberlin College, and published in 1886 in the Deseret News. [^40] This manuscript has become an excellent evidence for Joseph Smith's account of his possession of ancient records.

196If Spaulding's manuscript represents the best information available to an educated man in that day who was interested in the American Indians, then to use such a manuscript as a basis for the Book of Mormon would be to start with an intellectual as well as a literary bankruptcy. No convincing proof has ever been given that Sidney Rigdon knew anything about the Book of Mormon before it was presented to him by Parley P. Pratt late in 1830. During the time Joseph Smith had the Book of Mormon plates and the book was being translated and printed Sidney Rigdon was closely associated with Alexander Campbell who would have known of any such activities. Dr. Daryl Chase in a thesis Sidney Rigdon-Early Mormon (University of Chicago, 1931) concluded:

196"No one has ever presented a single sentence of direct evidence that Rigdon ever saw Joseph Smith until after the publication of the Book of Mormon."

196Though some such as Arbaugh [^41] have rejected in the past other theories in favor of the Spaulding-Rigdon theory.

196The untenability of the Spaulding-Rigdon theory has been shown by many, including a number of non-Mormon critics such as Bays, [^42] I. W. Riley, [^43] E. Meyer, [^44] DeVoto, [^45] and Ruth and Reginald Wright Kauffman, [^46] and David Utter whom the Kauffmans quote. Utter remarked:

197"No one who ever carefully read the Book of Mormon could fail to see that it never in any part was written for a romance, Â� Now, at last, the Spaulding manuscript has been found, and rests secure in the library of Oberlin College."

197The Spaulding-Rigdon "evidence" is a fine example of that ironic definition of history as "the consolidating of doubtful traditions into undoubted facts."

197The third type of theory is that Joseph Smith deceived himself and suffered from epilepsy or some mental disease. One of the great exponents of this view was Riley. The worth of Riley's speculations may be gained from a statement by George Wharton James: [^47]

197"The 'value' of this kind of absurd speculative writing may be understood when I call attention to the fact that Mr. Riley's publishers, the Putnam's Sons, have repudiated his articles on 'Christian Science,' and 'Mormonism,' which appeared in the last volume of the Cambridge History of American Literature series, have called in the copies of the book already issued, suppressed the remainder of the edition, and promised a new issue with fair and truthful articles replacing Riley's fantastic absurdities."

197The inherent difficulty of this theory is that no amount of psychological scrambling could give Joseph Smith information, teachings, literary devices which he did not possess. The Book of Mormon is a complex, consistent book, and not the chaotic ramblings of a disordered mind, or the disease-warped expressions of an ordinary man. The problem raised by the French Professor George H. Bousquet in Revue d'Histore des Religions [^48] is pertinent here:

198"I can not well understand how so long a text as the Book of Mormon (plus 116 pages of the manuscript) could have been written down in but a few months."

198There is a familiar story of the school boy who was not impressed by proverbs given in class because they seemed true and commonplace and was asked to think up a few new proverbs for the next day. [^49] The teachings and doctrines of the Book of Mormon would have been impossible for Joseph Smith to have conceived and written; for a Congregationalist minister like Solomon Spaulding, [^50] or any person educated in the doctrines and traditions of modern sectarian Christianity of 1830 or today for that matter to have written the book would have been even less possible, for many of its important doctrines do not agree with those of the Christian sects. [^51] To do so would have been a miracle of inventive genius and of book production for an untrained boy to settle the great theological controversies and sweep away the sectarian dogmas.

198The diligent search for possible inspirations by critics [^52] for items in the Book of Mormon recalls the story of the school boy who won a local hymn writing contest. After the contest a newspaper reported with considerable hard work showed that this adolescent's words could be found scattered through fifty other hymns. To attempt to explain the Book of Mormon by comparison with contemporary material is even more difficult and foolish.

198The search for other explanations has been largely because critics, due to their own prejudices, would not accept and understand Joseph Smith's story. The refusal to accept something because it is not completely understood recalls the question of the British scientist Oliver Heaviside who asked:

199"Shall I refuse my dinner because I do not fully understand the process of digestion?"

199Some years after Joseph Smith's death, his wife, Emma, was asked some direct questions by her son, Joseph III: [^53]

199When asked: "What is your belief about the authenticity of the Book of Mormon, she replied:

199"My belief is that the Book of Mormon is of divine authenticity-I have not the slightest doubt of it. I am satisfied that no man could have dictated the writing of the manuscripts unless he was inspired; for, when acting as his scribe, your father would dictate to me hour after hour; and when returning after meals, or interruptions, he would at once begin where he had left off, without either seeing the manuscript or having any portion of it read to him. This was a usual thing for him to do. It would have been improbable that a learned man could do this, and for one Â� unlearned as he was, it was simply impossibleÂ�."

199This is a statement that would be appreciated both by writers and by husbands.

199Joseph Smith's scribes and associates were convinced that he was making a translation of ancient records. The testimony of two groups of people that they saw the original plates from which the translation was made still stands. It was known in the community that Joseph Smith had records and was making a translation; the same story was told in 1827 as evidenced by Reverend John A. Clark's book and others as was told later.

199The theories that Joseph Smith was aided by material of Reverend Solomon Spaulding, Sidney Rigdon or others fell, through lack of convincing evidence. Each critic can see the beam in the other critic's eye (theory) but not the mote in his own. The suggestion that he was a fraud or deceiver no longer receives the same attention.

200 Joseph Smith was honest, sincere and not a deceiver. As Eduard Meyer concluded: [^54]

200"Never has a clairvoyant or prophet portrayed in so evident a manner what happens inside himself, as that discourse (Doctrine and Covenants, D&C 9) given here in stammering fashion, it is true, but still occurring completely understandable. At the same time any thought of conscious fraud is excluded by this entirely frank statement and because of his naive fully demonstrated personal testimony."

200As Professor Bousquet points out: [^55]

200"What, for me, makes the whole (Rigdon-Spaulding) theory so highly improbable that it must appear to every unbiased scholar as false, is the fact that, in the case of such a wicked plot the early history of the Church becomes psychologically absurd and contradictory. But if we assume that Joseph Smith did really dictate his own sacred text to those who believed in him, all becomes clear and evident If I were a member of a jury, before which the Prophet were indicted for fraud, I should certainly vote, 'not guilty,' and I firmly believe that this verdict must also be that of scientific history."

200And a recent comment by Marcus Bach: [^56]

200"It was not the kind of book a man would write for pastime or write for profit. It was solemn and ponderous and heavy as the plates on which it was inscribed. No Vermont schoolboy wrote this, and no Presbyterian preacher tinkered with these pages."

200The material in the Book of Mormon and its inspiring influence (as noted in previous chapters) is such that no man could have written it, whether sane or mentally disturbed. Joseph Smith was merely an instrument in the hands of God by which a translation of this ancient record was made available in our day to the end that more people might believe in and accept the teachings of Christ.
