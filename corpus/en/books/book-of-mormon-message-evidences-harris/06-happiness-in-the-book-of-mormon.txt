Franklin S. Harris, Book of Mormon: Message and Evidences ( 1961)

Chapter 5

Happiness in the Book of Mormon

Since man has been on the earth he has had certain fundamental desires. Though the desires have been modified by the environment and culture of the age, the striving of man has been for the same objectives, most of which can be summed in the phrase, the quest for happiness. The Book of Mormon in its claims to have important information from God to guide man, and because the Book tells of the history of peoples for many centuries, it should have information of considerable value to aid us in our search for happiness.

35According to the Book of Mormon the teachings of God are a philosophy for obtaining happiness, here as well as hereafter. The ascetic concept of blind obedience to God in following commandments for benefits in the hereafter only, and no benefits here, is denied by the Book of Mormon. The commands of God are actually the rules and methods of obtaining in this life the greatly desired happiness of the individual, and when applied by a group make for prosperity.

35A favorite quotation from the Book is, "Men are that they might have joy." [^1]

35And another, "If there be no righteousness, there be no happiness." [^2]

35The teachings of God are referred to as the "plan of happiness," [^3] and those who live its precepts are called "happy." In the words of the Book:

35"Ye should consider the blessed and happy state of those that keep the commandments of God. For behold, they are blessed in all things, both temporal and spiritual." [^4]

36And in contrast: "The Lord would not suffer them to take happiness in sin." [^5] And "Ye have sought for happiness in doing iniquity, which thing is contrary to the nature of that righteousness which is in our great and Eternal Head." [^6]

36Such virtues as forgetfulness of self, service to mankind, humility, hard work, consideration for the welfare of others lead to a harmonious society which results in material prosperity and a happy individual. The happiness comes, however, as a by-product and not from seeking it as an end in itself. Personal unhappiness and group inefficiency result from selfishness. This concept of the ultimate basis of prosperity being spiritual has been championed by, among others, the economist Roger Babson, in his Fundamentals of Prosperity [^7] and Professor Shailer Mathews in The Spiritual Interpretation of History. [^8]

36It should be observed that wealth and learning as such are not condemned by the Book, but the misuse or exaggeration of their importance, or the seeking for the possession of learning and material things for selfish rather than altruistic aims is condemned.

36The status of "learning" is given in the following: "O the vainness and the frailties, and the foolishness of men! When they are learned they think they are wise, and they hearken not unto the counsel of God for they set it aside, supposing they know of themselves, wherefore, their wisdom is foolishness, and it profit them not. And they shall perish. But to be learned is good if they hearken unto the counsels of God." [^9] In this connection Alma told his son Helaman: "O, remember, my son, and learn wisdom in thy youth; yea, learn in thy youth to keep the commandments of God." [^10] The personal limitations of the Book of Mormon historians were appreciated by them; they were humble and did not have intellectual arrogance. [^11]

37 Jacob taught with regard to the purpose for seeking for riches:

37"Think of your brethren like unto yourselves, and be familiar with all and free with your substance, that they may be rich like unto you. But before ye seek for riches, seek ye for the kingdom of God. And after ye shall obtain a hope in Christ ye shall obtain riches, if ye seek them; and ye will seek them for the intent to do good-to clothe the naked, and to feed the hungry, and to liberate the captive and administer relief to the sick and the afflicted." [^12]

37"Wo unto the rich, who are rich as to the things of the world, For because they are rich they despise the poor, and they persecute the meek, and their hearts are upon their treasures; wherefore, their treasure is their God. And behold their treasure shall perish with them." [^13] The motivation in the desire to have wealth and the utilization of wealth when acquired then determines whether its effect is good or bad on the individual. [^14]

37The type of life which the Book of Mormon encourages when followed by the people as a whole resulted in material prosperity [^15] which in one instance lasted two centuries up to 200 A.D. A description of conditions at this time reports:

37"There was no contention in the land, because of the love of God which did dwell in the hearts of the people Â�. Surely there could not be a happier people among all the people who had been created by the hand of God." [^16]

38This was not due to God's interference with nature, but resulted from obedience to natural law, since when a commandment, law or requirement is given to man by God there is a means for meeting it. [^17]

38The point of view running through the book is that given by King Benjamin: [^18]

38"All that he requires of you is to keep his commandments; and he has promised you that if ye would keep his commandments ye should prosper in the land."

38The great teacher Alma taught: [^19]

38"Be diligent and temperate in all things. See that ye are not lifted up unto pride; see that ye do not boast in your own wisdom, nor of your much strength. Use boldness but not overbearance; and also see that ye bridle all your passions, that ye may be filled with love; see that ye refrain from idleness. Do not pray as the Zoramites do, for ye have seen that they pray to be heard of men, and to be praised for their wisdom. Do not say: O God, I thank thee that we are better than our brethren; but rather say; O Lord, forgive my unworthiness, and remember my brethren in mercy-acknowledge your unworthiness before God at all times." King Benjamin [^20] told his people:

38"When ye are in the service of your fellow beings ye are only in the service of your God."

38Sometimes people feel that the righteous come out on the short end of things in life and that the wicked get everything. This leads to such reasoning as Lewis Browne has suggested in speaking of the early Jews: [^21]

39"Now that this world held but little joy in store for them, they began vaguely to dream of a world to come which only the righteous would inherit."

39This wishful thinking was not the viewpoint of the Book of Mormon peoples. They were taught that if they were righteous and sought for others' welfare that they would prosper through working together, honestly and efficiently. The evils of poverty [^22] and the opportunities of the poor [^23] are discussed. The emphasis on improving the conditions was through correct teachings [^24] and these would be reflected in action. No system of bookkeeping nor set of regulations can prevent dishonesty if the heart is not right.

39We may note in this connection that Nephi gave up his position as Chief Judge to preach to the people because general wickedness had made good leaders and laws ineffective [^25] as did Alma [^26] also. The importance of good leadership is emphasized by King Mosiah. [^27]

39A Nephite [^28] about 7 B.C., complained that material prosperity often leads to transgression: "O how foolish, and how vain, and how evil, and devilish, and how quick to do iniquity, and how slow to do good, are the children of men;Â� how quick to hearken unto the words of the evil one, and to set their hearts upon the vain things of the worldÂ� How quick to be lifted up in pride;Â� how quick to boast, and do all manner of that which is iniquityÂ�."

39There are many examples in the book of corrupt leadership, graft and the desire for power with the results that follow. [^29] The same principles apply to the ancient civilizations of America where wickedness led to their destruction. [^30] This misconduct often leads to such despair that the people again practiced right living to solve their problems. [^31] Pride, persecution, love of material things, neglect of the needs of others, oppression, lust for power, and crimes of all sorts are condemned. [^32] Excuses are not acceptable, [^33] nor is the denial of the justice of God, [^34] who cannot look upon sin with the least degree of allowance. [^35]

40Though, in a general way personal prosperity follows from hard work, thrift and the exercise of the virtues, yet because of the complexity of the environment in which an individual lives and the many factors over which he does not have control, we cannot say that the most prosperous are always the most righteous and the poorest the most wicked. We can say, however, that the exercise of personal righteousness in obedience to the teachings of God gives personal happiness in that inner peace of mind, contentment and satisfaction which righteous living produces and which can be obtained in no other way. This holds no matter what the individual's status may be with regard to wealth, education, position, or opportunity. The same principles for obtaining happiness apply to all.

40The Book of Mormon gives three reasons then for a good moral life-a life based on the teachings of God to man The first reason, discussed in the last chapter, is an impending judgment and a restoration of the effects of past deeds in the life of a person, giving instead of a mistaken idea of a physical hell of fire and brimstone punishment, an inescapable remorse of conscience until justice and mercy are satisfied. The second incentive is the teaching and experience of Book of Mormon peoples that community prosperity results from obedience to the teachings of God, and despair and chaos from their disregard. Thirdly happiness of the individual comes from living according to the instructions from God.

41Surely if any book teaches good doctrines such as these, it must be considered as worthwhile and it must have a lasting effect for good on its readers who follow its precepts.
