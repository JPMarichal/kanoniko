Franklin S. Harris, Book of Mormon: Message and Evidences ( 1961)

Chapter 1

Introduction

The spring of 1946, in New York City, the Grolier Club selected and exhibited 100 books, published before 1900, which most influenced the life and culture of the American people. [^1] These were such books as The Autobiography of Benjamin Franklin; Noah Webster's An American Dictionary; Abraham Lincoln's Gettysburg Address; and the Book of Mormon, which was given to the world by Joseph Smith in 1830. This recognition of the influence of the Book of Mormon recalls a letter written by the late Charles H. Hull, Professor of American History, Cornell University. [^2] He wrote:

7"I am perfectly willing to say to anyone that I suppose the Book of Mormon to be one of the most famous and widely discussed books ever published in America. I think an arguable case can be made for the assertion that it is the most famous and widely discussed book ever first published in America."

7Let us review some of the history of this renowned book over its more than a century of influence since first published in 1830. The Book of Mormon deals with the migration from the Old World to America, of three groups of peoples. It tells of their civilization and religious experiences of long ago, covering a number of centuries. It was the acceptance of this book which gave the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints its nickname of "Mormon," a Church which now numbers well over a million members.

7Curiously the Book of Mormon in First 1 Ne. 13:40, predicts its world-wide distribution. Yet with all the large reading public of today and the publicity techniques, publishers now rarely print, even for a very promising book, an initial edition of 5,000 copies. In Palmyra, New York, in 1830, it was daring indeed, to do so with the Book of Mormon. People in Palmyra wondered why such a large edition was printed when only a few copies would meet the local demand for a long time. If to the daring of such a large edition is added the boycotting of local citizens and the caustic and reviling opposition which immediately developed, it seems a miracle that the edition would ever be sold.

8Following the publication, though some people accepted the book, many more denounced it, such as the editor [^3] of the Rochester Daily Advertiser.

8"The Book of Mormon has been placed in our hands. A viler imposition was never practiced. It is an evidence of fraud, blasphemy and credulity, shocking to Christian and moralist."

8A few weeks later Joseph Smith was arrested on the charge, among other things, of setting the county in an uproar by preaching the Book of Mormon. [^4]

8Prediction was made in the Missouri Intelligencer and Boon's Lick Advertiser: [^5]

8"The Book of Mormon cannot survive this generation. The next will remember it only to smile at the credulity of the present."

8An editor of the New York Spectator also foretold a short life for the book. [^6]

8This seemed to be the popular point of view to take in those days. Rev. Wm. Harris [^7] used some of this same type of strong speech:

8"Probably in the history of the world, there is not to be found an instance of more cool impudence, and deliberate blasphemy, than is contained in the Book of Mormon."

9Unlike most of the critics of the Book of Mormon Alexander Campbell, the founder of the Disciples of Christ, made a careful study of the book in his Millennial Harbinger. [^8] Some of his criticisms will be discussed in detail later. But the book continued to increase in acceptance and he deplored in 1835: [^9]

9"Perhaps we were too sanguine when we thought that the fable was so barefaced that it could not stand upon its legs or palms in the face of day and the American people." But he referred to it as a fraud.

9Why such strong language in vigorously condemning this book and in predicting its short life? These people talk as though a serious menace must be stopped by all means, frequently using name-calling as a substitute for reason.

9What was the importance of the book to the young Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, founded April 6, 1830, and growing rapidly?

9It is "the keystone of our religion," emphasized Joseph Smith on November 28, 1841. [^10] He was the president of the Church and the translator of the records of ancient peoples who lived long ago on the American hemisphere.

9Similarly in 1850, Orson Pratt, in his Divine Authenticity of the Book of Mormon, p. 1, in the language of his day tells why the ministers were so aroused:

9"The Book of Mormon must be either true or false. If true, it is one of the most important messages ever sent from God to manÂ�. If false, it is one of the most cunning, wicked, bold, deep-laid impositions ever palmed upon the world."

9As the decades rolled on and the book increased in sales and popularity, many non-Mormons made their comments, favorable and critical. Sir Richard F. Burton, in 1861, observed: [^11]

10"America, like Africa, is a continent of the future; the Book of Mormon has created for it an historical and miraculous past."

10Often the critics complained of the language and sometimes of the message of the book. Now, according to W. J. Conybeare, in 1854: [^12]

10"This worthless bookÂ� contains no novel dogmas, Â� We do not remember, among all the ponderous folios which human dulness has produced, any other book of such unmitigated stupidity. It seems inconceivable how any man could patiently sit down, day after day, to weary himself with writing sheet after sheet, of such sleep-compelling nonsense."

10Yet at the same time, in 1851, The Editor's Table in Harper's New Monthly Magazine, [^13] evaluates the book in these terms:

10"Â�We do not hesitate to say it, Joe Smith, or whoever was its author, has made a book superior to that of the Arabian prophet [Mohammed], deeper in its philosophy, purer in its morality, and far more originalÂ�"

10Also, there must be something challenging in the message of a book, which would excite people like Dr. Willard Richards, a physician in Richmond, Massachusetts. When Dr. Richards first picked up the book and read the first page, he remarked, "Either God or the devil wrote this book," and then he read the book through twice in ten days. [^14]

10Some people have accused the Book of Mormon of dangerous doctrines, but not so Mark Twain, writing in 1872, in Roughing It: [^15]

10 "There is nothing vicious in its teachings. Its code of morals is unobjectionable."

11The author of The Mormons or Latter-day Saints, [^16] was to wonder "that so much plausible evidence should be collectible in support of the most transparent pious fraud ever attempted to be palmed off on the credulity of mankind."

11This is not surprising because already there had appeared part of the large body of evidence discovered and published by the earlier Book of Mormon students, among whom were Orson Pratt, George Reynolds, B. H. Roberts, Thomas W. Brookbank and J. M. Sjodahl. More has been published since.

11Do the Latter-day Saints, as a people, refuse to consider claims made by the critics? Rev. M. T. Lamb, like some others, felt if he could expose and discredit the Book of Mormon that it would explode the Mormon church. He lectured all over what was then the Utah territory, and published his lectures, in 1887, in the Golden Bible. Unusual interest was shown (pp. x-xi) by his Mormon audiences. He reported that he had been asked several times to speak at the Sunday services, and upon two occasions was cheerfully allowed the use of their school houses, on one occasion the bishop himself presiding. He concluded:

11"The Mormons, many of them at least, are willing to listen to such discussions, so long as their cherished convictions are treated with candor and kindness. They will accept and read whatever the author is willing to put into their hands upon this subject, provided it is written in the same kindly spirit."

11 Rev. Lamb is one of the two people who have at tempted seriously to discredit the Book of Mormon on the basis of the language of the book itself and in comparison with early American civilizations. The other effort, by Charles A. Shook, 42 years ago, [^17] was published to show that "the Book of Mormon is false in its historical claims and in its descriptions of the race, religion, arts, customs and civilizations of the ancient Americans, and, hence, cannot be accepted as from God."

12One notices, with the years, an increasing tone of respect among the critics. Let us note a few recent comments on the book. A Rochester newspaper editor, writing in 1930, [^18] felt this way: "Â� The book itself, however, remained on which was rounded the greatest religion of the continent and the century. It was not the book itself but the wonderful influence it had on America that counted."

12An interesting interview was reported in The Deseret News: [^19]

12"The Book of Mormon gives the clearest conception of the original American Indian of all historical records," according to the Christian Indian poet, singer and lecturer, Chief Kiutus Tecumseh, of Cashmere, Washington.

12Notice this appreciation by Charles J. Finger, in 1935: [^20]

12"Still Â� there is the literary masterpiece; how an unschooled lad could have had the imagination, the patience, the perseverance to write a volume of nearly six hundred printed pages; in imitation of the biblical style; teaching a faith for which men died; for which hundreds of thousands faced hardships; accepted as truth by more than half a million people today Â� . The book does not make exciting reading by any means, but I, for one, cannot cease to wonder at the broad sweep of canvas which Smith filled with his tale."

13 And from a prominent American: [^21]

13"Of all the American books of the nineteenth century, it seems probable that the Book of Mormon was the most powerful. It reached perhaps only one percent of the United States, but it affected this one percent so powerfully and lastingly that all the people of the United States have been affected, especially by its contribution in opening one of our great frontiers."

13Good and bad have been said about the book. A controversy about its claims and value has raged down to the present day. But in spite of the doubts which have been raised, the book is still a best seller, with 500,000 copies a year produced in English by The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints alone, and over four million copies [^22] of the book printed during its history in most of the twenty-seven different languages into which it has been translated. Each year many people go on reading it and accepting it.

13It is well known that the Church has spent large amounts of money in distributing the Book of Mormon, and tens of thousands of missionaries have been sent with it to the nations of the earth through great sacrifices by parents and friends. Why is the Church spending large sums of money? Why are missionaries giving of their time? Why have people endured persecution? To perpetuate a fraud? Or is it because as Thomas De Quincy would say this book is "a literature of power" [^23] Many have been persecuted for distributing the book or endured prison for reading it as did W. W. Phelps. [^24] People will sacrifice for causes they believe to be worthwhile, resulting in large numbers of people accepting the book as a message from God, leaving friends, fortune and comfort, and joining the Church, to take their lot with other members in the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints in furtherance of its message.

14The Book of Mormon has been a subject of continual controversy. Scores have tried to expose or explain it during its 122 years, and are still writing. How shall we judge the Book of Mormon? It is a physical, tangible object which cannot be whistled away. Yet is there a real basis to some of the criticisms made of the book? What is its present value to Christianity and the world? Does the book have a message of power to the mid-twentieth century? What is its relation to the Bible? In succeeding chapters we shall deal with these questions. We shall explain why it continues to persist in spite of all the critics have said against it, why it enjoys a greater circulation now than at any time in its history, why it continues to be a great power for good in the lives of men and women all over the world.
