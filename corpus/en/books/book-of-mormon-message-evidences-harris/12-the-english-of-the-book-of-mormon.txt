Chapter 11

The English of the Book of Mormon

The Book of Mormon which we have been considering for a number of chapters has an important message for our day. Any message must be given in some language. The language of the Book of Mormon has been of great interest since its publication in 1830, particularly since it claimed to be scripture of God's dealings with Near East peoples who came principally from Palestine to the American continent many centuries ago. The relation of the Book of Mormon to the Bible has been touched already in chapter 5, including some discussion of the apparent Bible-type language. Reading the Book of Mormon will make the reader aware that the language is not ordinary English, either in the finished literary sense of a scholar, or the colloquial sense of every day speech. Let us see what some of the non-Mormons have said about the language of the book, and then consider what we should expect the language to be in terms of the origin and translation of the book.

109The first serious critic was Alexander Campbell who commented in 1831. [^1]

109"The book professes to be written at intervals and by different persons during the long period of 1020 years. And yet for uniformity of style, there never was a book more evidently written by one set of fingers, nor more certainly conceived in one cranium since the first book appeared in human language, than this same book Â�. Shakespeare (sic) was read by 1 Ne. 2200 years before he was born 'The silent grave from whence no traveler returns.'"

109Three years later E. D. Howe [^2] said:

109"The whole work is written in a miserable attempt to imitate the style of King James the First, and the sameness is such, and the tautology of phrases from the beginning to the end of the work, that no one can be left in doubt in identifying the whole with one individual author."

110Professor J. B. Turner [^3] had about the same idea, when he wrote:

110"Others complain that the entire style of some twenty different writers, of as many different ages, is one and the same thing from beginning to end-testimony of the witnesses, preface, title-page, and all-while the styles of no other two writers on earth, sacred or profane, are alike, but exhibit totally different characteristics."

110And W. J. Conybeare had acquired the same point of view. [^4]

110"From the first to the last, though professing to be composed by different authors, under various circumstances, during a period of a thousand years, it is perfectly uniform in style, and maintains the dryness without the brevity of a chronological table."

110Sir Richard F. Burton [^5] tells his experience:

110"Surely there never was a book so thoroughly dull and heavy; it is monotonous as a sage-prairie. Though not liable to be terrified by dry or hard reading, I was, it is only fair to own, unable to turn over more than a few chapters at a time, and my conviction is that very few are so highly gifted that they have been able to read it through at a heat."

110The American humorist, Mark Twain, spent some time in Salt Lake City getting ideas for writing about the Mormons. He had a chapter on the Book of Mormon in Roughing It, written in 1872 in which he quipped: [^6]

110"It is chloroform in print. If Joseph Smith composed this book, the act was a miracle-keeping awake while he did it was, at any rateÂ�. Whenever he (Joseph Smith) found his speech growing too modern which was about every sentence or two-he ladled in a few such scriptural phrases as 'exceeding sore,' 'and it came to pass,' etcÂ�and made things satisfactory again. 'And it came to pass,' was his pet. If he had left that out, his Bible would have been only a pamphlet."

111The Oriental scholar Eduard Meyer [^7] noticed:

111"The style is awkward and monotonous in the extreme, the same phrases are continually repeated (for example in the account almost every sentence begins with 'now it came to pass'), the same Biblical manner of speaking; the vocabulary is very limited, apart from the many peculiar words and proper names invented by the author, in which he let his fancy run free."

111A gentleman, non-member of the Church, on a somewhat broader experience than many, reported [^8]:

111"I was not stumbled at the style, as many are, because I was too familiar with the objections of the Chinese to the Bible on the ground that if it was a book from heaven it would be in more correct and classical language. I had for years been familiar with the grave defects of Bible translations, even the much lauded English, and if that did not shake my confidence in the genuineness of it, why expect Joseph Smiths translation into English of an ancient language perfection? Even the Greek original of the New Testament, which I delight in, I know to be full of errors. Nothing is perfect on earth, and languages the most grammatical are no exception to the statement. I know very well that whatever the ideas an original writer or translator expresses, he could not go beyond his vocabulary. As Joseph Smith was confessedly a man of limited education, and the English Bible of 1611 his one familiar book, of course he wrote or dictated to another to write in the very style and vocabulary he possessed Â� Thinking men should not rule a book out of a claim to genuineness on account of its style, when the style and stated facts of Joseph Smith's education are so consistent."

112 When one looks carefully at the text and English of the learned critics of the Book of Mormon, sometimes there is a sigh of relief that they did not do the translating and writing. [^9] In the first place it is obvious that frequently they know little about the book or haven't read it, [^10] as well as making all kinds of errors in spelling and proof-reading. Thus Mrs. Ellen E. Dickinson [^11] calls Laman and Laban "Taman" and "Toran." Ruth Kauffman and Reginald Wright Kauffman [^12] spell Helaman as "Helamon" and Moroni as "Maroni."

112Part of the critical comments have been met by the straight-forward observation quoted from the gentleman non-member of the Church. Joseph Smith in his translating under the inspiration of God obtained the meaning of the Book of Mormon text. [^13] Joseph Smith neither had a Ph.D. in English nor had he been trained as a linguist. He gave the ideas in the English language in such phraseology and diction as he could master, and not being learned in the knowledge of the English, his expressions were oftentimes ungrammatical and the local expressions of his community sometimes occurred. As an illustration of how ordinary expressions may creep in, a student in a French class was translating an article about a poodle dog (caniche) and the English word he used was "pooch." That was the way he was accustomed to refer to a dog.

112The types of objections raised against the text and translation of the Book of Mormon have previously been raised (and met) with regard to the Bible. [^14]

113 Now let us note two instances of supposed modernisms in the Book of Mormon.

113Alexander Campbell referred to a quotation from Shakespeare. This has been repeated by many others including John Hyde, Jr, [^15] T. B. H. Stenhouse, [^16] M. T. Lamb, [^17] W. A. Linn, [^18] and G. B. Arbaugh. [^19]

113Let us compare three verses from Job in the Old Testament with Lehi in the Book of Mormon and with Shakespeare.

113 Job 10:20-21: "Let me alone that I may take comfort a little before I go whence I shall not return, even to the land of darkness and the shadow of death," and 16:22, "When a few years are come, then I shall go the way whence I shall not return."

113Lehi (2 Ne. 1:14) expresses the idea as: "Hear the words of a trembling parent whose limbs you must soon lay down in the cold and silent grave, from whence no traveler can return."

113Shakespeare: "The undiscovered country from whose bourne no traveler returns." Hamlet, Act III, Scene 1.

113It appears that Lehi is more like Job than Shakespeare. Since Lehi studied Old Testament records and Shakespeare was familiar with the Bible, it seems likely that they took their common inspiration from Job. Joseph Smith may have been familiar with Job but it is unlikely that he was familiar with Shakespeare. [^20]

113Reverend M. T. Lamb in his lectures on the Book of Mormon [^21] in giving the names of the four sons of Lehi who were Laman, Lemuel, Sam and Nephi, would ask where he had heard the name Sam before, and then recall that Sam was a good old Yankee nickname for Samuel, hence the Book of Mormon could not be an ancient record. At one meeting Elder W. W. Cluff recalled the name Dan from the Old Testament, and on the same reasoning observed that it was a good old Yankee nickname for Daniel, hence the Old Testament could not be an ancient book. The lecturer had no comment to make to this reply. [^22]

114Actually the word "Sam" is a typical Egyptian name as in the great Sam Tawi of the 11th dynasty. [^23]

114The Times and Seasons [^24] in 1843 commented:

114"Certainly, we should have felt ourselves justified in being sceptical as to the truth of the Book of Mormon, had we found it written in the style of modern divinity, knowing at the same time, that the individual who sent it forth had not had the advantage requisite to give a polished education."

114It is difficult for even the best trained writers to avoid internal inconsistencies and conflicts in plot or in detail in writing (Some of these were noted in Chapter 9). Thus we note that the great German dramatist Schiller has Wallenstein say he has brown hair (Wallen-stein's Death, V, 4) and yet two acts previous to this he says he has gray hair (III, 15). Homer in the Iliad has Pylaemen killed (V, 576), yet later has him in tears following a chariot with his son's body (XIII, 658). Shakespeare in Romeo and Juliet has Lady Capulet about 28 or 29 years old in Act 1, Scene 3, since Juliet is there 14 and her mother was that age when Juliet was born. Yet a few days later (V, 3) Lady Capulet speaks of "my old age." Shakespeare in Henry the Fifth has shillings appear in circulation. Shillings, however, were not used until the time of Henry the Seventh. In this play Henry the Fifth speaks of going to Constantinople and taking the Turk by the beard. Constantinople was not in the hands of the Turks until 1453, 31 years after Henry the Fifth died. [^25]

115 These examples show that it is very difficult when writing to avoid such mistakes. [^26] Had Joseph Smith, an untrained youth, been attempting to compose an account himself as complex in structure and character as the Book of Mormon it is to be expected that many such errors would be made, particularly when there is no evidence of rewriting or editing the text. The Book of Mormon seems free from such errors.

115The Book of Mormon in describing old civilizations, for instance, does not refer to watches and clocks or the hours of the day as measured by such instruments. No week-day names occur in the book since not one of the names of the days of the week is traceable to an original Hebraic source. No use of modern names for cloth is made such as "calico," "muslin," "delaine," "linsey," or "broadcloth."

115Elder Thomas W. Brookbank [^27] has prepared a long impressive list of modern words and customs which are not in the Book of Mormon, but some of which a modern writer would be likely to unconsciously include in writing of ancient times. The Book of Mormon, as an ancient record, however does not include such words as, to mention but a few examples, titles as Mr., Mrs., Prof., Dr., Lord, etc.; calico, muslin, broadcloth, cuffs, collar, skirts, universities, libraries, modern coin names, watches, modern geographical names, etc.

115What was the language from which the Book of Mormon was translated? [^28] The historians of the book reported that their original Hebrew language had been changed, and that the Egyptian characters had been altered and adapted for the writing of the Hebrew language for space-saving and taught specially, usually father to son. These characters were referred to as "reformed Egyptian," and for this reason the historians report it was harder to avoid mistakes, which they said were faults of the scribes and not of God. [^29] They also said that "none other people knoweth our language." [^30] The practice of writing one language in the alphabet of another is very common. [^31] It was just a few years ago that Turkish was changed from Arabic to Roman alphabet. The problem of the historians is very roughly like writing English using modified Chinese characters.

116Dr. Nibley has recently called attention to many new and interesting Book of Mormon sidelights which fit into the times and civilizations of the Old World which the Book of Mormon peoples left behind. A good number of them have to do with language, some of which are given below. Dr. Nibley [^32] in a discussion of the proper names of Lehi and his people in the Book of Mormon finds that Hebrew and Egyptian names together make up the overwhelming majority of the names and occur in about equal strength, which is reasonable since both languages were used among them. Hittite, Arabic and Ionian elements are also found. Such names as Ammon, Helaman, Korihor, Nephi and many others are discussed with their old-world counterparts. The name Lehi has been found as a place name and also as a personal name, Lemuel in an Edomite text from the south desert and Laman as a place name. [^33] Laman is also found in America in several places as a place and language name [^34] More popular than these names is that of Alma among the Arabs as also among the Nephites. [^35] For some reason the Jews at the beginning of the sixth century B. C. would have nothing to do with Baal names as shown in the Elephantine papyri list of 400. [^36] Baal names were previously common in the Old Testament. The Book of Mormon has no Baal names.

117Dr. Nibley has written: [^37]

117"What did Joseph Smith, translator of the Book of Mormon, know about the Old World? So much seems certain, that he knew:

117(1) A number of typically Egyptian names, queer-sounding words in no way resembling Hebrew or any other language known to the world of Joseph Smith's time.

117(2) He knew the sort of plot and setting in which those names would figure in the Old World and seems quite at home on the Egyptian scene.

117(3) He gives a clear and correct picture of cultural relationships between Egypt and Israel, with due emphasis on its essentially commercial nature, in the remarkably convincing picture of Lehi-a typical merchant prince of the seventh century B.C. The picture of life in the ancient east which the Book of Mormon allows us to reconstruct is the more wonderful in the light of those fantastic conceptions of the gorgeous East which be-dizened the heads of even the best scholars at the time the book came forth." There are 181 original words used as nouns in the Book of Mormon. There are also 53 nouns found in the Bible which are also used in the Book of Mormon with new and added identifications, and 88 Bible nouns which refer only to Bible persons, places or things. [^38]

117It is also interesting to note that, except for two, the Jaredite names end in consonants and most Nephite names end in a vowel. [^39] The originality of the large number of Book of Mormon words is excellent evidence that the untrained Joseph Smith who translated the volume in something over 60 days was getting the new words from an original account which he was translating. The invention of new words is not easy and few authors invent many.

118The new words are not of classical derivation. Joseph Smith [^40] wrote: "There was no Greek or Latin upon the plates from which I, through the grace of God, translated the Book of Mormon."

118The word "deseret" which means honeybee (Ether 2:3) is from the Jaredite account. "Now it is a remarkable coincidence that the word deseret, or something very close to it, enjoyed a position of ritual prominence among the founders of the classical Egyptian civilization, who associated it very closely with the symbol of the bee." [^41] The word is dsrt, the vowels were not written then.

118The practice of the most ancient languages of the Near East of ending words in m called mimation is found in the only Jaredite common nouns curelom and cumon and the only adjective shelem. [^42]

118The real basis for the apparent peculiarity of language is because the English follows too literally the Hebrew original resulting in an English with unusual forms and arrangements. E. Craig Bramwell has given a detailed discussion of a large number of these. [^43]

118The word "and" occurs as about 3.5 per cent of the words in Joseph Smith's own prose, but the Book of Mormon has 6 per cent, where many of the sentences begin with "and"; but this is very seldom the ease in Joseph Smith's own prose. The Old Testament contains also about 6 per cent "ands". The Hebrews were an "and" loving people. [^44]

119What about this phrase "and it came to pass," which has been noticed so much? Dr. S. B. Sperry [^45] has pointed out:

119"The Hebrew verb 'hayah' meaning 'to be or become' is commonly used in narration in the sense 'to come to pass.' An irregular form of the verb with a certain Hebrew letter before it is often translated 'and it came to pass.' The latter expression in the Book of Mormon is thus explained on the basis of the Hebrew. Not being an expert in the use of English, the Prophet Joseph Smith did not vary the rendering of it sufficiently to avoid monotony."

119Elders E. Cecil McGavin and A. S. Reynolds [^46] note that the phrase "and it came to pass" is characteristic of an abridgment both in the Bible and the Book of Mormon. It occurs 386 times in the Old Testament and 81 times in the New Testament. It is entirely missing from 15 books of the Bible, and also entirely absent in the Moroni appendix of nearly 7,000 words which Moroni adds to the abridgment of the rest of the Book of Mormon. Though the phrase occurs 1,382 times in the Book of Mormon, every time it is in the midst of summarized material.

119Though Moroni used the words "one hundred seventeen times in the thirty-six pages of Jaredite history he does not use them in his own book at the close of the volume. Ezra, the (Biblical) scribe, did the same with his compilations, freely employing this Hebrew idiom in his abridgment, but never once using it in his own writing." [^47]

119Those who do not like to read the phrase can read the Book of Mormon in the edition arranged and edited by Genet Bingham Dee [^48] where it has been mostly left out.

120As an example of where the Hebrew has been followed too closely we read in Jac. 5:2 "Hearken, O ye house of Israel, and hear the words of me, a Prophet of the Lord." We would write "and hear my words" instead of "hear the words of me." [^49]

120 Nephi tells that the brass plates contained "many prophecies which have been spoken by the mouth of Jeremiah." "By the mouth" is not ordinary English. The expression is perfectly good Hebrew and seems to have been translated literally by Joseph Smith. [^50]

120 Nephi referred to his homeland with the expression "the land of Jerusalem," though he may not have lived in the city. The Amarna letters show that at that time it was the rule for a large area around a city and the people to carry the name of the city. The use of "go down" meaning "away from" Jerusalem, and "to go up" for "to go to" is in the same manner as the Hebrews and Egyptians used with reference to the location of Jerusalem. [^51] There are special expressions in English for geography of "up north," "down south," "back east," and "out west."

120The "mist of darkness" referred to in Lehi's dream has been described by travelers in all parts of Arabia. [^52] Nephi telling that his brothers were trying to leave him in the wilderness to be devoured by wild beasts has been regular procedure for treating an enemy with the Arabs. [^53] The danger from marauders while traveling in the desert of Arabia even in recent times shows the wisdom of the practice then and now of not "much fire," making it necessary to eat the food raw. [^54] Lehi's description of fruit as "white" is a typical Egyptianism. [^55] The method of giving place names by Lehi in the desert is consistent with desert practice even today. Lehi named the stream at the first camp after his eldest son and the valley after his second. [^56] These and other details given by Dr. Nibley show a picture of contemporary civilization in the account of Lehi's travel to the sea, given in incidental remarks is a true-to-life account which even scholars could not have written until the last few years. [^57]

121Elder Thomas W. Brookbank has called attention to many idioms and analogies which seem typical of Hebrew usage in the Book of Mormon, in a long series of articles: [^58]

121Elder Brookbank noted that in Hebrew "steal away the heart of" means "deceive," "dupe," or "win over," in the intellectual sense. See Genesis 31: 20, in the Hebrew text, or note the literal translation in the margin of the Authorized Version, also 2 Samuel 15: 6. And in Mosiah 27:9, "And he became a great hinderment to the prosperity of the Church of God; stealing away the hearts of the people." This we would translate "deceiving many people." See also Alma 39:4, "Yea she did steal away the hearts of many." [^59]

121Brookbank also shows that many of the proper names in the Book of Mormon are formed in a typical Hebrew manner from roots, prefixes and suffixes. It is surprising that Meyer, as an Orientalist, would not have given these words the attention of his learning instead of skipping over them.

121Another type of Hebrew use which led to trouble has been discussed by A. S. Reynolds [^60] called an enallage, which in the examples given means the changing of a singular form of the verb to the plural in the first case, and the changing of a plural for a singular relative pronoun in the other. Mosiah 18:8 was changed from "Here is the waters of Mormon," to "Here are the waters of Mormon." The other example is from Alma 1:17, "Now the law could have no power on any man for their belief," was changed to "Now the law could have no power on any man for his belief," to conform with English usage. The original translations in the first edition followed the Hebrew too literally.

122Thomas W. Brookbank [^61] pointed out the use of the enallage further, which in the example is to be noted in the changing from singular to plural, or vice versa, in the same sentence.

122 Ex. 22:22, 23 reads: "Ye shall not afflict any widow, or fatherless child. If thou afflict them in any wise, and they cry at all unto me, I will surely hear their cry."

122 Deut. 8:19 reads: "And it shall be, if thou do at all forget the Lord thy God, and walk after other gods, and serve them and worship them, I testify against you this day, that ye shall surely perish."

122In the Book of Mormon we find in 1 Ne. 17:19: "We knew that ye could not construct a ship. For we knew that ye were lacking in judgment; wherefore thou canst not accomplish so great a work."

122And in 1 Ne. 17:12: "For the Lord had not hitherto suffered that we should make much fire, as we journeyed in the wilderness; for he said, I will make thy food become sweet, that ye cook it not." Another construction permissible in Hebrew but not in good English is the double negative. [^62] Mosiah 3:17 was changed from the literal "nor no other way nor means" to "nor any other way nor means."

122The Book of Mormon is no harder reading than the Old Testament.

122The critics quoted at the beginning of the talk make a great deal out of a seeming uniformity of style. It would appear that they had been more interested in noticing the English of a too-literal translation from the Hebrew than in actually examining the style.

123There is a marked difference in style between the different historians of the Book. The accounts of the two main records, those of the Nephites and Jaredites, are very different. The Jaredite account (Book of Ether) is a straightforward narration without the ornate Hebrew literally embellishments in the Nephite story. [^63]

123The Book of Mormon was written by many historians, but the bulk of it by four men. [^64] Although almost all the book was abridged and then translated the individuality of style is very marked. Mormon and Moroni are much more direct in expressing their ideas than Nephi. [^65]

123In Hebrew and other languages "son" and "descendant" are both expressed by the same word, hence ben in Hebrew sometimes means "son" and sometimes "descendants." [^66] In the genealogy in Ether 1:6, 16, 23 there are 3 times in the 30 names when the word "descendant" occurs instead of "son."

123The tendency to repetition of Nephi, mentioned by Meyer, is absent in the synopsis by Mormon. [^67] The part from the smaller plates of Nephi in the first of the Book of Mormon is free from the explanations and interpolations by the editors, which is marked in the part Mormon abridged. [^68]

123The vocabulary of the Book of Mormon is 5665 words compared to the Bible's about 6000 but the Bible is twice as long. [^69]

123Another count of the Book of Mormon leaving out all inflected forms of the words found 2896 general words, 245 person's names, 166 place names for a total of 3307. The Doctrine and Covenants has ordinary words 2445, person's names 230, place names 47, for a total of 2722. The vocabularies are of the same order especially when allowance is made for the greater compass of the Book of Mormon in time and history. [^70]

124The style is not as direct and condensed as the Bible in the English translation, as George Reynolds comments in the preface to his monumental Concordance of the Book of Mormon. [^71] Using this concordance, anyone can make a study of the differences in language of the different writers. T.W. Brookbank, has made a painstaking, detailed study in which he found, for example, that the book of Alma contains 675 words which are found in no other book, many of them many-syllabled. There is an avalanche of cultured words as would be expected from the governors, great warriors, prophets, priests and highly educated people. Mosiah uses 559 words that are not used in the first 133 pages of the Book of Mormon preceding it, and of these 203 are used in no other part of the book. The surprising fact that Elder Brookbank's work shows, is that instead of the new words becoming fewer as the vocabulary becomes relatively fixed and complete as the book progresses, the rate of introduction of new words is maintained at a high rate; the last book, Moroni, is next highest in the number of new words introduced, in spite of coming at the end of the book. A comparison of words ending in "-eth" shows that the books of First and second Nephi contain six times as many as any other book, and half of them are used only in First and Second Nephi. No wonder a tongue can get twisted in that part. First and Second Nephi are also strong in words ending in "-ing" and "-ly." Ether and Moroni have almost no words using these suffixes.

125 Professor Moyle Q. Rice [^72] in a master's thesis has analyzed many of the characteristic features of the language and observed: [^73] "Stylistic differences in the work are quite easily detectedÂ�." Marked differences in the style of the various authors, in vocabulary, in sentence structure and in literary background can also be noted.

125Indeed, Joseph Smith's style in the translation shows that, although he was making the translation of an abridgment, still the difference of style of the original authors appears markedly in the English. The English of the translation was different from Joseph Smith's own natural prose style, yet it reflected his untrained English and the speech of the community. The correct, too-literal translation of Hebraic phrases and constructions which largely have been smoothed out in our English Bible, but which appear in the Book of Mormon, and the lack of mention of modern customs and practices, indicate the Book of Mormon is a translation of records of people with an early Near East background.

125In conclusion, it would appear therefore, that those very characteristics of language of which the early critics made so much, have become in the light of modern scholarship, one of the strongest evidences in support of the claim that the Book of Mormon is a translation from ancient records.
