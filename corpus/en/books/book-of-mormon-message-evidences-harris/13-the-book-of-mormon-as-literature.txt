Chapter 12

The Book of Mormon As Literature

In an article written about 1840 in the New Yorker signed by "Josephine," the following comments were made about the Book of Mormon. [^1]

126"One of the greatest literary curiosities of the day, is the much abused Book of Mormon. That a work of the kind should be planned, executed and given to the scrutiny of the world by an illiterate young man of twenty-that it should gain numerous and devoted partisans, here and in Europe, and that it should agitate a whole state to such a degree that law, justice and humanity were set aside to make a war of extermination on the new sect, seems scarcely credible in the nineteenth century, and under this liberal government; yet such is the fact.

126"The style is in close imitation of the scriptural, and is remarkably free from any allusions that might betray a knowledge of the present political or social state of the world. The writer lives in the whole strength of his imagination in the age he portrays. It is difficult to imagine a more difficult literary task than to write what may be termed a continuation of the Scriptures, that should not only avoid all collision with the authentic and sacred word, but even to fill in many chasms that seem to exist; and thus receive and lend confirmation in almost every book."

126This powerful book has the qualities which "Josephine" ascribes to it. What are its other literary values? It has stood the test of time, which is one criterion of literature. It is "distinguished by artistic form or emotional appeal. [^2]

126We may recall that in the last chapter it was pointed out that the language of the Book of Mormon itself is rather simple, limited by the lack of training of the translator Joseph Smith and his knowledge of English.

127It was shown that there is a great difference in style between the different writers in the Book of Mormon. The Hebrew original language of the book is evident in the English because of the too literal translation of the original in phrase, construction and manner of expression.

127To get a picture of the type of material in the Book of Mormon, [^3] Elder George Q. Morris has noted that the book contains 239 chapters, of which 54 deal with wars, 21 are historical, 55 on visions and prophecies, 71 on doctrines and exhortations, 17 on missionary work and 21 with the ministry of Christ.

127The book is made up of 15 books, 239 chapters, 6,604 verses and 306,118 words. [^4]

127The Book of Mormon was not written as fiction. No writer in a fictional work would give prophetic previews of the rest of the book as are given in First and Second Nephi. Nor would a fictional writer expect to interest the literary world of the nineteenth century with the details of the organization on the American continent of a church like the primitive church in Palestine, together with a discussion of its doctrines. [^5]

127Although the book can and has been read in a single day, [^6] like any great literature it is worthy of careful study and re-reading.

128There are two forthcoming books which will give detailed discussion about the Book of Mormon as literature, Dr. S. B. Sperry's Our Book of Mormon [^7] and Elder E. Cecil McGavin's Literary Aspects of the Book of Mormon. [^8] We may note, however, some of the types of literary forms in the book.

128Many forms of literary technique have been employed, figures of speech such as similes, metaphors, hyperboles and others, narration, exposition and description, oratory, [^9] epic (missionary travels of Alma and the dealings of Nephi with his brothers), lyric, [^10] logic, [^11] in Moroni's argument to unbelievers, parables as in the parable of the tame and the wild olive tree [^12]; also prayers, songs of praise and patriarchal admonition. [^13]

128Three interesting types of Hebrew literary forms are found in the Book of Mormon. [^14] Examples of each will be given.

128The First has been called synonymous parallelism. The second line reinforces the thought of the first by repeating it in different form. [^15]

128"They wandered many days in the wilderness. "Even forty days did they wander." [^16] And:

128"Therefore, let us glory,

129 "Yea, we will glory in the Lord;

129"Yea, we will rejoice, for our joy is full;

129"Yea we will praise our God forever." [^17]

129The second type is antithetical parallelism in which the thought of the first line is emphasized, or confirmed by a contrasted thought expressed in the second line. The examples are all from 2 Nephi. [^18]

129"To be carnally-minded is death,

129"And to be spiritually-minded is life eternal."

129"The words of truth are hard against all uncleanness;

129"But the righteous fear them not,

129"For they love the truth and are not shaken."

129"My soul abhorreth sin,

129"And my heart delighteth in righteousness."

129The third literary device is called an antinomy. It is effective by showing sharp contrasts and extreme opposites. In the second chapter of 2 Nephi, there are twenty-two such opposites, such as holiness versus wickedness, good and bad, wisdom and foolishness, joy and misery, carnel and spiritual, bitter and sweet.

129Another type of literary expression discovered by A. S. Reynolds is the sorites, which is a series of syllogism leading up a conclusion, wherein the predicate of the preceding forms the subject of the succeeding sentence. Of the many found in the book we give one: [^19]

129"If ye say there is no law, ye shall say there is no sin.

129"If there is no sin, there is no righteousness.

129If there be no righteousness there be no happiness.

129If there be no righteousness nor happiness there be no punishment nor misery.

129"If there be no happiness nor misery, there be no reward nor punishment.

129"If these things are not, there is no God.

129"If there is no God we are not, neither the earth; for there could have been no creation,

"Neither of things to act nor to be acted upon; wherefore, all things must have vanished away."

130One of the fine sections in the book is Nephi's psalm, or song of lamentation, written soon after his father Lehi's death. [^20] It has been arranged in poetic form by a number of people. The arrangement here is that of J. M. Sjodahl Genet Bingham Dee's Voice from the Dust, has arranged many sections of the book as free verse and other literary devices including varied type, illustrations, captions and notes.

Nephi's Psalm

Behold, my soul delighteth in the things of the Lord; And my heart pondereth continually upon the things which I have seen and heard. Nevertheless, notwithstanding the great goodness of the Lord in showing me His great and marvelous works, my heart exclaimeth: "O wretched man that I am!" Yea, my heart sorroweth because of my flesh; My soul grieveth because of mine iniquities

130I am encompassed about Because of the temptations and the sins which do so easily beset me. And when I desire to rejoice, my heart groaneth because of my sins. Nevertheless, I know in whom I have trusted; My God hath been my support. He hath led me through mine afflictions in the wilderness; And He hath preserved me upon the waters of the great deep. He hath filled me with His love, Even unto the consuming of my flesh. He hath confounded mine enemies, Unto the causing of them to quake before me.

131 Behold, He hath heard my cry by day And He hath given me knowledge by visions in the night-time. And by day have I waxed bold in mighty prayer before Him. Yea, my voice have I sent up on high, And angels came down and ministered unto me. And upon the wings of His Spirit hath my body been carried away upon exceeding high mountains And mine eyes have beheld great things, yea, even too great for man; Therefore, I was bidden that I should not write them.

131O, then, if I have seen so great things, If the Lord in His condescension unto the children of men hath visited man in so much mercy, Why should my heart weep and my soul linger in the valley of sorrow, And my flesh waste away, and my strength slacken because of mine afflictions? And why should I yield to sin because of my flesh? Yea, why should I give way to temptations, that the evil one have place in my heart, to destroy my peace and afflict my soul? Why am I angry because of mine enemy?

131Awake, my soul! No longer droop in sin, Rejoice, O my heart, and give place no more for the enemy of my soul Do not anger again because of mine enemies Do not slacken my strength because of mine afflictions. Rejoice, O my heart, and cry unto the Lord and say: "O Lord, I will praise Thee forever; Yea, my soul will rejoice in Thee, my God and the rock of my salvation.'

131The first three verses of 1 Nephi, different from the rest of the text following, are a typical colophon, a literary device that is highly characteristic of Egyptian compositions, such as in the Bremer-Rhind Papyrus. Nephi gives first his name, than the merits of his parents with special attention to the learning of his father and an avowal that the record is true, and "I make it with mine own hand." Egyptian literary writings regularly close with the formula iw-f-pw "thus it is" as does Nephi. [^21]

132"Lehi's didactic spirit and his habit of giving long formal addresses on moral and religious subjects 'in the manner of the fathers' to his sonsÂ� The form of these discourses, with their set introductions and formal imagery might have come right out of an Egyptian school-room, though their content smacks more of the 'learning of the Jews.'" [^22]

132Lehi in speaking to Laman and Lemuel [^23] uses the form of the earliest type of poetry of the desert, the qasid, as shown by 7 characteristics which can be sensed even in the English. [^24] The part to Lemuel reads:

132"O that thou mightest be like unto this valley, firm and steadfast, And immovable in keeping the commandments of the Lord!"

132There are a number of letters in the book, the majority are war epistles which are recorded in Alma. [^25] The letters contain information on events during a war between the Nephites and the Lamanites. General Moroni's second letter [^26] to Pahoran, the political head of the government, contains these stirring words:

132"Can you think to sit upon your thrones in a state of thoughtless stupor, while your enemies are spreading the work of death around you? Yea, while they are murdering thousands of your brethren?"

132Just prior to his death, Mormon wrote two letters to his son, Moroni, [^27] in which he shows great affection for a faithful son. In his second letter he writes:

133"My beloved son. I write unto you again that ye may know that I am yet alive.Â� I have had a sore battle with the Lamanites, in which we did not conquerÂ�. We have lost a great number of our choice men."

133One of the earliest detective stories in history is given in [^28] describing the murder of the chief-judge and the trapping of the murderer.

133There are stories of many campaigns and of the tactics and strategy of the armies involved. The general Moroni in the first century B.C. was a clever and resourceful leader. He fought successfully against Zerahemnah, Ammonihah and Ammoron.

133There are many sermons and discourses in the book. Elder Roy A. West has given a list. [^29] Readers of the book will recall the powerful discourses of King Benjamin, Abinadi, Alma, Amulek, Nephi and Samuel for examples. There are sublime teachings on many subjects.

133One, for example, recalls Alma on the resurrection; [^30] Mormon on good and evil, faith, hope and charity; [^31] and Moroni on faith. [^32] Nephi's final testimony [^33] is the testimony of a fine, sincere man of deep convictions and who loves his fellow men. There are a whole series of discussions or debates on various problems, mostly spiritual; Nephi vs his brothers; [^34] Jacob vs Sherem; [^35] Abinadi vs the priests of Noah; [^36] Alma and Mosiah vs unbelievers; [^37] Alma II vs Nehor; [^38] Amulek vs Zeezrom; [^39] Alma vs Korihor; [^40] Moroni vs Ammoron; [^41] Samuel the Lamanite vs unbelievers. [^42]

134Thomas W. Brookbank [^43] has called attention to 2 Ne. 4 as a fine type of Christian experience. Lehi's vision (1 Ne. 11, 12) of the rod of iron leading to the love of God is one of the finest sections in the book. [^44]

134As "Josephine" pointed out in the introductory quotation, the book is perfectly natural and true to life, which is one of the tests of literary value. The description of Nephi and his relations to his brothers is simply human nature all over again. [^45]

134This typical human nature is evident in the following examples: Laman and Lemuel were reluctant to leave their gold, silver and property and go into the wilderness and said that their father Lehi was a visionary man misled by the "foolish imaginings of his heart." [^46] Sarah, as any mother, worried about her sons when they were sent back to Jerusalem. [^47] Lehi was concerned "with all the feeling of a tender parent," for his sons Laman and Lemuel who did not partake of the fruit. [^48] Lehi's great astonishment when he found the Liahona. [^49] Ishmael's daughters were discouraged by the hard life and their father's death. [^50] King Lamoni's father would rightly be "astonished" at what Ammon and Lamoni told him and what Ammon did. [^51] The Zoramites would not believe in Alma's words because of his son Corianton's misconduct. [^52]

134 John Henry Evans has noted: [^53]

135 "The Book of Mormon has rare character drawing. And this is admittedly the most difficult thing to do in imaginative work. As a rule, great writers live because of their skill in creating characters rather than because of any talent they may have for plot, setting, purpose or style. Shakespeare, George Eliot, Thackeray, Dickens, Tolstoy, Hugo-these are remembered chiefly by reason of their power in character portrayal."

135Biographies and sketches of many of the great characters have been given in various Sunday School lessons and books. [^54]

135The great man Mormon, who gives his name to the book, has been described by Leland H. Monson [^55] portraying his life as a religious leader, historian and general-the greatest military leader of his time.

135Moroni, the last of the Nephite historians, anticipated many of the events of our day and his message to us has been recently summarized. [^56] Moroni lived for thirty-six lonely years from the time his compatriots were killed in battle at Cumorah after which he wrote the preface which appears on the title page, and grimly remarked that so far he had escaped the hands of the Lamanites. [^57]

135The importance of a family of outstanding men in the Book of Mormon has been given by Elder A. F. Bennett. [^58] In his biographies of these men, who cover nine generations and nearly five hundred years, it is shown that they were great leaders of right living, servants of the people, religiously and politically, as men of affairs.

136Alma, the younger [^59] was likely the greatest intellect in the Book of Mormon. He has a wide range of knowledge, with a simple, clear discussion of many problems and on some subjects the best in Scripture. Alma was a soldier and statesman as well as a theologian.

136Are these men's lives inspiring? The following Book of Mormon names have been used as personal names: [^60] Alma, Mahonri, Moriancumer, Nephi, Lehi, Abinadi, Ammon, Mathoni, Mulek, Pahoran, Moroni, Lamoni, Zenos, Ether, Jacob, Joseph, Gideon, Zoram, Mathoni-hah, Teancum, Laman, Lemuel, Shiblon, Zeniff, Helaman, Jared, Mormon, Mosiah, Jared.

136The place names of Bountiful, Manti, Deseret, Lehi, Nephi, Moroni in Utah and Lemhi (for Limhi) [^61] and Ammon in Idaho and Lehi in Arizona are due to the Book of Mormon.

136 Nephi has made a great impression on many people. The late President Heber J. Grant [^62] said:

136"I read the Book of Mormon as a young man, and fell in love with Nephi more than with any other character in profane or sacred history that I ever read of, except the Savior of the world. No other individual has made such a strong impression upon me as did Nephi. He has been one of the guiding stars of my life."

139The Book of Mormon uses the various literary forms, and has many sections and verse of free literary quality, but the most important thing is what is the net influence of the book on human lives?

139The Book of Mormon and its teachings have given the Latter-day Saints a sympathetic approach to the Indians. As Albert Reise [^87] points out:

139"Eloquently Joaquin Miller defends the cause of the native whom only Penn and the Mormons consistently treated peaceably and fairly since the savage first lifted his hands in welcome to Columbus."

140 George Q. Cannon [^88] relates his experience with the Book of Mormon:

140"Some of my readers may be placed in circumstances similar to those which surrounded me a part of the time on the Sandwich Islands, and it may be profitable to tell them how I kept from losing courage and becoming homesick. My love for home is naturally very strong. For the first year after I left home I could scarcely think about it without my feelings getting the better of me. But here I was in a distant land, among a people whose language and habits were strange to me. Their food was foreign to me, and unlike anything I had ever before seen or tasted. I was much of the time separated from my companions, the Elders. Until I mastered the language and commenced preaching and baptizing the people, I was indeed a stranger among them.

140"Before I commenced holding regular meetings I had plenty of time for meditation and to review all the events of my short life, and to think of the beloved home from which I was so far separated. It was then I found the value of the Book of Mormon. It was a book which I always loved. If I felt inclined to be lonely, to be low spirited, or home-sick, I had only to turn to its sacred pages to receive consolation, new strength and a rich outpouring of the Spirit. Scarcely a page that did not contain encouragement for such as I was. The salvation of man was the great theme upon which its writers dwelt and for this they were willing to undergo every privation and make every sacrifice.

140"What were my petty difficulties compared with those afflictions which they had to endure? If I expected to share the glory for which they contended, I could see that I must labor in the same Spirit. If the sons of King Mosiah could relinquish their high estate, and go forth among the degraded Lamanites to labor as they did, should not I labor with patience and devoted zeal for the salvation of these poor red men, heirs of the same promise?

141 "Let me recommend this book, therefore, to young and old, if they need comfort and encouragement. Especially can I recommend it to those who are away from home on missions. No man can read it, partake of its spirit and obey its teachings, without being idled with a deep love for the souls of men and a burning zeal to do all in his power to save them."

It created for them a firmer faith in God. In the presence of its spirit, doubt took wings. Its moral and spiritual standards they find to be the highest and noblest.'" [^90]
