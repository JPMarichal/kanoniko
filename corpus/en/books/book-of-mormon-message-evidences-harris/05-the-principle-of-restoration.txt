Chapter 4

The Principle of Restoration

In the last two chapters the Book of Mormon has been presented as having a universal message of law: the universality of God's regard for man; the universality of obedience to please God, and the universality of tolerance for all men, but punishment for those who injure others. In this chapter we shall consider a fundamental of temporal and spiritual progress as taught by the Book. The happiness sought by all of us for here and hereafter is of great importance to us. The Book of Mormon principle of restoration has an important bearing on our search for happiness. What is this principle of restoration?

29The principle of restoration may be characterized as being a law of conservation of thought, habits, attitudes and way of life; that the manner of life we have been following tends to persist, and that our character tends to remain the same. This emphasizes consistent effort during all of life. The total actions of a man's lifetime count. There is no legerdemain to get blessings from a capricious God. Punishment is the natural result of breaking law which governs the universe. But let Alma speak from the Book: [^1]

30 Alma tells also of God's willingness to help and reward good desires and Moroni warns that good works without really honest intentions profit nothing.

30Thus we become honest by doing honest works. Restoration means a continuation of development in the same direction, a conservation in way of life. The emphasis is on consistent effort all during life, on the total actions of man. There is no legerdemain method of getting blessings. Nephi [^2] carries the discussion into the consequences in the hereafter:

30"If they should die in their wickedness they must be cast off also, as to the things which are spiritual, which are pertaining to righteousness; wherefore, they must be brought to stand before God, to be judged of their works; and if their works have been filthiness they must be filthy; and if they be filthy it mustÂ� be that they cannot dwell in the kingdom of God, if so, the kingdom of God must be filthy also. 31And Amulek is very much concerned with what we should do now and in this life. He speaks of our present responsibility in this manner:

31"For behold, this life is the time for men to prepare to meet God; yea, behold the day of this life is the day for men to perform their labors. And now, as I said unto you before, as ye have had so many witnesses, therefore, I beseech of you that ye do not procrastinate the day of your repentance until the end; for after this day of life, which is given us to prepare for eternity, behold if we do not improve our time in this life then cometh the night of darkness wherein there can be no labor performed.

31This denies that death-bed repentance can annul a wasteful and wicked life and enable one to escape justice.

31Or in another way as Joseph Smith [^5] expressed it: "Daily transgression and daily repentance is not that which is pleasing in the sight of God." And "The Spirit of the Lord will not always strive with man." [^6]

32 Moroni continues the discussion wherein he examines how to know good from evil: [^7]

32"The spirit of Christ is given to every man, that he may know good from evilÂ� Everything which inviteth to do good, and to persuade to believe in Christ, is sent forth by the power and gift of Christ; wherefore ye may know with a perfect knowledge it is of God. But whatsoever thing persuadeth men to do evil, and believe not in Christ, and deny him, and serve not God is of the devil."

32The devil is an actual spirit. [^8]

32Now the effect of the spirit of the Lord is to make men do good. [^9] Or as Alma expresses it: [^10]

32"Counsel with the Lord in all thy doings, and he will direct thee for good."

32And King Benjamin's people exclaimed:

32The Book of Mormon was written to do good for men, [^12] and its value can be judged by this test of its influence for good.

32In connection with the evaluation of this life in a judgment in the next world the Book of Mormon has some clarifying information.

32After this life there is a judgment [^13] based on actual enlightenment received, [^14] but through a misinterpretation of the Bible, the Christian world after the ministry of Christ was misled into a literal belief in physical punishment for all misdeeds. The history of this fearful perversion is given by Hypatia B. Bonner in The Christian Hell. [^15] This type of punishment is given in excellent literary description by Dante in his Divine Comedy, and pictured by DorÃ© in his famous illustrations for that book. This doctrine was firmly entrenched and preached in 1830 when the Book of Mormon was published. The Book of Mormon states, however, that the real basis of the doctrine is a simple law of justice, with the remorse of conscience being the torture for evil deeds done or good works neglected. [^16]

33In the words of Amulek: [^17]

33"We shall be brought to stand before God, knowing even as we know now, and have a bright recollection of all our guilt."

33Or as Moroni [^18] expresses it:

33"Do ye suppose that ye shall dwell with him (the Lamb of God) under a consciousness of your guilt? Do ye suppose that ye could be happy to dwell with that holy being, when your souls are racked with a consciousness of guilt that ye have ever abused his lawsÂ�? Ye would be more miserable to dwell with a holy and just God, under a consciousness of your filthiness before him, than ye would be to dwell with the damned souls in hellÂ�. When ye shall be brought to see your nakedness before God, and also the glory of God and the holiness of Jesus Christ it will kindle a flame of unquenchable fire upon you."

33 Alma reports that with Zeezrom, "His many other sins did harrow up his mindÂ� under a consciousness of his own guiltÂ� he began to be encircled about by the pains of hell.'' [^19]

34 The endless torment is because God is endless and therefore justice cannot be escaped, instead of the duration of the punishment being endless. Punishment is given only until the demands of justice are met. [^20]

34Some ever-present sophistries are mentioned by Nephi:

34"And there shall be many which shall say: Eat, drink, and be merry, for tomorrow we die; and it shall be well with us. AndÂ� many which shall say Eat, drink and be merry; nevertheless, fear God-he will justify in committing a little sin; lie a little, take the advantage of one because of his words, dig a pit for thy neighbor; there is no harm in this; and do all these things, for tomorrow we die; and if it so be that we are guilty, God will beat us with a few stripes, and at last we shall be saved in the kingdom of God." [^21]

34The Book of Mormon emphasizes that we cannot escape justice or the consequences of our way of life. The principle of restoration is then the conservation of desire, works and manner of living. This does not mean that a person cannot repent and turn to righteousness here or hereafter, and get the proportionate rewards of right living. The mercy of God is in effect here and hereafter. The problem of the relation between justice and mercy is treated by Alma. [^22]

34The Book of Mormon in its treatment of punishment in the hereafter portrays the remorse of conscience as the means of meeting the demands of justice. The principle of restoration shows that a manner of thinking and living tends to stay the same and also that it is the sum of the actions during life which counts, hence the importance of now and in the future leading the best life we can with the help of God to obtain happiness.
