Franklin S. Harris, Book of Mormon: Message and Evidences ( 1961)

Chapter 13

Christ in Ancient America

In previous chapters part of the message of the Book of Mormon has been discussed as well as some features of the civilizations of its peoples. The greatest interest and value of the Book of Mormon, however, is in its description of the visit of Jesus Christ, after his resurrection, to the Western Hemisphere. Christ had told his disciples previously in Palestine: [^1]

142"Other sheep I have, which are not of this fold: them also I must bring, and they shall hear my voice; and there shall be one fold, and one shepherd."

142In brief the Book of Mormon tells that Christ visited the Western Hemisphere and instituted a church with the same organization and principles as in Palestine. Such a great event should have made a mighty impression on those peoples and been reflected, even many centuries after, in their legends, beliefs and practices, distorted as these may have become in 1900 years. In this chapter we shall consider some of the evidences found in America which confirm such a visit, and in Chapter 14 we shall give the Book of Mormon description of the visit.

142In considering the legendary evidence it is well to keep in mind that as traditions, legends and myths go from generation to generation, they become greatly changed. Parts are left out of the stories, some are changed, and new parts added; sometimes several stories may be confused or combined. The stories which have accumulated about George Washington and Abraham Lincoln in a few decades may serve as modern illustrations. The interpreters and translators have a bias which may affect the account. The wide variety of interpretations of the intentions and value of the late President Roosevelt, or President Truman for that matter, depends on the point of view of the individual. These factors make it difficult to be positive in interpretation of stories from Ancient America, especially with the limitations of the available information. But there is much information that is valuable for study on the beliefs and practices as found in the available records.

143The most important feature of the traditions of ancient America at the time of the discovery was the almost universal legend of the visit of a great god or hero centuries before, who had made great changes in the civilization and religious practices of the peoples. There is great variation in the stories, and elements in some of the stories show a confusion with other stories, and mixing in with accounts of later kings, in some cases. A few references from authorities will show the universality of the story.

143T. A. Joyce [^2] remarks:

143"The next feature of Chibcha mythological history is one common to all the cultural peoples of Central and South America, namely the arrival of a white culture-hero who gave the people laws and instructed them in arts and industries. Such is the Quetzalcoatl of the Nahua, the Uiracocha of the Peruvians, and the Tsuma of Venezuela. This personage is known variously as Bochica, Nemterequeteba, and XueÂ�"

143The late Dr. George C. Vaillant [^3] tells us:

143"Quetzalcoatl, 'the Feathered Serpent,' God of Civilization and the planet Venus, seems to have been widely venerated but under different guises Â�.Quetzalcoatl had several forms shared by distinctive divinities. The sculptures of Teotihuacan and Chichen Itza show that a feathered snake was honored, and the local records mention Quetzalcoatl and Kukulcan, Nahuatl and Maya names having the same meaning."

143Quetzalcoatl and other culture-heroes of America were associated with the serpent as a symbol, which recalls the Semitic-Sumerian serpent symbol of immortality. [^4]

144The serpent was used as a symbol for Christ by Moses [^5] of which Christ said: [^6]

144"And as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, even so must the Son of man be lifted up: that whosoever believeth in him should not perish but have everlasting life."

144Now the Book of Mormon peoples understood this symbolism, as Nephi, the son of Helaman, taught just before the birth of Christ: [^7]

144"Did he (Moses) not bear record that the Son of God should come? And as he lifted up the brazen serpent in the wilderness, even so shall he be lifted up who should come. And as many as should look upon that serpent should live, even so as many as should look upon the Son of God with faith, having a contrite spirit, might live, even unto that life which is eternal."

144Dr. H. B. Alexander in his excellent Latin-American Mythology, [^8] found in most libraries, may be used as a summary:

144"From their remote beginnings, as with other peoples whose traditions lead back into an age of migrations, the Mayan tribes remembered culture-heroes, tutors in the arts as well as founders of empire, priests as well as kings, who may have been historic, but who in origin were probably gods rather than men-gods whom time had confused with the persons of their priestly or royal worshippers, and in whose deeds, cosmic and historic events were distortedly intermingled. Tales of three such heroes hold a central place in Mayan mythology: Votan, the hero of the Tzental legend, whose name is associated with Palenque and the tradition of a great 'Votanic Empire' of times long past; Zamna or Itzamna, a Yucatec hero; and Kukulcan, known to the Quiche as Gucumatz, who is the Mayan equivalent of Quetzalcoatl. All three of these hero-deities are reputed to have come from afar-strange in costume and in custom-to have been the inventors or teachers of writing, and to have founded new cults."

145Many other authorities [^9] could be quoted.

145These accounts refer to events of many centuries before. Henry R. Wagner, [^10] says:

145"All the native writers assure us that this legend, for legend it was, had been generally believed in by the Mexicans for a long time; in fact, it may have originated several hundred years before."

145In the Aztec belief, Quetzalcoatl was the divine ruler of the second era of the creation. [^11]

145That Quetzalcoatl was a white or fair god and was to return does not seem to be contained in the earliest accounts. [^12] This tradition seems to be well-established. [^13]

145In contrast to the other gods, Quetzalcoatl was gentle. [^14] In fact his character was such as to elicit the following remarkable statements from J. T. Short: [^15]

145"The doctrines of the benign and saintly Quetzalcoatl or Kukulcan must be classed among the great faiths of mankind, and their author, alone of all the great teachers of morals except Christ himself, inculcating a positive morality, must be granted a precedence of most of the great teachers of Chinese and Hindoo antiquity."

146Or as Prescott [^16] remarked:

146"None of the deities of the country (Mexico) suggested such astonishing analogies with Scripture as Quetzalcoatl."

146F. A. MacNutt [^17] has written: "The identity of Quetzalcoatl remains an unsolved mystery. So numerous and striking were the analogies to Christian teachings presented by the Mexican beliefs and rituals that the conviction has obtained amongst many, that this mysterious personage was no other than a Christian priest or bishop. The Mexican traditions concerning his appearance amongst the Toltecs, his teachings, his miracles, and his final disappearance, seem to be hopelessly interwoven with legends of other deities, his personality became merged in that of other mythical characters, with a plumed serpent for his emblem; but there still remained a sufficient number of intelligible and authentic doctrines and practices traceable to him, to argue their Christian origin."

146The historian Bancroft [^18] concluded:

146"His (Quetzalcoatl's) teachings, according to the traditions, had much in common with those of Christ in the Old World."

146D. G. Brinton [^19] tells us:

146"We thus arrive, still in primitive conditions, to such personal ideals as Quetzalcoatl among the Aztecs of whom it was said in their legends that he was of majestic presence, chaste in life, averse to war, wise and generous in actions, and delighting in the cultivation of the arts of peace; or, as we see among the Peruvians, in their culture hero, Tonapa, of whose teachings a Catholic writer of the sixteenth century says, 'So closely did they resemble the precepts of Jesus, that nothing was lacking in them but his name and that of his Father.'"

147T. A. Willard in his popular account Kukulcan, The Bearded Conqueror, [^20] notes:

147"Thus passes Itzamna, this reputed son of a god, perhaps our Christian God under another name, and the Itzas believed that his soul went to dwell with his heavenly Father.'"

147The Book of Mormon [^21] and the Bible [^22] tell that Christ wept when greatly moved. Viracocha, in Peru at Tiahuanaco was represented with tears coursing down the cheeks, which has resulted in his being called the Weeping God of Tiahuanaco [^23] In the artistic representation, Kukulcan is usually shown with teardrops under the eyelids.

147The sign of the birth of Christ had been predicted by the Book of Mormon prophet, Samuel the Lamanite [^24] as being two days and a night of continuous light, together with a new star. This happened as described. [^25] In the New Testament, the birth of Christ had a star as a sign [^26] and Christ is referred to as the morning star. [^27]

147In traditions there seems to be some support for a night without darkness. Juarros [^28] tells of a day when three suns were seen. Ixtlilxochitl and Veytia refer to a tradition of the sun stopping still for a whole day in its course. [^29] Bastian reports that at the disappearance of Quetzalcoatl, both sun and moon were covered and a single star appeared in the heavens. [^30]

148 "Quetzalcoatl was sometimes depicted as a white Tezcatlipoca, associable with the east as a morning star and the west as an evening star." [^31] Quetzalcoatl is sometimes called the God of Venus. [^32]

148At the death of Christ, as recorded in the Book of Mormon, [^33] there was a great destruction by earthquakes, winds, fire and darkness which lasted for three days. Isaac B. Ball [^34] has shown that the Book of Mormon account is a good description of the action and effects of hurricanes and earthquakes. There may also have been volcanic action. Wagner [^35] has suggested that the tradition of the great wind that was raised when Quetzalcoatl was deposed as god seems to have some bearing on his worship as god of the air or wind.

148There are many traditions of darkness, earthquakes and great destruction in the far past. Ixtlilxochitl, [^36] for example, gives a Toltec tradition that some time after the invention of the Toltec calendar, "the sun and moon were eclipsed, the earth shook, and the rocks were rent asunder, and many other things and signs happened, though there was no loss of life."

148The Pedregal lava sheet which flowed centuries ago from the volcano Xitli in Central Mexico covered at least two ruins near Copilco and Cuicuilco just south of Mexico City. These cultures flourished for perhaps a thousand years around the time of Christ. [^37]

148About the time of Christ there were cataclysmic volcanic eruptions in Central Salvador which rendered it uninhabitable and greatly influenced the rest of the people of that region to move elsewhere. [^38]

149The birth and visit of Christ made such an effect on the Book of Mormon peoples that they reckoned their years from his birth. [^39] It is interesting to note that there is a tradition that Quetzalcoatl taught the people a version of the calendar, [^40] and another that he started the year count. [^41] Torquemada says he invented the calendar. [^42] Kukulcan was the inventor of the calendar. [^43]

149Other traditions relate that Quezalcoatl founded a priesthood and that priests were named after him. Torquemada says: "He (Quetzalcoatl) had priests who were called quequetzalcohua, that is to say 'priests of the order of Quetzalcoatl.'" [^44] In the Book of Mormon priests were ordained after the order of the Son of God. [^45]

149The early Europeans were greatly surprised to find baptism being practised with somewhat similar forms and meaning to that which they knew. Baptisms among the Maya, Mexican and other American peoples runs back to a period far pre-conquest. [^46] Landa [^47] gives an account as follows:

149"Baptism is not found in any part of the Indies except in Yucatan [^48] Â� a name which means 'to be born anew or again,' which is the same as renascot in the Latin language, for in the language of Yucatan sihil means 'to be born anew or again,' and it is only used in compound words, and so caput sihil means 'to be born anew.'"

150Dr. Tozzer adds another note, in addition to that quoted in Ref. 47:

150"Also in the Relaci of (rida (1:50-51) we read, 'It has been inferred from certain ceremonies and customs which the Indians observed in the time of their heathenism that the gospel had been preached to them, because they had baptism, and they baptized in this fashion: The high priest of the idols took water and threw certain flowers into it and said certain words over it and dampened a stick and touched with it the forehead and the eyes and the face, saying three times "Ah, ah, ah," which seems to signify and mean, live again, or remember; and they could not marry nor be priests if they were not baptized; and if anyone died without baptism they believed that he would have to suffer more torments in hell than a baptized person.'"

150Continuing the direct quotation from Landa:

150"We have not been able to find its origin, more than that it is a custom that has always existed, and for which they had so much devotion that no one failed to receive it; and so much reverence for the rite that those who had sins, if they were capable of knowing they had committed them, were obliged to make a special confession of them to the priest, in order to receive baptism, and they had such great faith in it that they never repeated it in any way. That which they thought they received by it was a preliminary disposition towards being good in their way of living and not to be injured in their temporal affairs by the evil spirits, and by this means and by a well-ordered life to attain glory."

151The native Maya, Gaspar Antonio Chi, has given another description written in 1581. [^49] Other accounts of baptism in early America could be given. [^50]

151Many other apparent Christian beliefs and practices could be discussed. [^51]

151The cross of the early explorers and priests, was thought to be evidence of previous Christian influence. Later careful study has shown that the cross in Ancient America was not regarded as a crucifix. [^52] Neither was the cross used as a symbol in the early primitive Christian church. [^53]

151What has been the effect of these Christian beliefs and practices? Have they been convincing to observers?

151In 1930, Charles S. Braden [^54] in discussing Quetzalcoatl said:

151"Father Duran supposes that one of the apostles preached in Mexico; Garcia, Becerra Tanco, and particularly the eminent writer Siguenza y Gongora believe that it was the Apostle St. Thomas who left the tradition of his coming and passing which have come to surround the figure of Quetzalcoatl. The last mentioned writer has a work of 517 pages in which he deals with the subject."

151Las Casas, Oviedo, Charlevoix [^55] and others of the early Spanish writers accepted Christianity as having previously reached America. Torquemada, Acosta and some others thought that Satan had by some means taught counterfeit doctrines to prevent the Indians from accepting the true faith. [^56]

152 "It certainly seems to me that in some way (the Indians) must have received some light from the cross of our Redeemed Christ." Castaneda, chronicler of Coronado's expedition. [^57]

152Waldeck in 1838 [^58] thought there were Christian dogmas apparent in the Toltec cult. From 1831-48, Edward King, Viscount Kingsborough, published his magnificent nine-volume Antiquities of Mexico costing him $160,000 in which he gathered all possible evidence that could possibly be construed for Christianity in pre-Columbian America. During this time in 1843, George Jones published his History of Ancient America for which part of the subtitle reads "and the Introduction of Christianity into the Western Hemisphere by the Apostle St. Thomas."

152In 1880 the Mexican historian Orozco y Berra [^59] expressed himself as willing to accept some evidences of Christianity.

152At the end of the last century P. De Roo was working in the Vatican Archives on some historical problems and occasionally came across original and unpublished material pertaining to the religious history of America, either of the time of the Spanish discovery or before it. He met with, on every side, "vestiges of a Christianity which evidently was not introduced by the relatively late Northmen." One volume of his two-volume work, History of America Before Columbus, is devoted to a detailed discussion of evidences for a pre-Columbian introduction of Christianity into America.

152More recently a German archaeologist, Dr. Carl Maria Kaufmann, published Amerika und Urchristentum, [^60] setting forth some of the evidence which made him conclude that Christianity had been in the Maya and Inca realms long before the time of Columbus.

153He says:

153"In 1922 I referred to previously unknown world routes of primitive Christianity, whose study lies close to the probability of direct relations between the Old World and America in pre-Columbian times. Clues leading in this direction I met frequentlyÂ�.Christianity must have taken foot in Central and South America in an epoch which was closer to the middle of the first millennium than to its end."

153These authors have not found wide acceptance however.

153The Book of Mormon is the only account that has ever seriously asserted that Jesus Christ himself visited the Western Hemisphere. [^61] The account of that visit explains the widespread American traditions of the visit of a culture-hero having the characteristics of Christ. This hero or god, remembered by various names, taught high principles, and the signs of his birth and death are recorded in tradition. On this visit he changed the calendar, conferred the priesthood and instituted the rite of baptism.

153This short ministry of Christ in America had such lasting effects that 1900 years later his visit can be recognized by the legends and other knowledge available. Though in the last few centuries many have been impressed with various Christian resemblances in native America, the attempts to account for them have been unsatisfactory and unconvincing. The Book of Mormon with its unique story of the actual visit of Christ to America offers a challenging account of the origin of Christian beliefs and practices in pre-Columbian America.
