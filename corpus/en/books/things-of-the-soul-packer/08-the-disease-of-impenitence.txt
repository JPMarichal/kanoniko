Boyd K. Packer, Things of the Soul ( 1996)

The Disease of Impenitence [^1]

I recall an occasion when I rode on a plane with a certain M.D. There was a man across the aisle from us. After some time the M.D. nudged me and said, "That man has such-and-such disease." I asked him how he knew. He explained by pointing out the color of the man's skin, noting what he ate, how often he had gone back to the restroom, and the color of his eyes. He had noticed the signs. He said he would have to make some tests to be absolutely sure, but all the symptoms were there.

I asked him, "Why don't you do something?"

He said, "Not me!"

"Won't you say something to him?"

"No."

We got off. The man stayed on the plane, and it went on its way. I wondered why a trained M.D. would not use his skill in such a situation.

Doctor's Reluctance to Volunteer to Help

Another time I was traveling with another doctor, a surgeon, and I chided him somewhat when I told him the story. I wondered about his oath and asked why a doctor would do such a thing. He said, "You don't know much!" He then told me a similar story:

He was on a plane once when the hostess came around asking for an M.D. She asked repeatedly, and finally the surgeon got out a small medical kit he carried with him. He could see a man lying in the aisle. He went forward and checked the man out. The man had kidney stones, about as painful a thing as one can have, and he was in great pain. The pilot came back and asked what should be done. They were mid-way in flight, so the M.D. recommended they continue on and call for an ambulance at their destination. He gave the man some morphine and did all he could to make him comfortable. Before the plane landed, three other M.D.s came up to him and asked what the man's problem was. So there were four M.D.s on that plane, and only one, after a long searching of his heart, offered help.

Once I was coming from Florida on a Delta flight. Before takeoff the call came for an M.D. The stewardess repeatedly asked, and before long she was obviously in some panic. Finally an M.D. went forward. There was a delay of about an hour before we took off. The next day in Salt Lake City I read that the copilot on a Delta flight had died of a heart attack just before takeoff. We had been delayed while they took his body off the plane. The question again came: Why would a doctor refuse to act at once in such a situation?

I know why. You don't have to go through too many lawsuits to learn that you don't volunteer information that is not asked for. Medical doctors are subject to penalties by the law; people who want to enrich themselves look for every opportunity to sue for malpractice. So a doctor becomes very careful.

We Can Receive by Asking

I've come to think that perhaps we as General Authorities get to be like that. You don't have to go too often through the bitterness and resentment which occurs when you have "done damage to the patient" before you learn not to volunteer information that is not sought. You just wait: when people ask you, they receive. "Ask, and it shall be given you; seek, and ye shall find; knock, and it shall be opened unto you: for every one that asketh receiveth; and he that seeketh findeth; and to him that knocketh it shall be opened" (Matthew 7:7-8).

The Lord has also said: "Behold, I stand at the door, and knock: if any man hear my voice, and open the door, I will come in to him, and will sup with him, and he with me. To him that overcometh will I grant to sit with me in my throne, even as I also overcame, and am set down with my Father in his throne." (Revelation 3:20-21.)

We have all seen the picture of the Christ, the lantern in his hand, and the door. It is said that a young girl asked the artist why he had not painted a latch on the door. He replied that it was the door of the heart; the latch was on the inside and could be opened only by the individual.

In this chapter I want to depart from my own advice and answer a question I have not been asked. I want to impose the doctor's advice on any who may need it.

The Sin of Impenitence

My subject is the disease of impenitence and its symptoms. It is a disease that is self-cured-it is easy to cure it. Not many know the symptoms; and even if they knew, especially in their youth, they wouldn't know what to do about "heal thyself." This sin of impenitence or unrepentance takes care of itself if treated correctly. We all have a penchant for getting into trouble, even though we kind of wish we could avoid it. But I know that among the youth of the Church, even in the elite, are many who find themselves in deep problems. They didn't want to get that way, and they are not sure what to do to get out of the trouble they find themselves in; so the problem or the disease festers and feeds upon itself.

There are two categories of problems or troubles. The first includes such things as pride, intemperance, and other sins against ourselves and our divine nature-unwise indulgences. The other category contains sins that are more overt and more serious and more difficult to erase. It includes those sins which involve others. There are some things we can make restitution for. If I were to steal your automobile I could restore it somehow, even if I had to buy you a far better one. I could make it good with you; it is an elementary matter to straighten it out. This first kind of sin is fairly easy to resolve. Sometimes it takes no more than an "I'm sorry" to another, or to yourself, or to the Lord.

But the area of transgression is infinitely more difficult when you take something you can't restore. Murder is in this category; so is unchastity, as the Lord has made abundantly clear. This category also includes actions done when we enter into transgression with others.

Only One Kind of Peace

The world is rent with discord, contention, cruelty, violence, and civil war, making peace apparently elusive. We read that "they will cry peace, but there is no peace." There is only one kind of peace-peace in the heart. There is no peace in general save it exist in particular. Some with the "disease" have become involved and are "innocent." Their involvement was not willful, so they try to find a course which will give them a moment's peace. It doesn't help when others say, "It doesn't matter-others are doing it." You can get encouragement of this type from some psychiatrists but it doesn't erase the lack of peace.

There was a seminary teacher some time ago who was capable but had very serious problems. He was found to be in very bad shape: he had come unglued. But he got himself straightened out. Like some others, he had innocently (and I use that word, in place of ignorance, in the sense that he did not realize the seriousness of the issue) become involved in his youth with something. The time finally came that he had to face what he was. He sought psychiatric help, and the only advice from there was "forget it." But he couldn't forget it. When he applied the remedy, however, he was cured almost at once. The cure was there; he just had to take advantage of it.

People have come into bishops' offices with problems and it is almost as if the bishops reach out and take great weights off their shoulders and say, "Go in peace."

Peace through Forgiveness

What is it one must do? If the sin is of one category, pride and vain ambition, get it settled with the Lord. If you have harmed others, make it right with them. Brigham Young gave the Saints this advice:

You never hear me ask the people to tell their follies. But when we ask the brethren, as we frequently do, to speak in sacrament meetings, we wish them, if they have injured their neighbors, to confess their wrongs; but do not tell about your nonsensical conduct that nobody knows of but yourselves. Tell to the public that which belongs to the public. If you have sinned against the people, confess to them. If you have sinned against a family or a neighborhood, go to them and confess. If you have sinned against your Ward, confess to your Ward. If you have sinned against one individual, take that person by yourselves and make your confession to him. And if you have sinned against your God, or against yourselves, confess to God, and keep the matter to yourselves, for I do not want to know anything about it. (In Journal of Discourses 8:362.)

A short restatement of this principle might be: "If you sin against God, go to Him. Go to your brother, ask forgiveness from the innocent. What is done in secret will be forgiven in secret. What is done openly and confessed will be forgiven openly."

Let us look at a few highlight scriptural passages on the cure for this disease of impenitence.

And, again, I say unto you, that whoso having knowledge, have I not commanded to repent? (D&C 29:49.)

I don't know anyone who does not need repentance.

For I the Lord cannot look upon sin with the least degree of allowance;

Nevertheless, he that repents and does the commandments of the Lord shall be forgiven;

And he that repents not, from him shall be taken even the light which he has received; for my Spirit shall not always strive with man, saith the Lord of Hosts" (D&C 1:31-33).

In this area bishops and stake presidents find themselves acting as spiritual doctors when they see spiritual lights go out. So often they want to reach across the aisle to help. They may come to sense in a measure the Lord's feelings when he cried: "O, Jerusalem, Jerusalem, thou that killeth the prophets, Â� how often would I have gathered thy children together, even as a hen gathereth her chickens under her wings, and ye would not" (Matthew 23:37).

Behold, verily thus saith the Lord unto you, O ye elders of my church, who are assembled upon this spot, whose sins are now forgiven you, for I, the Lord, forgive sins, and am merciful unto those who confess their sins with humble hearts (D&C 61:2).

What could be more glorious than hearing that one's sins are forgiven? In history there has been a constant search for a fountain of youth. People do all kinds of things to erase the effects of time in their bodies-hairdos, makeup, exercises, and so on. But time moves on, and disintegration takes place. We can't ever make ourselves physically young again. Spiritually, however, we can be innocent and can maintain our lives in purity if we will recognize the sin of impenitence in our lives and work to rid ourselves of it. "Behold, he who has repented of his sins, the same is forgiven [and this next expression is the most beautiful in the English language for me], and I, the Lord, remember them no more" (D&C 58:42).

I'm counting on that-on going before the Lord and being able to look into his eyes and see no accusation there.

Local leaders such as bishops are called upon to handle many serious problems. Someone will come in for an interview, perhaps for a mission, and will not be worthy. Whether he or she is a youth or a mature person, ugly transgressions may be involved. Yet it is not too uncommon an experience that, if he repents, on meeting him in other circumstances later the bishop won't remember the reason for that earlier meeting. Thus the divine generosity is given in some measure even to the Lord's earthly servants, and they "remember [the sins] no more."

What is more wonderful than being clean? "By this ye may know if a man repenteth of his sins-behold, he will confess them and forsake them" (D&C 58:43).

Let me make the bottom line explicit and clear. If you must confess, go to your bishop, clear it up, and know peace.

The scriptures point out that "though your sins be as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow" (Isaiah 1:18). A marvelous cleansing spirit turns the lights on again, which puts the color back into life. When it has entered a person's life he can be in the presence of himself, his fellows, and the Lord, and be at peace.

If your sins are in the other area, repent, and go to the Lord. Agree with thine adversary quickly. I heard President David O. McKay talk on this once. He said, in effect: If you are at odds with another, go to him and seek agreement. Get it settled.

In the context of these principles, it is important that each person take a good look at himself and ask some questions. Do you have the humility to ask for forgiveness? What if you're not sure you're responsible for the problem? Can you go to the one who seems to have some things against you so that he might forgive you, or seek your forgiveness?

Some First Principles

John tells of the woman who was brought before the Lord, having been caught in the act of adultery. It was a hard case for Him. If He said she should be granted her freedom, that would be preaching against the law. There was no question about the sin. Should she be stoned as the law demanded?

Jesus seemingly ignored the accusers as he wrote on the ground with his finger. They kept after him as if saying: "Pay attention! We need a judgment here." Then He stood and said, "He that is without sin among you, let him first cast a stone at her." (See John 8:3-11.) Then comes an indication of how it struck home: the accusers all left, beginning at the eldest. He asked the woman, "Where are those thine accusers? hath no man condemned thee? She said, No man, Lord. And Jesus said unto her, Neither do I condemn thee: go, and sin no more."

There are questions you ought to ask: What is faith? How can I increase it in myself? How does one repent? How are we to seek forgiveness? These are some of the first principles we preach to the world.

One of these principles is the relationship between being forgiven and being forgiving:

Nevertheless, he has sinned; but verily I say unto you, I, the Lord, forgive sins unto those who confess their sins before me and ask forgiveness, who have not sinned unto death.

My disciples, in days of old, sought occasion against one another and forgave not one another in their hearts; and for this evil they were afflicted and sorely chastened.

Wherefore, I say unto you, that ye ought to forgive one another; for he that forgiveth not his brother his trespasses standeth condemned before the Lord; for there remaineth in him the greater sin.

I, the Lord, will forgive whom I will forgive, but of you it is required to forgive all men.

And ye ought to say in your hearts-let God judge between me and thee, and reward thee according to thy deeds.

And him that repenteth not of his sins, and confesseth them not, ye shall bring before the church, and do with him as the scripture saith unto you, either by commandment or by revelation.

And this ye shall do that God may be glorified-not because ye forgive not, having not compassion, but that ye may be justified in the eyes of the law, that ye may not offend him who is your lawgiver.

Verily I say, for this cause ye shall do these things. (D&C 64:7-14.)

Impenitence Is Widespread

There is much evil in the world today, and modern means of communication have allowed it to spread faster and further than ever before: it gets to more people. There is impenitence of all kinds in the world. We must look at ourselves. The Lord has set rules and standards and will hold us accountable unless we seek forgiveness. If we truly repent and ask for forgiveness, we will receive it. We all need to get our lives in order. I don't know of anyone who does not.

There are those who from time to time get critical of the Church or think the counsel is hard, that the leaders are not informed, and so forth. Nephi's words to his brothers come to mind: "And it came to pass that I said unto them that I knew that I had spoken hard things against the wicked, according to the truth; and the righteous have I justified, and testified that they should be lifted up at the last day; wherefore, the guilty taketh the truth to be hard, for it cutteth them to the very center. And now my brethren, if ye were righteous and were willing to hearken to the truth, and give heed unto it, that ye might walk uprightly before God, then ye would not murmur because of the truth and say: Thou speakest hard things against us." (1 Nephi 16:2-3.)

Laman and Lemuel apparently were wicked and rebellious to the end. King Benjamin explained such extreme-case consequences: "Therefore if that man repenteth not, and remaineth and dieth an enemy to God, the demands of divine justice do awaken his immortal soul to a lively sense of his own guilt, which doth cause him to shrink from the presence of the Lord, and doth fill his breast with guilt, and pain, and anguish, which is like an unquenchable fire, whose flame ascendeth up forever and ever" (Mosiah 2:38). This is a perfect description of hell.

The same prophet-king pointed out some of the joys and blessings of penitent and steadfast gospel living: "And behold, I say unto you that if ye do this ye shall always rejoice, and be filled with the love of God, and always retain a remission of your sins; and ye shall grow in the knowledge of the glory of him that created you, or in the knowledge of that which is just and true" (Mosiah 4:12).

Are We Good Church Members?

In this generation we face great challenges, both individually and as a Church. There are narrow places through which the Church must pass. There has never been a more crying need for loyalty. There has never been a more crying need for faith. The world is floundering in intemperance, immorality, perversion, drug addiction, cruelty, violence, and other forms of wickedness. Yet some among us would rather criticize the Lord and His church than concentrate on the problems. That is a symptom of impenitence.

As a "spiritual doctor" I counsel you to follow the Brethren. If you don't understand a problem or a position the Church has taken, restrain your tongue. Check the mote in your own eye before you criticize.

Follow the Brethren. Three words. There is nothing in your life that will destroy you if you will follow the Brethren. Even if it were all brought together and focused on you, there is not enough evil in the world to destroy you unless you consent to it.

All the water in the world

However hard it tried,

Can never sink the smallest ship

Unless it gets inside.

All the evil in the world,

The blackest kind of sin,

Can never hurt you the least bit

Unless you let it in.

A friend of mine once told me of an experience at a university when, as a young Ph.D., he was being interviewed for a position. The Dean asked if he was a Mormon, and he said he was. The next question was, "Are you a good member of your Church?" He said, "Well, I try to be," or something to that effect. The Dean got a bit irritated-he really wanted to know. "Are you or are you not a good member of your Church?" His reply was "Yes sir!" He told me that there were few things more important or precious to him than when he had to stand up and say, "Yes sir, I am a good member of the Church."

Each of us should ask himself or herself that question. Can you answer yes? Or do you stutter "Â� a good member in all but Â�" Or, "a good member in most things, but Â�" Are you?

Let us exercise the gifts of faith and repentance, and wash impenitence away. Let us face life clean, that the Spirit may rest upon us. Let us forgive and be forgiven. In this way we shall find peace.

Address given at Boston Stake fireside 5 May 1968.
