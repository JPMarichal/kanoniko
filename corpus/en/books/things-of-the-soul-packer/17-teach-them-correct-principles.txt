Boyd K. Packer, Things of the Soul ( 1996)

17

Teach Them Correct Principles [^1]

Early in 1990 an announcement went out that had a major effect on the budgeting of the Church. It related to the activities and operation expenses of the local units being paid for in the future from the tithes and offerings of the Church.

It is important that Church members understand the spirit and purpose of the change. We are following the admonition of the Prophet JosephSmith: "I teach them correct principles and they govern themselves" (Messages of the First Presidency, comp. James R. Clark, 6 vols. [Salt Lake City: Bookcraft, 1965-75], 3:54). We should not, according to the scriptures, need to be commanded in all things (see D&C 58:26).

Under this now-current arrangement in the United States and Canada, most of the deciding is left to the members of the Church, acting in harmony with the principles announced in the guidelines. The change has required some considerable adjustment in our thinking. It is not possible to do all of the things we had been doing in the same way we had been doing them. It has brought an inevitable reduction in programs. That was intended. There has to be some "pick and choose." Nothing essential has been lost; rather, essential things are being rediscovered, found.

The Effect Will Be Spiritual

We have been taught that tithing is not so much a matter of money as a matter of faith. While the change in budgets and assessments and fund-raising may have seemed at first to be a temporal matter, the effect of it will be spiritual.

Priesthood and auxiliary leaders have commented with keen insight that this change turns us more directly to spiritual matters. They are perceiving that, in effect, the announcement had more to do with spirit than with money. There is more dependency upon the Spirit and a greater need of personal revelation when the decisions are left to local leaders.

Some Few "Resourceful" Leaders

We have also heard of some very clever inventions calculated to circumvent the instructions and maintain some of the expensive, even extravagant, activities to which we had become accustomed. It is hoped that the resourceful souls concerned have found cause to repent once they have come to understand the spirit of the decision and the purpose for it all.

A Surprise

To many, the announcement came as a surprise, a very welcome surprise, and yet if you had been listening carefully you should not have been too surprised.

For years, Presidents of the Church have talked of and prayed for the day when tithes and offerings would be the only payments needed to qualify members for full participation in the Church.

President Joseph F. Smith, as early as 1907, stated: "We may not be able to reach it right away, but we expect to see the day when we will not have to ask you for one dollar of donation for any purpose, except that which you volunteer to give of your own accord, because we will have tithes sufficient in the storehouse of the Lord to pay everything that is needful for the advancement of the kingdom of God" (in Conference Report, April 1907, p. 7).

The First Presidency has counseled us on this again and again: "Dear Brethren and Sisters," they wrote, "we are seriously concerned over the demands made upon the people of the Church in carrying forward its many programs. We are most anxious that these requirements not become so heavy as to have an adverse effect on family life, vocational pursuits, or the pursuit of needed educational undertakings. We are also concerned about the financial requests made upon our peopleÂ� . We have reason to feel that these requirements are becoming unduly burdensome for many." This was printed in the Priesthood Bulletin in 1978, 1982, 1984, 1985, and 1987-five times!

The First Presidency sent yet another letter entitled "Reduction of Time and Money Required for Church Programs." It read in part: "We are very anxious that the cost of participation in Church activities not become unduly burdensome to our members. There is concern lest some who are not able to meet these costs may withdraw themselves from full participation in the Church. Particularly the youth programs of the Church should be so managed that all of our young people may enjoy full participation." (Letter to stake presidents, bishops, and branch presidents, 2 May 1978.)

Time Is an Issue

Notice that each of those statements called for a reduction in both time and money required of members.

"I have told you many times," President Brigham Young said, "the property which we inherit from our Heavenly Father is our time, and the power to choose in the disposition of the same. This is the real capital that is bequeathed unto us by our Heavenly Father." (In Journal of Discourses, 18:354; emphasis added.)

Some have asked why this change should come just when the forces of temptation are surrounding our youth as never before. They ask, "Do we not need more impressive activities and more meetings, rather than fewer?"

Sometimes more can be less, and sometimes less is more. Even with all we expended and all we did, we were not doing as well as we should have and there was little evidence that the expensive activities really secured our youth.

There is a lesson, a profound lesson, in the Book of Mormon. In Zenos's parable of the olive tree, the Lord of the vineyard wept because he had worked so hard yet the trees had brought forth wild fruit. "What could I have done more?" he asked. "Have I slackened my hand, that I have not nourished it, and digged about it and pruned it and stretched forth mine hand almost all the day long? What could I have done more for my vineyard?" (See Jacob 5:47.)

How many bishops with disappointing results have felt to say those very words in their souls? "What could I have done more for my ward? Why wild fruit after all our work?"

It was the servant-it always is the servant-who said: "Is it not the loftiness of thy vineyard-have not the branches thereof overcome the roots which are good? And because the branches have overcome the roots thereof, behold they grew faster than the strength of the roots, taking strength unto themselves." (Jacob 5:48.)

"Nevertheless," the Lord of the vineyard said, "I know that the roots are good" (Jacob 5:36; italics added). So he brought cuttings from the trees he had planted in poor ground, for he found them to be strong; and he grafted them in that "the root and the top may be equal in strength" (Jacob 5:66).

For the Church in our generation there is great meaning in this parable.

Meetings and activities can multiply until they take "strength unto themselves" at the expense of the gospel-of true worship.

This change in budgeting is having the effect of returning much of the responsibility for teaching and counseling and activity to the family, where it belongs. While there still are many cheaper activities, they are scaled down in cost of both time and money. There are fewer intrusions into family schedules and into the family purses.

Some Church activities are now replaced by family activities. Just as we have been taught as regards temporal affairs, independence, thrift, and self-reliance are to be re-enthroned as guiding principles in the homes of Latter-day Saints. And, just as stake leaders now sponsor fewer activities, leaving more of the time and money to ward leaders, ward leaders in turn leave more of both to the families.

This decision sets a better balance between families being assessed time and money to support Church activities and Church activities complementing what families should do for themselves. That is a difficult balance because some families need more support than others. Perhaps we previously were over-programming stable families in order to meet the needs of those with problems. We must seek a better way.

I can see parents grateful indeed to have a better family balance with activities on Saturdays. Saturday church activities can be scheduled to allow our youth to have their activities but on occasion to be home on some Saturdays to be taught how to work, to help, to learn to find recreation in the family setting. And the moms and dads who have obediently left home to oversee Church activities can find more time to spend with their own children.

Money Too Is an Issue

President Spencer W. Kimball said, "This is a shocking thing to me Â� to come to a realization of what we have been attempting to do, all with the best of intentions." He said the cost of membership in both time and money was "becoming prohibitive for the members of the Church, and they find it very difficult and sometimes we lose the members of the Church because they do not want to admit they cannot afford the things we expect." ("Reduction and Simplification," 13 April 1979.)

This change in budgeting could well have considerable reactivating influence on those who held back because they could not afford the cost of Church activities.

Consider parents struggling under the pressure of providing all that a growing family requires. Can we not see them being less firm in pressing their children to attend Church activities when they really can't afford the costs? When we press them too hard, and they don't respond, we may infer that they aren't good enough providers. If you understand the human ego at all, you will know that some parents will withdraw from activity rather than say they can't afford the cost.

Can you see a seventeen-year-old boy overhearing his mother on the phone saying: "Yes, yes, I know. We will try to make a partial payment next month. I know we did, but we just didn't have it. We've had some medical expenses. Yes, I understand that. We just have not been able to do it. Oh, please, don't do that. If you take it, it will be impossible for my husband to get to work. Please, can't you give us a little more time?"

Question: Will that boy sign up for an expensive youth camp or conference, no matter how desperately he wants to go? Will he attend regularly if every activity requires "just a few dollars?" The money he can supposedly earn to pay his own way may have more pressing uses.

You may say we can provide for him. Be careful about that human ego! Remember, we have already been teaching him and his parents to be independent, thrifty, and self-reliant.

Question: Will that boy go on a mission? I have known young men who have thought to disqualify themselves rather than to put what they feel is an impossible financial burden upon the family, with perhaps the mother leaving younger children at home so as to work and support him on his mission.

Now funds which previously would have been spent on these things may be salted away for missions. And this saving commitment can have a very protective moral and spiritual influence upon a young man that in some ways can be more powerful than one more exciting youth activity. It can indeed be a "saving" commitment.

The scriptures speak of tithes and of offerings; they do not speak of assessments or fundraising. To be an offering, it must be given freely-offered. The way is open now for many more of us to participate in this spiritually refining experience.

Some of us know families that are overburdened with missionary and other expenses. We can offer to help. The bishop can act as intermediary, and we can give the anonymous gift. What a privilege! It has to be done carefully, lest we undo the self-reliant part.

We must devise ways of letting those who have drawn back because of expense know about the change. They weren't in the meeting when the announcement was made. We should send our youth out to call them back. Let us tell them what the prophet said: "Come, my brethren, every one that thirsteth, come ye to the waters; and he that hath no money, come buy and eat; yea, come buy wine and milk without money and without price." (2 Nephi 9:50.)

For those who can and are willing, there comes the opportunity to make generous offerings. In leaving such decisions to the individual, the Church is allowing the fundamental doctrine of moral agency to assert itself. Do we see the change from assessment to offering as something of the testing that is fundamental to our mortal probation?

I have thought much of the other prophet Joseph, who interpreted the dream of the pharaoh. I have thought of the seven years of plenty and a time to prepare before the years of famine.

I have thought of a pharaoh humble enough to heed the counsel of a prophet, and of a people who were saved because of it. I have thought of a family that was united-the family of Israel.

I could not adequately express the depths of my feeling about that 1990 announcement. It was a course correction; it was an inspired move. It will have influence upon the Church across the world, not just in our generation but also in the generations to come. I have the certain conviction that it is pleasing to him who is our Lord and our Redeemer, even Jesus Christ, our Savior.

Address given at Member Finances Fireside broadcast 18 February 1990.
