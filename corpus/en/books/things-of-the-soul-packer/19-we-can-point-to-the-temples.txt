Boyd K. Packer, Things of the Soul ( 1996)

Part Four

Temple

19

We Can Point to the Temples [^1]

I have a deep regard for the work that is accomplished in our temples. Temples are the single most tangible, impressive evidence of our knowledge and our faith in the resurrection, and of our certain knowledge that there is life beyond death.

Sometime in the 1970s, shortly before the passing of his wife, Ida, I took President Marion G. Romney home from the office early one evening. We were driving along saying very little. I asked how his wife was doing, and he expressed his concern over her health. After a period of silence he said, "Boyd, when I think that in a short time, perhaps twenty-five years at the outside, Ida and I will both be beyond the veil, and that we will be free from our human frailties, and that we will be together, I am filled with such joy that I can hardly contain it." Somewhere in that expression is the essence of what temples mean.

The doctrines relating to genealogy and the ordinances performed in the house of the Lord are fundamental. These doctrines more than any others set us apart from all the other Christian churches and verify that this is, indeed, "the only true and living church upon the face of the whole earth" (D&C 1:30). Without these doctrines and without the authority to perform these ordinances, we should be left puzzled and troubled over how to answer a most fundamental question.

No Other Name Offers Salvation

The book of Acts records an incident in which Peter and John were going into the temple. At the temple gate was one who was borne there every day, who had been lame from birth. There at the temple gate he begged, perhaps supposing that those who came there would be inclined to be more generous than others. He reached out his hand and asked alms of the two Apostles. Peter, looking intently on him, said, "Look on us." And then he said: "Silver and gold have I none; but such as I have give I thee: In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth rise up and walk." And the New Testament records that he "leaping up stood, and walked," and went with them into the temple. (See Acts 3:1-8.)

Shortly thereafter Peter and John were arrested and brought before the Sanhedrin to account for what had taken place at the temple. They were asked by what power or name they had healed the man. And Peter said: "Be it known unto you all, and to all the people of Israel, that by the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth whom ye crucified, whom God raised from the dead, even by him doth this man stand here before you wholeÂ� . Neither is there salvation in any other: for there is none other name under heaven given among men, whereby we must be saved." (Acts 4:10, 12.)

Problem: If there is none other name given under heaven whereby men can be saved, what of those who never hear that name?

Redemptive Power of Temple Work

In 1981, Sister Packer and I spent three weeks in the People's Republic of China. A billion people live there-one-fourth of the earth's population. Almost none of them have ever heard the name of Jesus Christ, much less come within reach of the meaning of it.

And then there is baptism. The Lord said very directly to His Apostles: "Go ye into all the world, and preach the gospel to every creature. He that believeth and is baptized shall be saved; but he that believeth not shall be damned." (Mark 16:15-16.) Baptism-an absolute essential. The name of Christ-an absolute essential. What of those who never come within reach in mortal life? And as we measure ourselves against the vast populations that have lived on this earth, that is most of them.

The Prophet Joseph Smith made this very direct statement: "One dies and is buried, having never heard the Gospel of reconciliation; to the other the message of salvation is sent, he hears and embraces it, and is made the heir of eternal life. Shall the one become the partaker of glory and the other be consigned to hopeless perdition? Is there no chance for his escape? Sectarianism answers 'none.' Such an idea is worse than atheism." (Teachings, p. 192.) The work that goes on within the temples is a great redemptive, protective work of the Church. We are familiar with it in the sense of its being part of the three-fold mission of the Church: the preaching of the gospel (missionary work); the perfecting of the Saints (that work that goes on within the wards and the branches of the Church); and the redemption of our dead.

We have at home a little three-legged stool. One of the legs is set up so it can be removed. On occasions, to demonstrate some principle I will take that one leg from the stool and ask someone to cause the stool to stand. Most of the legs are there, but with one missing the stool cannot be made to stand on its own.

It is not safe for us collectively, nor is it safe for us individually, to neglect in our lives this sacred, redemptive work which constitutes one-third of the responsibility that rests upon this Church.

Temple Covenants

As a young married man, I had been ordained a high priest and called to serve on a high council. On one occasion in our ward high priests group we had a project of raising corn. One day I was in company with some very old men as we picked some sweet corn. We were shelling it so as to sell it for our building project. As we sat there shelling corn and talking about spiritual things, one venerable old brother told me something that I shall never forget. It had a profound effect upon me.

As a young man he lived in a little community, a country ward. There lived in that ward a very close relative of the President of the Church. This relative passed away, and the man telling me the story went to the funeral. Although they were some considerable distance from Salt Lake City, just before the service began the President of the Church walked in. They had not expected him, even though he was a close relative, for this relative of his had not been valiant in the Church. The President had come by train and a further distance by wagon.

In the service the eulogies were spoken. The goodness of the deceased man was extolled. People told of the sacks of flour he had taken to the widows at Christmas time and his generosity in doing this and that. A picture emerged of a very fine, ethical man. And then the President of the Church rose to speak. Those present would not forget his sermon. It was not comforting; perhaps it was a sermon that only the President of the Church could give. At the pulpit he made comment about the compliments paid to his relative, and said, "That is all well and good"; and he acknowledged the good things that the man had done. "But nevertheless," he said, "the fact is that he did not keep his covenants." There were some things this man had done that he ought not to have done and some things he ought to have done that he had not done that now stood in the way of his eternal progression. There would have to be provision for repentance before he could progress.

That story was very sobering to me and from it I gained a new appreciation for covenants and ordinances and for our responsibility for the things that transpire in the temples.

We Can Point to the Temples

On another occasion, speaking just from notes, I used a scripture from memory and misquoted it. The scripture was, as I paraphrased it, "Where much is given, much is expected." A few days later I received a letter from a woman who corrected it, pointing out that in both the New Testament and the Doctrine and Covenants the scripture reads, "Of him unto whom much is given, much is required." I have thought of the people in the far distant parts of the earth who are not within reach of the temples. I am so grateful for the number of temples the Church has built and will continue to build over the years. They are scattered about the earth in order that the Latter-day Saints can receive their covenants and partake of the ordinances therein performed.

So when the question comes, "What of those countless billions who have lived and died without either coming within reach of baptism or coming under the authority and saving influence of the name of Christ?" we can point to the temples.

As many of the early Saints who could do so received the temple ordinances in the Nauvoo Temple. After that temple was destroyed, the Brethren carried those ordinances in their minds for over thirty years. Finally in Utah, when the St. George Temple was finished and there was at last a repository for the written ordinances, President Brigham Young in one of his last endeavors assigned Elder Wilford Woodruff of the Quorum of the Twelve, who was appointed president of the temple, to write the ordinances. Assisted by one or two others, he did so. In 1877, the year that Brigham Young died, he went over the ordinances carefully with the writers, for there was now a place where they could be written and a place where they could be housed-a temple.

I have a deep and a profound regard for genealogical work, which is the preparatory labor for our temple work. These two combine to put families together.

I am indebted to William C. Mickleson for the following poem.

Temples

The temple marks our mortal way

To enter God's eternal day.

Here Saints may covenant to live

The fulness of his word and give

Complete commitment to abide

With God as everlasting guide.

Within the temple's holy place

The Lord may come in peace and grace

To cleanse his people, ease their cares,

Receive their tokens and their prayers,

And lift his Saints through knowledge of

Eternal law to perfect love.

In holy temples through all lands

The Saints who keep the Lord's commands

May bless their kin till, one by one,

The sacred sealings will be done

To link the living and the dead

Through Jesus Christ, our Lord, our Head.

The work that goes on within our temples is a sacred work. The genealogical work which precedes it is an inspired work. By receiving the ordinances and making our covenants in the temple, by honoring those covenants, and wherever possible by regular temple attendance in service to those who have departed this life without having received these blessings, we can draw ever closer to our Father.

Address given at Jordan River Temple dedication 17 November 1981.
