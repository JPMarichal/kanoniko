Instructions to the Twelve Apostles-Missionary work, November 12, 1885

1885-November 12

ORIGINAL LETTER on file, Church Historian's Office, Salt Lake City.

This letter of the First Presidency to Wilford Woodruff as President of the Council of the Twelve and to the Council outlines their duties and responsibilities in the selection of missionaries to be called by the President of the Church. Neither the Presiding Council of Seventies nor the Young Men's Mutual Improvement Association should relieve the Council of the Twelve of this responsibility of selecting and interviewing missionaries. The letter also contains an interesting instruction on the establishment of Ward cooperative farms for the support of missionaries and their families. The reference to "Elder's who may find it necessary to go away" alludes to married men who found it necessary to leave Utah because of Federal persecution for polygamy. Several of the General Authorities of the Church were at the time on such missions because of this situation.

Salt Lake City November 12th, 1885

President Wilford Woodruff, and Council of the Twelve Apostles.

Dear Brethren:

Letters which we receive in response from those who are called to go on missions prompt us to communicate with you upon this subject. We are sending out a large number of missionaries in various directions, and it is important that in making selections, we should get the best material available for the purpose. It is not unreasonable to suppose that many very worthy persons who would make excellent missionaries are overlooked, while many who are not so suitable are selected. It appears to us that this business of selecting missionaries can be more systematized and be attended with more satisfactory results than it is at the present time. The calls for missionaries should be considered by the Twelve Apostles. They should understand the fields to be supplied and the kind of men that are needed to supply them. At the present time they travel considerably among the various stakes, and have opportunities of becoming acquainted with those who are most suitable in these stakes for missionaries. By keeping in mind or noting down in their memorandum books the wants of the various fields, they can learn by conversation with the Presidency of Stakes and with Bishops, who there are that might answer to go as missionaries, to the various places requiring their presence. They should also as far as practicable see and converse with these individuals themselves, and take down the names of those who should strike them as being suitable with their qualifications and their circumstances. Then when you get together in council you can from these names select missionaries for the various fields to supply the demand. By taking this course we think the best men in the community, who are in positions to go on missions, can be selected and greater efficiency be imparted to our missionary service. There are many considerations which should enter into the selection of missionaries; these the Twelve are familiar with. Sickly men or men who are crippled or deficient in physical stamina, are not the kind of men to send away from home. The Seventies, especially, should not permit themselves to become so embarrassed by debt that they can not go when called, but should so shape their affairs that they could go at any time without difficulty. Where men are loaded down with debt, or are in some other peculiar circumstances, which prevent them from going at once, they should have time given them to arrange their affairs, so that they will not have to make too great sacrifices. Indeed all missionaries who are called should have a reasonable time allowed them to make proper arrangements for the care of their families and of their business while they are absent.

If the Twelve adopt this course, they will be in a position to suggest names of suitable persons whenever a call for missionaries is made. At the same time the operations of the Presiding Council of Seventies in securing names need not be suspended, but the Council of the Apostles should exercise a supervisory care over these nominations, and learn all that is possible respecting the men suggested, so that unworthy representatives of our cause shall not go out. We have been impressed with the need of greater care in this direction.

You should make your arrangements that if a sudden call should come for a missionary or missionaries that whoever may be in the city, (Bro. F. D. Richards and others can make the desired selection and supply the call.) Elders who may find it necessary to go away, can also report to them and if suitable can be appointed.

We may say while upon this subject that though we value the young men's Mutual Improvement Associations and think the organizations capable of performing great good, we do not think it proper to apply to their Presidents to select names for missions. This belongs properly to the Priesthood and its various quorums and should be attended to by those who preside over them. In connection with this subject the circumstances of the men who are called ought to be taken into consideration. Men ought not to be obliged to break up their affairs to go upon missions, and leave themselves without either house or home while there are plenty of others who could go without being embarrassed.

Your consideration is requested also to a subject which has been mentioned for several years past the providing for the families of men who may be sent on missions. In Bannock, Oneida, Cache, and some few other stakes some of the wards have purchased and set apart lands of from 40 to 160 acres to be cultivated for the purpose of sustaining missionaries' families who may need assistance. The design is to have this cultivation done by the Seventies and other Elders who, for various reasons, do not go on missions abroad. In this way the missionaries during their absence, can be freed in a great measure from the care and sustenance of their families. This principle if carried out throughout all the stakes and wards, would greatly relieve many of the missionaries and their families during their absence. It is designed that this plan shall be thoroughly carried out in all the stakes of Zion. The Presidents of Stakes have been spoken to upon this subject, and if the Twelve in their labors in the stakes can urge the completing of this arrangement it would greatly relieve many missionaries from embarrassments under which they now labor. Let this plan be properly carried out, and many who are now qualified to go on missions, but who cannot leave their families on account of their want of means to sustain them during their absence, will be able to go and their families can be made tolerably comfortable. We think it a duty that we owe to the Seventies, Elders and others who are engaged in missionary labors and ought not to be put off any longer. It is several years since this was first instituted, but we are afraid that in many parts it has been very little attended to, and it is a measure and a duty that should not be overlooked, for anything that can be done towards facilitating this labor is a duty devolving upon us to accomplish.

With love, we remain your Brethren in the new and Everlasting covenant, JOHN TAYLOR, GEORGE Q. CANNON,

Copies of this letter were sent to Moses Thatcher at Logan and to Erastus Snow on his way to Mexico.-F. D. R.

November 17, 1885

1886, March-MS 48:305-314, 321, 329 (May 17, May 24, 1886); also in pamphlet form, B. Y. U. Library Special Collections.

This Epistle of the First Presidency was prepared in March for delivery, in absentia, at the April General Conference of the Church. If the enemies of the Saints were getting bolder, so the First Presidency was becoming bolder and more frank in their public denunciation of the enemies of the Church. The charge or assertion is made in this Epistle that the persecution of the Church and its members over polygamy was:

"... the results of a deep-laid and carefully planned conspiracy, which has been in process of formation for years... . Religious and irreligious, ministers in sacerdotal robes and atheistic scoffers, business men of integrity and blacklegs, temperance men and drunkards, men of strict morality and pimps and harlots, are crowded together on the platform they have constructed, and they find no inconvenience from each other's companionship."

The awareness of the First Presidency over the years of the designs of their enemies towards them is well reflected in the diaries of L. John Nuttall, their private secretary and intimate companion during these "underground" days. It is also reflected in the letters that Nuttall wrote to his friends and to members of his family. Perhaps a brief quotation or two here will serve to illustrate this point. I cannot emphasize too strongly that the serious student of this era should be thoroughly familiar in detail with the Nuttall collection.

On May 21, 1885 Nuttall wrote his son Leonard, who was an L.D.S. missionary in England, from the "Safe Retreat" as follows:

"This war is waged on our religion, and not in the cause of morality, for this has been so stated by the prosecuting officers and sustained by Judge Zane... . A great desire has also been made manifest to get our leading brethren into their power. The Spirit has plainly manifested that they should keep out of their power at least for the present."

On October 6, 1885, Nuttall again wrote his son Leonard in the following vein:

"... I am still with President T——-r & C—-n, and notwithstanding the efforts of our enemies, the Lord has been kind and merciful and we have been signally preserved from their power to perform our daily labors in the Church and Kingdom of God... .

Our enemies have not slackened in their efforts to bring us into their power and to destroy the work to God. Every device that could be thought of to ensnare the people and to make them subject to their will has been resorted to. The submission of a few to the demands of the Court, the enemies hoped would have the effect to cause all who are accused to hasten to court and make similar acknowledgments and give similar promises. When Bishop Sharp was induced to do as he did, the general expectation was, on the part of our enemies, that the whole people would hasten to follow his example and the First Presidency and other authorities would be left without a following.
