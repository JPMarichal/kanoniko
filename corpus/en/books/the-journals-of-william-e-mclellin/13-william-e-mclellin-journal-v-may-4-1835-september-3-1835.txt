The succinct description of the purpose of this mission that McLellin gives here reflects both the Prophetâs instruction to the Twelve and the overall makeup of Mormonism in this, the fifth year of the organization of the Church. By this time, the âtent of Zionâ had organized stakes in Kirtland and Missouri, where the Mormons were creating gathering centers as they responded to the call to assemble âin unto one place upon the face of this landâ (D&C 29:7â8). In addition, small congregations of persons who had accepted the LDS message and had been baptized by missionaries like McLellin were spreading across the countryside. Led by local converts, these branches, as they were usually called, kept in touch with the center in two ways: they subscribed to the Messenger and Advocate, which was published by the Church in Kirtland; and they were visited periodically by elders sent from the gathering centers on preaching missions or assignments. President Smithâs instructions to the Twelve regularized the overall structure of the Church by specifying that the Twelve âhave no right to go into Zion [Missouri], or any of its stakes, and there undertake to regulate the affairs thereof; . . . but it is their duty to go abroad and regulate all matters relative to the different branches of the Church.â HC 2:220.

At the same time, the Twelve were to inquire into the teaching, conduct, and faithfulness of all traveling elders (missionaries) who had recently labored within the boundaries of the various conferences. The former established a form of oversight over the scattered branches; the latter, a crucially important type of inquiry, allowed the Twelve, as representatives of the church President and familiar with recent revelations and other organizational developments, to determine what was being taught under the aegis of the Church.
