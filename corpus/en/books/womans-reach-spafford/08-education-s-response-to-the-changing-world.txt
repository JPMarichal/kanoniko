Belle S. Spafford, Woman's Reach ( 1974)

Chapter 7

Education's Response to the Changing World

Some time ago I read the following item in a newspaper that seemed to be an apt comment on today's time:

It is a gloomy moment in history. Not in the lifetime of any man who reads this paper has there been so much grave and deep apprehension; never has the future seemed so dark and incalculable.

In France, the political cauldron seethes and bubbles with uncertainty.

England and the English Empire is being sorely tried and exhausted in a social and economic struggle.

The United States is beset with racial, industrial, and commercial chaos, drifting we know not where.

Russia hangs like a storm cloud on the horizon of Europe, dark and silent. It is a solemn moment, and no man can feel indifferent, which happily no man pretends to feel on the issue of events.

Of our own troubles, no man can see the end.

You may be sure it was a surprise to me to read at the end of this item the following statement: "This quote appeared in the Oct. 10, 1847, issue of Harpers Magazine." (You may recall this was just a year after the beginning exodus of the Saints from Nauvoo.) So we must recognize that some of our present concerns were major concerns more than a century ago, and all of our problems are not due to changes of recent years.

We need further to remind ourselves that change is not always bad, that today we have countless creature comforts and endless opportunities not enjoyed by people in 1847. Personally, I have no great longing to go back to the days of the coal stove and candlelight, to the covered wagon and non-hard-surfaced roads, to surgery without benefit of a skilled anesthesiologist. I would rather have the benefits and advantages of the present and put up with the problems of our day. I believe it is well to remind ourselves occasionally of the blessings of change and to give serious consideration to our individual responsibility toward shaping change to man's good, rather than to continue to belabor how extensive and devastating is change.

I was brought up short one day by my young granddaughter, age five, who said, "Grandma, tell me about the olden days when you were a little girl."

"What would you like to know about them?" I asked.

"Tell me about the cartoons they had on television," she replied.

"Oh," said I, "we didn't have television when I was your age."

"No television!" she exclaimed. "What did you do for something to sit and watch when your mother was busy or not home?"

Upon another occasion, the child rushed in and excitedly exclaimed, "Guess what I found out! Guess what I found out! Potato chips are made from potatoes! Not the kind in the package like we use, but the old-fashioned kind like you use, Grandma, the kind that grows in the ground."

Is it any wonder there is a so-called generation gap, that there is much confused thinking and many conflicting viewpoints today? Is it any wonder that there are differing measuring rods for good and bad, that there are many problems that must be worked through? Where, then, may we properly turn for help?

Is the world justified in looking toward education to be an effective medium through which people may better adjust to change—a means whereby human relationships may be improved and man helped to live a happier, more productive and more rewarding life? Should we be able to rely on education as a compass leading the world toward more peace and less conflict? It is my opinion that we are justified in expecting this, if we take the broad view of education.

Education may be defined, I believe, as the discipline of mind and character that comes through study, instruction, and investigation. It is learning that comes through teaching and training in the schools. It is the acquisition of knowledge through experience, observation, conversation, doing, and testing. It is scholarship that comes through faith and the light of the Holy Ghost.

Its goals are intellectual growth and character development, the discovery of truth, vocational and/or professional proficiency, economic security, social acceptance, competent and satisfying living, eternal well-being.

Education and training are among the very important things of life. They help the individual realize his full potential as an individual. They help him to meet the competition of his day and adequately accommodate himself to life. They open doors of opportunity to a life rich in cultural values and highly compensatory service.

The Lord has placed divine approval on learning. The leaders of our church from the Prophet Joseph Smith down to present-day leaders have shown great faith in education, accepting its divine approval and extending its influence into eternity. The Doctrine and Covenants confirms this truth:

Whatever principle of intelligence we attain unto in this life, it will rise with us in the resurrection. And if a person gains more knowledge and intelligence in this life through his diligence and obedience than another, he will have so much the advantage in the world to come. (D&C 130:18-19.)

From the very beginning of the restoration of the Church, education and salvation have gone hand in hand in the gospel plan of exaltation. The Prophet set a good example for his people. He not only taught from the pulpit the importance of education, but he also organized and attended schools. Josiah Quincy, in his account of his visit to Nauvoo, said the Prophet read from the Hebrew New Testament in order to make clear a theological point. How did he learn Hebrew? He hired a teacher and sat in a beginners' class, where he applied himself to study, just as we do when we want to master a difficult subject. There was always a close association between education and the most sacred activities in the life of the Prophet.

Many times we quote in support of education, often unaware that it is mentioned also in the dedicatory prayer given by revelation and uttered by the Prophet at the completion of the Kirtland Temple: "And do thou grant, Holy Father, that all those who shall worship in this house may be taught words of wisdom out of the best books, and that they may seek learning even by study, and also by faith. . . . " (D&C 109:14.)

True education to the Latter-day Saint is education that builds faith and promotes righteousness. Education that fails to do this cannot be considered as ought but inferior education.

The Book of Mormon makes this clear. The ancient prophet Jacob said: ". . . to be learned is good if they hearken unto the counsels of God." (2 Nephi 9:29.) The same prophet gave us an example of bad education when he said:

O that cunning plan of the evil one! O the vainness, and the frailties, and the foolishness of men! When they are learned they think they are wise, and they hearken not unto the counsel of God, for they set it aside, supposing they know of themselves, wherefore, their wisdom is foolishness and it profiteth them not. And they shall perish. (2 Nephi 9:28.)

Inasmuch as we have two kinds of education—bad and good, the bad being that which fails to build faith or that destroys it, and the good being the kind that builds faith—it is for us to select the kind of learning that will be beneficial to us. The Holy Ghost has been given us to guide us unto all truth.

Only as the Church concept of education is impregnated in the teachings of our colleges and universities will they fully meet the challenge placed upon them by a troubled world to help students properly cope with life, regardless of what life may bring.

Many major universities are recognizing their responsibility to meet the challenges of change. They sense a need to modify their curricula, their teaching procedures, their administrative policies, their teaching facilities, and so on, to meet the demands made upon men and women of this twentieth century society. There is a growing consciousness that education in the colleges and universities today should no longer be fashioned according to outmoded stereotypes. There is an increasing awareness that the university must bring its resources to bear on current problems and current human needs in a more effective manner.

I would like to present a few opinions with regard to modifications that have been suggested by educators and other concerned leaders, as well as a few opinions taken from studies that have been made. Some of these proposals, I am confident, have already been adopted by some institutions of higher learning. None of the opinions are my personal opinions, nor do I feel qualified to endorse or reject any of them. I list them as a matter of interest.

Whether or not these opinions are sound and workable, they do indicate that consideration is being given to the role of education in helping people to meet change, and the programs and procedures of our colleges are receiving critical analysis. Universities appear to be seeking to know how they can best provide an atmosphere conducive to human growth and well-being. One educator has said, "When we become concerned enough to build an institution or remodel existing ones so that greater value derives from learning than from holding a degree, then we will have reached the high road."

Just what education's response within the schools will be to our changing times, time alone will tell.

What education's response within the Church will be to our changing times is certain. It will be the same as it has always been—the teaching of eternal truths as revealed by the Lord to his prophets. It will be the search for words of wisdom out of the best books. It will be the seeking of learning even by study and also by faith.
