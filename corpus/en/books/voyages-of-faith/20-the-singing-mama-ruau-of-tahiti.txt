French Polynesian Musical Tradition

Long before Europeans arrived in French Polynesia, musical folklore had always been an integral part of culture of the people from the widespread area of the Society Islands, the Tuamotu-Gambier Archipelago, the Austral Islands, and the Marquesas. Edmond de Bovis said of these pre-European people:

They had poetry and a sort of literature. They composed the most perplexed war chants and songs of pleasure. They also had a sort of rhythmical prose that they would repeat, resting on certain syllables which were also marked with a beat of hands, with a trampling of their feet, or with movements of their bodies. . . . There were even legends and genealogies among which were included pieces of religious history and mythology.2

With the evangelical changes brought first by the London Missionary Society and later by the Catholic mission, the native forms of chanting and singing were adapted and incorporated into religious teaching sessions. Even in the late twentieth century, local Protestant worshipers gathered on Sunday evenings for the tuaroâiâa lengthy meeting in which the congregation will âcrack open and dissect a Biblical verse that the minister has chosen. . . . As it would be too tedious to listen to commentaries and nothing else, the organizer wisely intersperses three explanations with a âhimene [hymn] tarava,â which is made up solely of biblical words and chanted like litanies.â3

Brother Pu Tihoni, now a LÄâie resident, says he remembers his Protestant mother going to those âsinging meetingsâ when he was a small boy. The LDS Church in the Tuamotu Archipelago

With the many changes in lifestyle brought on by foreign influences (the Mormons included), Tahitians were often caught in an awkward transition from island culture to what was considered proper Christian worship. Clergymen, even some early LDS leaders, forbade many traditional practices, especially dancing, when they felt there was anything immoral about it. Another damper on traditional Tahitian culture occurred around 1901 when the government drew up local regulations requiring institutions to hold their classes in French.4

The Mormons, however, flourished in the Tuamotusâa scattered chain of eighty coral atolls extending more than six hundred miles northwest to southeast of Tahitiâlargely because they never even attempted to digress from the native tongue to which they owed their missionary success. They and the French Protestants agreed on this one early view:

To remain in contact with this people, to prepare the youth to play a social and religious role, to preserve above all its originality and personality, it was necessary to make broad and justifiable concessions to the Tahitian language. Mission work in a native country which did not understand that fact would inevitably fail of its own accord, at the same time as it would add to the destruction of the spirit of those whom it claimed to enlighten.5

It was this kind of attitude that kept the LDS Church growing, even under the most adverse conditions. In the Tuamotus the branch members retained the old chants and the more conservative dancing styles. Besides the himene tarava mentioned earlier, other types of music and dance included: fagu (sacred chants), laments, incantations, prayers, types of haka (rhythmic, foot-stamping chants), putu (hand-clapping chants), teki and mereu (love songs), work songs (also used as welcoming chants), faâateniteni (historical chants), tuatau (used to restore courage and energy), and aparima (graceful hulalike dances).6

Along with accepting this traditional music, the mission established the first brass band in the Tuamotus on the island of Takaroa in 1920. Elder LeRoy Mallory, a talented missionary whose family generously donated the gleaming instruments, directed the Takaroa band. It was made up of approximately twenty-five young island men who eventually mastered their new toys. The group was called the Pupu Pu Momoni (fig. 2). Elder Mallory also organized the women and youth into choirs. He is spoken of as the person who raised the peopleâs musical consciousness by introducing true choir concepts and, in some instances, drilling the singersâ English diction so that an unsuspecting listener might think it was an American choir. So it was that the Tuamotu Saints were blessed with such diverse experiences in music.

The Origin of the Mama Ruau

Working in Papeete during 1956âa crucial point in MormonâFrench Polynesian relationsâwas an enthusiastic young elder named Thomas R. Stone. The Continued Performances of the Mama Ruau

The Mama Ruau were asked to perform himene tarava at conferences, building dedications, and sacrament services. They perfected little dance routines and even composed new chants that extolled gospel teachings and missionary work. The Mama Ruau took their talents to Orofara, a leprosy colony some twenty to thirty miles away from town, where they entertained the afflicted people. As a service to Tahitiâs tourism department, they, along with other Church members, continued to greet the Mariposa and the Monterey. Then, as now, they delighted their audiences with a spontaneous charm. At Christmastime they visited the hospitals, where they left the warmth of their performances as well as presents for the patients. With the choir, the Mama Ruau sailed to outer island conferences on the Paraita, an eighty-two foot schooner owned by the mission and used for interisland travel by the members. The group also performed at a grand ball for the French Marines.

On a private level, individual members of the group were sometimes contacted by hotels such as the Bel Air and Travel Lodge to present Mama Ruau-style programs. Service fees given to these performers helped pay for their instruments and uniforms. Although the Church itself never sponsored a group, the Mormon Mama Ruau practiced on their own and entered the singing competitions in the July Bastille Day festivities. Of these competitions, a missionary in Papeete in the 1960s said: âThe Mama Ruaus also performed during the fete and their dances and singing were unusual and fresh. Conclusion

I asked Poura Mariteragi, originally from the main island of Tahiti, how she felt about being one of the first Mama Ruau as we know them today. In translation she said, âI was happy to learn the songs. On Rairoa our Mama Ruau were a new thing to them. The other Saints would host us when we traveled to the outer islands. . . . The philosophy of the Church leaders in Tahiti during the twentieth century was summed up in this 1968 message from Elder Scott Anderson, an assistant to the mission president at the time:

Let us build a strong mission with strong branches that can be made into stakes and wards. The first work is to strengthen the members. . . . They will, as missionaries, bring new converts into the church. Enthused members are the keys to success here in French Polynesia. Tahitians, like everyone, want to go where the action is. If the action is taking place at the bars, or at the movie houses, that is where they will be. Letâs have the action chez nous at the Mormons. The Church should be the social center for the members, and for the community. If the members are alive, if the Church is working, converts will come.14

One can see that this story of the singing Mama Ruau is simply a focused example of love and sensitivity toward a specific population of the Saints in a certain culture. Not only did the increased involvement of the Mama Ruau strengthen the Church, it also gave life and popularity to songs and dances of French Polynesia. The Mama Ruau since then have helped perpetuate Pacific tradition. Therefore, it is the hope of this author that Latter-day Saints in French Polynesia will remember the close historical relationship between the Mama Ruau and the Church in their country so that the spiritual and cultural knowledge of our Mama Ruau will not slip from the grasp of future generations.

Afterword (September 2000)

It is a relief to see that Mama Ruau groups have survived in French Polynesia. Within the Church itself, my sources say the womenâs involvement has been somewhat diversified and modernized, with less emphasis on the full performance schedules and high visibility that caught peopleâs attention years ago. Mormon Mama Ruau, however, continue to enliven special Church functions, especially at the temple. Ironically, it is the Protestant ladies who regularly take the public center stage and are very strong in promoting this cultural aspect of island music and dance. Nevertheless, it must be appreciated that our original Mama Ruau played a crucial, catalytic role in preserving and even enhancing the traditional musical style, eventually stimulating others to do the same. In reflecting upon their contributions of the past and present, we will always cherish the Mama Ruau for their joi de vivre and their exuberant testimony in song.

On a private level, individual members of the group were sometimes contacted by hotels such as the Bel Air and Travel Lodge to present Mama Ruau-style programs. Service fees given to these performers helped pay for their instruments and uniforms. Although the Church itself never sponsored a group, the Mormon Mama Ruau practiced on their own and entered the singing competitions in the July Bastille Day festivities. Of these competitions, a missionary in Papeete in the 1960s said: âThe Mama Ruaus also performed during the fete and their dances and singing were unusual and fresh. As usual they performed well and gave a good name to the Church and earned 20.000 CPF [approximately $200 U.S.] prize money.â10

The Influence of the Mama Ruau

The Papeete Mama Ruau motivated other districts to start their own groups. It was a blessing to the activation program, as indicated by one missionary in Paea: âOur Mama Ruau group here in Paea has really improved. Hiro Mariteragi told the group they needed about 20 women. So the members went out and found 10 more women for the group, most of them inactive members.â11 Another missionary on Raiatea cited an example of the Mama Ruauâs contribution to a fund-raising soiree: âThis Saturday we saw a soiree at Avera. It was one of their better efforts, including some excellent Mama Ruau numbers. . . . The pieces were a great financial success for the Avera Branch.â12

In 1963 when the first Tahitian excursion to the New Zealand Temple was organized, the Mama Ruau within that group performed for the Saints in New Zealand. It was a very touching experience for the Mama Ruau. By the late 1960s, Mormon Mama Ruau from Tahiti had also cut a record album and performed with touring groups at Disneyland in California and the Polynesian Cultural Center in Hawaiâi. In January 1966, they were an important part of the program in the missionâs most successful âSoiree Musicale Annuelle,â where the guests of honor were the governor of Tahiti and his wifeâGovernor and Madame Jean Sicurani (fig. 4). Mission President Thomas R. Conclusion

I asked Poura Mariteragi, originally from the main island of Tahiti, how she felt about being one of the first Mama Ruau as we know them today. In translation she said, âI was happy to learn the songs. On Rairoa our Mama Ruau were a new thing to them. The other Saints would host us when we traveled to the outer islands. . . . Before, most of the activities were for the youth and younger adults, although the Relief Society had quilting and handcrafts.â Tetua Tihoni expressed the same happiness in being able to actively participate in the missionâs growth. She said, âThe old folks are happy; they can contribute. People would call the Mission if they wanted us; the Mission would call the group leaders. Some of us had never been to the outer islands. Whenever we traveled with the Mama Ruau, it was exciting. And we didnât even have to pay.â

The philosophy of the Church leaders in Tahiti during the twentieth century was summed up in this 1968 message from Elder Scott Anderson, an assistant to the mission president at the time:

Let us build a strong mission with strong branches that can be made into stakes and wards. The first work is to strengthen the members. . . . They will, as missionaries, bring new converts into the church. Enthused members are the keys to success here in French Polynesia. Tahitians, like everyone, want to go where the action is. If the action is taking place at the bars, or at the movie houses, that is where they will be. Letâs have the action chez nous at the Mormons. The Church should be the social center for the members, and for the community. If the members are alive, if the Church is working, converts will come.14

One can see that this story of the singing Mama Ruau is simply a focused example of love and sensitivity toward a specific population of the Saints in a certain culture. Not only did the increased involvement of the Mama Ruau strengthen the Church, it also gave life and popularity to songs and dances of French Polynesia. The Mama Ruau since then have helped perpetuate Pacific tradition. Therefore, it is the hope of this author that Latter-day Saints in French Polynesia will remember the close historical relationship between the Mama Ruau and the Church in their country so that the spiritual and cultural knowledge of our Mama Ruau will not slip from the grasp of future generations.

Afterword (September 2000)

It is a relief to see that Mama Ruau groups have survived in French Polynesia. Within the Church itself, my sources say the womenâs involvement has been somewhat diversified and modernized, with less emphasis on the full performance schedules and high visibility that caught peopleâs attention years ago. Mormon Mama Ruau, however, continue to enliven special Church functions, especially at the temple. Ironically, it is the Protestant ladies who regularly take the public center stage and are very strong in promoting this cultural aspect of island music and dance. Nevertheless, it must be appreciated that our original Mama Ruau played a crucial, catalytic role in preserving and even enhancing the traditional musical style, eventually stimulating others to do the same. In reflecting upon their contributions of the past and present, we will always cherish the Mama Ruau for their joi de vivre and their exuberant testimony in song.

At the 1983 MPHS annual conference, Marvelee Soon Tahauri, a ward Relief Society president and high school English teacher, discussed the Mormon origins of the singing Mama Ruau (grandmothers) of French Polynesia in the 1950s. At first this Mormon innovation was criticized and resisted by other religions. In time, however, erstwhile opponents embraced and imitated the idea, and by the 1960s, Mama Ruau groups had become a prominent feature of the Tahitian cultural landscape. As Tahauri tells the story, it is clear that the organization of the Mama Ruau exhibited such hallmarks of the Latter-day Saint spirit as the imaginative, extra-mile fulfillment of oneâs church assignment; respect for, and a desire to preserve wholesome islander culture; and a love of music and the performing arts. In presenting to the MPHS this little-known chapter of Pacific Mormon history, Tahauri broke new ground, and, as will be clear to the reader, she did it with a warm and engaging style.
