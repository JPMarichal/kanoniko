At the beginning of the nineteenth century, the Hawaiian Islands were populated almost entirely by native Hawaiians. A century later, the Hawaiians were a minority in their own land. Foreign diseases and other hardships exacted their toll, but almost as significant in effecting the changing demographics was the extensive immigration of Chinese and Japanese laborers in the second half of the 1800s. Tens of thousands of Asians came to Hawaiâi, and by the twentieth century they outnumbered the Hawaiians. In the early 1980s, two BYUâHawaiâi librarians, Russell T. Clement (now head of the Northwestern University Library art collection) and Sheng-Luen Tsai (now deceased) produced the first comprehensive overview of the Churchâs outreach to Hawaiâiâs Asian population. This pioneering work was presented at the 1981 MPHS annual conference. Clement and Tsai take readers from the missionariesâ halting first steps to the intensification of their efforts represented by the formal creation of the Japanese Mission within the Hawaiian Islands to the eventual rich harvest of Hawaiâi-born Latter-day Saints of Asian ancestry. In the second half of the twentieth century these Saints not only contributed to the Church in the islands but also served as mission and temple presidents in Asia and, in the cases of Adney Y. Komatsu, Sam Shimabukuro, and Chieko Okazaki, went on to hold general Church positions.

Chinese and Japanese Emigration to Hawaiâi

When Captain James Cook arrived in the Sandwich Islands in 1778, the native Hawaiian population was estimated between three-hundred to four-hundred thousand. A century later, it had been reduced through disease and infertility to an alarmingly low fifty-eight thousand. Already in the mid-nineteenth century, intermarriage between Hawaiian women and foreigners was steadily increasing. The resulting offspring were generally classified according to the fatherâs ethnic group, and thus demographic figures show a larger decrease. The drastic decline in the native Hawaiian population had far-reaching effects on the economy as well, specifically causing plantation owners to look to foreign sources for labor.2 One of these foreign sources was Asia.

Although the first Asians may have arrived in Hawaiâi as early as 1788, the major period of immigration for the Chinese and the Japanese was between 1852 and 1924. The Chinese were the first to come in significant numbers, and they originated almost exclusively from southeast Chinaâs Kwangtung Province.3 Two distinct ethnic groups, the Punti (local people) and Hakka (guest people), signed contractual agreements with plantations beginning in 1852. The Punti spoke Cantonese and were the original inhabitants of Kwangtung. The Hakka spoke their own dialect and originated from northern China. Antagonism between the two groups was carried to Hawaiâi.4

Chinese peasants arrived in Hawaiâi intent on becoming rich in a foreign land and then returning to China. After being plagued at home with such disorders as the First Anglo-Chinese or Opium War (1839â1842) and the Taiping Rebellion (1850s), these young men were eager to work in the reportedly idyllic âSandalwood Mountainsâ of Hawaiâi. The first 196 Chinese laborers signed five-year contracts at a modest wage. Before the 1870s, Chinese labor emigrants came on a relatively small scale. After 1870, however, the Chinese emigrants arrived in Hawaiâi in increasingly large numbersâas many as 3,924 in 1881 and 4,243 in 1883. Some of these later Chinese immigrants were skilled and avoided the harsh plantation life by opening shops and starting small industries. Hawaiâiâs Chinese population grew from 364, or 0.5 percent, in 1853 to 18,254, or 23 percent of the islandsâ total population, by 1884. The largest number of Chinese immigrants arrived between 1879 and 1887 when 24,974 entered the Hawaiian Kingdom, though another 10,381 departed.5

The first Japanese labor immigrants, a group of 148, arrived on June 19, 1868, from Yokohama. Because of complaints of ill treatment on the plantations, Japanâs government halted emigration until 1885. With the establishment of the Irwin Convention in 1885, better contracts and working conditions were negotiated and emigration received full governmental approval. From a total Japanese population of only 116 in Hawaiâi in 1884, the number of immigrants increased to 24,407 by 1896, or about 24 percent of the total population. The Japanese came in larger numbers than the Chinese. It is estimated that between 1885 and 1924 as many as 218,000 Japanese entered Hawaiâi. Of that number, approximately 125,000 remained. During the government contract period from 1885 to 1894, Japanese emigrants came mainly from southwestern Japan, specifically from the four prefectures of Hiroshima, Yamaguchi, Kumamoto, and Fukuoka, which were overpopulated with farmers. Hawaiâi offered a temperate climate similar to these areas, and it was also close to the sea.6

Both Chinese and Japanese immigrants became integral ethnic groups in Hawaiâi. Because the vast majority of laborers was male, intermarriage with Hawaiians was commonplace and formed the basis for Hawaiâiâs present multicultural population. From inauspicious beginnings on sugar plantations, succeeding generations of original Chinese and Japanese immigrants have lifted themselves economically and educationally. In Hawaiâi today, Chinese are noted leaders in the financial and business sectors, and Japanese hold important roles in government, education, and industry. The descendants of humble, imported laborers have permeated every facet of Hawaiâiâs society and are helping to build its international future.

The next year the new mission president, Edward Partridge Jr., updated President John Taylor on conditions at the LÄâie plantation and described his dealings with the Chinese.

On November 27, 1936, the First Presidency announced the appointment of Hilton A. Contributions of Chinese and Japanese Saints in Hawaiâi

To evaluate the Asian Latter-day Saint experience in Hawaiâi properly, one must bear in mind the similarities and differences in social evolution between what was occurring in the larger society of Hawaiâi and the much smaller world of the Asian Saints. With the Asiansâ higher educational, social, and economic status, it has been argued that traditional values for some Chinese and Japanese groups living in Hawaiâi were reshaped and redefined to the point that ethnic cohesiveness was no longer valid.39 We believe, however, that Asian Latter-day Saints generally have been successful in blending and balancing traditional cultural values with their new religion. Moreover, widespread racial mixing, with its corresponding variety in self-identity choices, complicates the matter of defining exactly who in Hawaiâi stems from Chinese or Japanese heritage. These considerations should be kept in mind throughout this concluding analysis.

An assessment of the contributions and experiences of Chinese and Japanese Latter-day Saints in Hawaiâi during the nineteenth and twentieth centuries is perhaps best approached through an examination of the progress of each generation. What we have termed the first generation of Chinese and Japanese Latter-day Saints comprises the handful of Asians who were converted from the 1870s to around 1920. Notable examples include the unidentified Chinese emigrant from Honolulu who was healed through faith and baptism in 1871; Brother Toko reportedly joined the Church in 1892 in Hawaiâi; Dr. T. Katsunuma, who was educated and converted in Utah and lived in the Islands from 1898 to 1950; and Lau Ah You, the mission cook for thirty-five years who converted in 1932.

These pioneer Asian Saints must be regarded as novelties in the nineteenth- and early twentieth-century mission dominated by Hawaiians and haloes. Since the early missionaries found success among the Hawaiian population, there were few, if any, attempts to spread the gospel to foreign labor immigrants, even those working on leased land in LÄâie. Church officials seemed most concerned with establishing LÄâie as a gathering place for Hawaiian Saints. In this objective they were successful. The uneducated, illiterate early Asian immigrants undoubtedly seemed as foreign and remote to nineteenth-century Mormons as they did to all nineteenth-century Hawaiians.

Unfortunately, but understandably, few opportunities arose for this first generation to contribute to the building up of the Church because of the language barrier and different cultural background. They seemed primarily concerned with remaining active members and applying Mormonism to traditional values. The inactivity of Dr. Katsunuma, because of his discouragement in not understanding church services conducted in Hawaiian, and the long, close relationship of mission cook Ah You before his baptism, serve as examples.

During the second generation of Asian members (1920s to 1950s) in Hawaiâi, local Church leaders became aware of the great potential the islands held as a training ground for future missionary programs in Asia. During this period, Asians were actively and specifically proselytized, with the greatest success being realized among Hawaiâiâs Japanese. They learned the gospel and became well acquainted with the Churchâs culture and organization. As the faith, knowledge, and membership of this second generation multiplied, opportunities for volunteer missionary work and some leadership positions were available. These opportunities and contributions expanded through the years of the Japanese and Central Pacific Mission in Hawaiâi. Real incentive for service and training among Asian members came with the withdrawal of mainland U.S. missionaries during World War II. The backbone of the Asian Latter-day Saint contingent in Hawaiâi was formed by this second generation (fig. 4).

Members who epitomized this new level of Church sophistication included Henry W. Underlying factors behind the success and lasting contributions of this generation were an increased emphasis on converting Asians, more knowledge of Church doctrines and institutions, and increasing opportunities for service and leadership.

The third generation, from the 1950s to the 1980s, clearly builds on the foundation laid by the previous generation. After World War II, those of Chinese and Japanese ancestry in Hawaiâi advanced rapidly in social and educational realms. This led to substantial roles in government, education, and business circles. Some members of Asian ancestry have even ascended the ranks of Church leadership, such as General Authority Adney Y. Komatsu, Regional Representative Glenn A. Lung, and a plethora of Asian mission presidentsâall born and trained in Hawaiâi. Particularly noteworthy in this third generation is its demonstrated faith in the gospel and dedication to the Church, as well as its ties to ethnic traditions. In light of these developments among Asian Latter-day Saints in Hawaiâi, credit must also be given to the Church College of Hawaiâi (now BYUâHawaiâi). Since its beginning in 1955, the college has played an overwhelmingly significant role in training potential leaders who return to build up the Church in their native countries. The first Chinese students arrived at the college in 1958, and they have since become one of the largest Asian ethnic groups on campus: students from Asia or Hawaiians of Asian ancestry make up nearly one-third of the student body. Not only do these often fledgling members gain spiritual strength and leadership training while on campus, but they are able to share their culture and their culturally conditioned comprehension of the gospel with the rest of the community, thereby enriching all.

Those of Chinese and Japanese heritage in Hawaiâi have been joining the Church for over one hundred years. Although their numbers, roles, and contributions were somewhat limited until the twentieth century, todayâs Asian members from Hawaiâi provide much of the Churchâs strength and leadership in the region and contribute to its growth in Asia. Chinese and Japanese Latter-day Saints in Hawaiâi have effectively and convincingly demonstrated the value of Hawaiâi as a spiritual meeting ground between East and West. One can only conclude that future generations of Asian Saints in Hawaiâi will be even more knowledgeable, more eloquent, and better prepared to build up the kingdom of God throughout the world.

Chinese and Japanese Emigration to Hawaiâi

When Captain James Cook arrived in the Sandwich Islands in 1778, the native Hawaiian population was estimated between three-hundred to four-hundred thousand. A century later, it had been reduced through disease and infertility to an alarmingly low fifty-eight thousand. Already in the mid-nineteenth century, intermarriage between Hawaiian women and foreigners was steadily increasing. The resulting offspring were generally classified according to the fatherâs ethnic group, and thus demographic figures show a larger decrease. The drastic decline in the native Hawaiian population had far-reaching effects on the economy as well, specifically causing plantation owners to look to foreign sources for labor.2 One of these foreign sources was Asia.

Although the first Asians may have arrived in Hawaiâi as early as 1788, the major period of immigration for the Chinese and the Japanese was between 1852 and 1924. The Chinese were the first to come in significant numbers, and they originated almost exclusively from southeast Chinaâs Kwangtung Province.3 Two distinct ethnic groups, the Punti (local people) and Hakka (guest people), signed contractual agreements with plantations beginning in 1852. The Punti spoke Cantonese and were the original inhabitants of Kwangtung. The Hakka spoke their own dialect and originated from northern China. Antagonism between the two groups was carried to Hawaiâi.4

Chinese peasants arrived in Hawaiâi intent on becoming rich in a foreign land and then returning to China. After being plagued at home with such disorders as the First Anglo-Chinese or Opium War (1839â1842) and the Taiping Rebellion (1850s), these young men were eager to work in the reportedly idyllic âSandalwood Mountainsâ of Hawaiâi. The first 196 Chinese laborers signed five-year contracts at a modest wage. Before the 1870s, Chinese labor emigrants came on a relatively small scale. After 1870, however, the Chinese emigrants arrived in Hawaiâi in increasingly large numbersâas many as 3,924 in 1881 and 4,243 in 1883. Some of these later Chinese immigrants were skilled and avoided the harsh plantation life by opening shops and starting small industries. Hawaiâiâs Chinese population grew from 364, or 0.5 percent, in 1853 to 18,254, or 23 percent of the islandsâ total population, by 1884. The largest number of Chinese immigrants arrived between 1879 and 1887 when 24,974 entered the Hawaiian Kingdom, though another 10,381 departed.5

The first Japanese labor immigrants, a group of 148, arrived on June 19, 1868, from Yokohama. Because of complaints of ill treatment on the plantations, Japanâs government halted emigration until 1885. With the establishment of the Irwin Convention in 1885, better contracts and working conditions were negotiated and emigration received full governmental approval. From a total Japanese population of only 116 in Hawaiâi in 1884, the number of immigrants increased to 24,407 by 1896, or about 24 percent of the total population. The Japanese came in larger numbers than the Chinese. It is estimated that between 1885 and 1924 as many as 218,000 Japanese entered Hawaiâi. Of that number, approximately 125,000 remained. During the government contract period from 1885 to 1894, Japanese emigrants came mainly from southwestern Japan, specifically from the four prefectures of Hiroshima, Yamaguchi, Kumamoto, and Fukuoka, which were overpopulated with farmers. Hawaiâi offered a temperate climate similar to these areas, and it was also close to the sea.6

Both Chinese and Japanese immigrants became integral ethnic groups in Hawaiâi. Because the vast majority of laborers was male, intermarriage with Hawaiians was commonplace and formed the basis for Hawaiâiâs present multicultural population. From inauspicious beginnings on sugar plantations, succeeding generations of original Chinese and Japanese immigrants have lifted themselves economically and educationally. In Hawaiâi today, Chinese are noted leaders in the financial and business sectors, and Japanese hold important roles in government, education, and industry. The descendants of humble, imported laborers have permeated every facet of Hawaiâiâs society and are helping to build its international future.

Contributions of Chinese and Japanese Saints in Hawaiâi

To evaluate the Asian Latter-day Saint experience in Hawaiâi properly, one must bear in mind the similarities and differences in social evolution between what was occurring in the larger society of Hawaiâi and the much smaller world of the Asian Saints. With the Asiansâ higher educational, social, and economic status, it has been argued that traditional values for some Chinese and Japanese groups living in Hawaiâi were reshaped and redefined to the point that ethnic cohesiveness was no longer valid.39 We believe, however, that Asian Latter-day Saints generally have been successful in blending and balancing traditional cultural values with their new religion. Moreover, widespread racial mixing, with its corresponding variety in self-identity choices, complicates the matter of defining exactly who in Hawaiâi stems from Chinese or Japanese heritage. These considerations should be kept in mind throughout this concluding analysis.

An assessment of the contributions and experiences of Chinese and Japanese Latter-day Saints in Hawaiâi during the nineteenth and twentieth centuries is perhaps best approached through an examination of the progress of each generation. What we have termed the first generation of Chinese and Japanese Latter-day Saints comprises the handful of Asians who were converted from the 1870s to around 1920. Notable examples include the unidentified Chinese emigrant from Honolulu who was healed through faith and baptism in 1871; Brother Toko reportedly joined the Church in 1892 in Hawaiâi; Dr. T. Katsunuma, who was educated and converted in Utah and lived in the Islands from 1898 to 1950; and Lau Ah You, the mission cook for thirty-five years who converted in 1932.

These pioneer Asian Saints must be regarded as novelties in the nineteenth- and early twentieth-century mission dominated by Hawaiians and haloes. Since the early missionaries found success among the Hawaiian population, there were few, if any, attempts to spread the gospel to foreign labor immigrants, even those working on leased land in LÄâie. Church officials seemed most concerned with establishing LÄâie as a gathering place for Hawaiian Saints. In this objective they were successful. The uneducated, illiterate early Asian immigrants undoubtedly seemed as foreign and remote to nineteenth-century Mormons as they did to all nineteenth-century Hawaiians.

Unfortunately, but understandably, few opportunities arose for this first generation to contribute to the building up of the Church because of the language barrier and different cultural background. They seemed primarily concerned with remaining active members and applying Mormonism to traditional values. The inactivity of Dr. Katsunuma, because of his discouragement in not understanding church services conducted in Hawaiian, and the long, close relationship of mission cook Ah You before his baptism, serve as examples.

During the second generation of Asian members (1920s to 1950s) in Hawaiâi, local Church leaders became aware of the great potential the islands held as a training ground for future missionary programs in Asia. During this period, Asians were actively and specifically proselytized, with the greatest success being realized among Hawaiâiâs Japanese.
