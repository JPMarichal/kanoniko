Conclusion

One of the many heroes of Kalaupapa was Joseph Dutton, originally of Stowe, Vermont, who worked selflessly among the patients for forty-four years. Before âBrotherâ Dutton, who was a member of another faith, died in Honolulu in March 1931, he wrote in his self-effacing style: âI wish to guard you against having too high an estimate of the work here. Yet virtually all who have visited Kalaupapa and written about it have been consistent in one point: our souls have been unforgettably touched by the place and the people.

After visiting Kalaupapa, Elder Matthew Cowley wrote:

I went there apprehending that I would be depressed. I left knowing that I had been exalted. I had expected that my heart, which is not too strong, would be torn with sympathy, but I went away with a feeling that it had been healed. I went away . . . appreciating my friends, loving my enemies, worshipping God, and with a heart purged of all pettiness. This is a transformation for me, and for it, I am indebted to the . . . We are fortunate to have lived at a time when we could know some of the individuals at Kalaupapa, for the day is coming when Kalaupapa and its beautiful people will be memorialized only by written records and moving photographic images.

Establishment of the Colony

In 1850, the year the Mormon missionaries first came to Hawaiâi, the Hawaiian Board of Health was established, ostensibly to deal with cholera, but it was forced to turn its attention more and more to Hansenâs disease. In January 1865, the Hawaiian government officially expressed its concern when King Kamehameha V signed an act to prevent the spread of leprosy. For eighteen hundred dollars, seven to eight hundred acres and fifteen to twenty good houses were purchased at Kalawao on the peninsula. On January 6, 1866, the first patientsânine men and three womenâwent ashore at Kalawao. Unfortunately, the healthy evacuees from the sold land had been absent long enough that the crops they left could not be harvested. Thus began an eight-year problem of adequately feeding the patients.

Were Mormons among the first patients? It seems likely when one considers the prevalence of Latter-day Saints among nineteenth-century Hawaiians, perhaps approaching 15 to 20 percent of the population. The first Latter-day Saint patient of whom I am certain, however, was Kitty Richardson Napela, wife of Jonatana Napela, an aliâi (chiefly) convert and subsequent leader in the Church. In November 1869, about four years after the first patients arrived at Kalawao, the Napelas returned from a trip to Utah, where they had traveled to receive their endowments. They could not have known that within three years Kitty would discover her own leprous condition. Though Jonatana himself was then free from the disease, in a touching act of love that would be repeated by other kÅkuas (helpers) over the years, he joined his wife as a resident of Kalawao on May 10, 1873. The famed Catholic Father Joseph Damien, who devoted his life to ministering to those with Hansenâs disease (fig. 1), arrived the same year as the Napelas.10 Because those without the disease were not allowed at the colony, Elder Napela apparently asked to be given supervisory responsibility so he could accompany and remain with his sick wife.11 His life on the Kalaupapa peninsula was one of increasing difficulty from the time of his arrival until his death six years later from conditions associated with having contracted leprosy himself. After 1887 the Board of Health began moving the health facilities and patients from Kalawao to Kalaupapa. By the 1890s, the bulk of the population was living at Kalaupapa, and the number of patients had increased significantly. During that decade, the leprosy epidemic reached its peak âwith more than a thousand people (including a sizable Latter-day Saint presence) at Kalaupapa, [representing] around 2 percent of the Hawaiian population.â14 The year-end mission report for 1894 listed the Kalaupapa Branch as having 149 members, the fourth largest in Hawaiâi.15

The Saints at Kalaupapa

In 1904, Elders William Waddoups and David Johnson dedicated the two Kalaupapa meetinghouses. The chapels at Kalawao and Kalaupapa were described by Waddoups as some of the finest in the Hawaiian mission. The chapel at Kalaupapa had been under construction for about five years and was built entirely from volunteer labor (fig. 2). The elders baptized ten people and noted that the new converts swelled Mormon membership at Kalaupapa to more than two hundred, all of them patients.16

Although the number of patients at Kalaupapa began to taper off in the succeeding years, the Church continued to thrive due to the membersâ devotion and the long periods of service by their leaders. A cancer victim himself, Brother Albino completely recovered from the disease through great faith and was given the assignment to conduct sacrament meetings for the little group in the Kalaupapa Branch (fig. 3). Brother Albino brought a change of clothes with him when he lead his group of tourists on mules down the trail and usually arrived just after the three active membersâKuulei Bell, Lucy Kaona, and Peter Keolaâhad met for Sunday School.

Conclusion

One of the many heroes of Kalaupapa was Joseph Dutton, originally of Stowe, Vermont, who worked selflessly among the patients for forty-four years. Before âBrotherâ Dutton, who was a member of another faith, died in Honolulu in March 1931, he wrote in his self-effacing style: âI wish to guard you against having too high an estimate of the work here. Work performed with good intention, to accomplish the will of Almighty God, for his glory, is the same in one place as in another. Oneâs Molokaâi can be anywhere.â25 No doubt Dutton was right in his geographical allusion, reminding us that it is not important where we serve. Yet virtually all who have visited Kalaupapa and written about it have been consistent in one point: our souls have been unforgettably touched by the place and the people.

After visiting Kalaupapa, Elder Matthew Cowley wrote:

I went there apprehending that I would be depressed. I left knowing that I had been exalted. I had expected that my heart, which is not too strong, would be torn with sympathy, but I went away with a feeling that it had been healed. I went away . . . appreciating my friends, loving my enemies, worshipping God, and with a heart purged of all pettiness. This is a transformation for me, and for it, I am indebted to the . . . Saints of Kalaupapa.26

We are fortunate to have lived at a time when we could know some of the individuals at Kalaupapa, for the day is coming when Kalaupapa and its beautiful people will be memorialized only by written records and moving photographic images.

A Servant of God: The Story of Jack Sing Kong Kuulei Bell

Today is a special day for me to be here to speak about a man who was special, not only to me, but to all the members of the Kalaupapa Branch and the Kalaupapa community.

Jack Sing Kong was born on December 4, 1892, in Canton, China. His father was Hoi Tak and his mother was Shee. Hoi Tak and Shee had eight children, two girls and six boys. They came from the Kong Dynasty, but Jack chose to use Sing as his surname. The family moved from Canton, China, to Hanalei, Kauai, which was their first home in Hawaiâi.

Jackâs dad purchased some land and started growing rice. Through the hard work of the family it became a very profitable business. Jack had many talents, including singing and performing, which he loved to do. He often sang at our community parties. Kalaupapa was known for its parties, and Jack was really a ham. Once he told me that he sang in a barbershop quartet, and his part was singing bass. The quartet placed second in a contest, and Jack boasted about that a lot.

In 1918, Jack contracted Hansenâs disease (leprosy) and was sent to Kalihi Hospital, a receiving station for leprosy patients.
