In Search of a Tale: A Personal Account Kenneth W. Baldridge

The general outline of events of December 7, 1941, has been retold countless times through congressional investigations, personal accounts, books, and motion pictures. About six oâclock on that Sunday morning, 183 planes took off from a Japanese task force of six aircraft carriers, two battleships, and about two dozen other craft for the three-hundred-mile flight to Pearl Harbor. A second flight of 170 planes was launched from the carriers at 7:15 a.m., a few minutes before the first group had passed over at Kahuku Point. The object of both flights was to destroy the U.S. Pacific Fleet, especially the aircraft carriers Enterprise and Lexington, which fortunately for the U.S. were at sea. To carry out this objective safely, it was necessary for the Japanese to neutralize U.S. military air defenses at the bases at Kaneohe, Ford Island, Hickam Field, âEwa, Wheeler, and Bellows. Maps depicting the attack routes show that the first wave divided north of Oâahu: some planes went along the north shore toward Haleâiwa, while others paralleled the windward coast toward Kaneohe Naval Air Station. The squadron acted quickly and decisively. Before the day was through, approximately 3,500 Americans had been killed or wounded. The once proud U.S. Pacific Fleet was in shambles.

LÄâie in the early 1940s was largely a sleepy Mormon community, composed primarily of Hawaiians and some Samoans, near the northeast corner of the island of Oâahu. The people farmed, fished, and worked at various trades. The only building of any size was the beautiful, white Hawaiâi Temple, dedicated by President Heber J. Grant in 1919. For decades stories have circulated among Church members about an attempted bombing of the temple during the surprise raid on Pearl Harbor. What did or did not happen in the skies over LÄâie that December day in 1941 has been the subject of considerable curiosity and some controversy. This article is an account of the various stories related to that day, an attempt to evaluate their validity and the extent to which they have spread, and finally, an appeal for perspective.

Since the late 1960s, the story of the Pearl Harbor raid has been told among the people of LÄâie and elsewhere to Latter-day Saintsâ joy or consternation, depending upon the listenerâs point of view. Part two of the story is equally intriguing. After failing to discharge his bomb over LÄâie, the pilot continued on his way, successfully dropping his load at Pearl Harbor or Kaneohe. Reports of this story have surfaced in Japan, Australia, Korea, Hawaiâi, Canada, and mainland America. Perhaps by now it has spread even further. My search for truth has produced some interesting results. For example, there are at least four variations of the bombing attempt besides the one previously cited. What is the possibility that an attempted bombing might have happened? The odds against it are staggering. First, the raid was planned and conducted in great secrecy. Surprise was the necessary element for success. The primary objective was the destruction of the American fleet, and Pearl Harbor was the first target hit. (Kaneohe was hit about the same time; there is even a possibility that the first bomb fell there a minute or two before Pearl Harbor was attacked.) Although the Japanese passed over the huge army post at Schofield Barracks and the two Army Air Corps bases at Wheeler and Hickam Fields, these were not attacked until after the first bombs fell on âBattleship Rowâ alongside Ford Island. It is not likely that a plane would be dispatched to bomb some unknown building while still some fifteen minutes from the primary target. Second, some versions describe the pilot as flying off on his own unauthorized bombing mission. Anyone who has served in the armed forces of either the United States or Japan finds this possibility difficult to accept. Third, the attacking planes bombed only confirmed military targets.2

Surely there must be some explanation for the alleged bombing story, I thought. Behind nearly every rumor there is a grain of truth that served as the original kernel from which the story grew. But the question was how to find that kernel.

The scriptures tell us that two witnesses are required to establish the validity of a story. Japanese Military Aircraft and the Bombing Story

Because Kahawaii described the aircraft he saw that morning, a comparison can be made between his description and the three types of aircraft used by the Japanese at Pearl Harbor. Knowing the ordnance the planes carried and the specific missions and capabilities of each type of plane can also help determine which plane Kahawaii might have seen. There were two bomber types, the Nakajima B5N2, nicknamed the âKateâ by the Americans, and the Aichi D3A1, or âValâ as it was called by Americans. These bombers were escorted by the famed Japanese Zero (Zeke), or Mitsubishi A6M2 fighter plane, which was almost certainly not involved in an attempted bombing of the temple, for this airplane carried no bombs and was assigned only bomber protection and strafing duties.31

Kahawaii claims that he saw bomb bay doors under the fuselage of the Japanese plane. However, none of the Japanese carrier aircraft in 1941 had bomb bays. Rather, the bombs were affixed externally under the wings and to the fuselage of the aircraft involved in the attack. The Kate (type 97) bombers were the only planes armed with bombs that looked like torpedoes, but they carried only one bomb each. Their bombs were designed to be dropped from high altitudes in horizontal flight on American ships. These facts tend to rule out the Kate, since Kahawaii described the plane he saw as diving and carrying a torpedolike bomb, and Stout claimed the pilot dived toward the temple to bomb it.

The Val (type 98) dive bomber, with two crew members, is the more likely candidate for the attack. The Vals did not carry torpedo-shaped bombs but did dive to deliver their ordnance. However, none of the Vals in the first attack carried multiple bombs, so it would not be likely that one would have âa bomb left,â as Stout understood from Ogawa Sanâs story. None of the attack aircraft in the second attack carried torpedoes or torpedo-shaped bombs.

Bob Kahawaiiâs report of the bomb he saw is significant. It was ânot to[o] big, a smaller bomb, and long just like a torpedo.â One of the three types of Japanese aircraft involved in the Pearl Harbor attack carried ordnance roughly fitting Kahawaiiâs description. The Kate bomber carried a 1,760-pound bomb encased in a torpedo housing, but the bomb was designed for dropping on sea, not land targets. There was only one such piece of ordnance attached to each of the Kates in the first of the two attacks, so the pilot probably would not have had this type of bomb left when he returned to his ship. The Kates in the second wave that morning carried multiple bombs, but none of these was torpedo shaped. The details of Kahawaiiâs story indicate that the alleged bomber was part of the first wave of the Pearl Harbor attack.

Another of Kahawaiiâs details may be significant in helping investigate the veracity of his account. He recorded that the bomb was hanging down just as if it were on a hook, though it did not fall. Actually, the Japanese had to design and create special equipment for releasing their bombs, since at least two types of bombs dropped were specially designed for the Pearl Harbor attack. The release mechanisms were so new that they were still being installed and adjusted right up to the time the thirty-vessel Japanese task force reached Hitokappu Bay in the Kurrle Islands, the gathering point for the task force. Thus, a Japanese pilot may have had difficulty dropping his bomb because of mechanical failure, although no report of such has come forth.32

The area around Kahawaiiâs home was close to the center of LÄâie (as it was layed out in 1941). Surely, even in a sleepy village others would have stood alert. Especially on a Sunday morning, other people besides Kahawaii should have observed (and reported) the Japanese bomber if it had dived toward the temple. Moreover, both the Val dive bombers and the Kate torpedo and high-level bombers had multiple crew members. Unfortunately, no additional crew member is mentioned as part of either manâs story.

The possible name of the pilot, as recorded in Stoutâs journal, could be a clue to help verify Kahawaiiâs and Stoutâs stories. Professor Ikuhiko Hata of Tokyo compiled a roster of the Japanese flyers at Pearl Harbor. Unfortunately, the roster was not translated into English and part of it is missing. Many Pearl Harbor pilots were killed in the Battle of Midway. However, if Stoutâs story is true, the pilot in question survived the war.33

Stout recalled that the name outside the Matsumoto City house he and Wright tracted was Nishimoto, but since the house was a duplex, Stout concluded that the name belonged to the other party in the dwelling. Of the three variant names for the pilot in Stoutâs journalâOkawa, Ohara, and Ogawaâthe first and last are common Japanese names, but no lead related to these names has developed.34

An Evaluation of the Storyâs Details

One of the central issues to consider is the designated targets of the Japanese attack. The primary targets for the Japanese aircraft were U.S. naval vessels, military air bases, and aircraft at these bases. Japanese leaders considered it vital to cripple the American fleet and aircraft on the ground. Japan wanted to operate for at least six months relatively unimpeded by American involvement as it pursued its conquest of the South Pacific and Asian rim. Nevertheless, Zero fighters did strafe homes, pedestrians, and cars.35 Moreover, the Shokaku and Zuikaku, the newest aircraft carriers in the Japanese fleet, had relatively untrained pilots. They were assigned only land targets that were considered large enough for inexperienced pilots to hit on their maiden combat missions.36 Certainly one of these pilots could have had his target obscured by smoke or clouds or have had a malfunction of his bomb release mechanism. No pilot would have returned to his ship and tried to land with a bomb attached and dangling precariously from its hanger. World War II planes customarily jettisoned unused bombs over water, if possible, and without arming the bombs, so they would not explode. Thus, in this respect, Stoutâs story fits reality better than Kahawaiiâs.

Another question concerns the assertion in Stoutâs journal that the pilot thought he would suicide dive, jisatsu, on his last try at the temple. The kamikaze phase of the war did not occur until late in 1944 and 1945. Stoutâs account thus appears, at first, to be an anachronism. However, Gordon W. Prangeâs brilliant thirty-seven year study of the Pearl Harbor attack reveals that Japanese pilots did discuss diving their stricken aircraft onto American targets, and some may have done so before 1944.37

A geographical issue concerns the flight path of the attacking and returning Japanese aircraft. No outbound flight plans show any plane within five miles of LÄâie. Planes returning to their carriers were to use a rendezvous point twenty miles northwest of Kaâena Point, far from LÄâie.38 However, prior to the attack on Pearl Harbor, Japanese fighters had never taken offensive action more than a hundred miles from their base, and the planes had no homing devices.39 In the excitement and confusion of combat conditions, it is conceivable that a Japanese plane could have passed over LÄâie. The return to the carriers was much less orderly than the inbound flight. American radar sites could obtain no definite plot for the returning planes.40

Some critics of the temple bombing story have asked how it could be true when God apparently did not see fit to protect his other temples, such as Solomonâs temple, the Nauvoo temple, and others. Incidents like these have caused many to wonder why God did not act to prevent damage or invasion. But the Polynesian people have a strong faith, perhaps powerful enough to move Deity to protect âtheirâ sacred building from imminent destruction. However, no one had any reason to believe that the temple was being threatened at the time, nor would anyone wish to suggest that the faith of the Jews or of the Mormons in Nauvoo was any less great.

Of the two first-person accounts of the purported attack, Stoutâs is harder to explain away. Stoutâs lack of experience in Japanese never prevented him from understanding the alleged bomberâs hand motions, pidgin English, and facial expressions when he saw the picture of the Hawaiâi Temple. Stout remains convinced that the events recorded in his journal demonstrate that God protected his temple. Stout has pondered the remarkable interview with the Japanese pilot for over forty years, and to him, at least, divine intervention occurred in LÄâie on that fateful day in December 1941. As historian Benedetto Croce wrote: âThere is no history that completely satisfies us because any construction of ours generates new facts and new problems and solicits new solutions.â41

So was there an attempt to bomb the Hawaiâi Temple, and if so, was it prevented by divine intervention? That remains uncertain. As Plutarch observed, âHow difficult a matter it is to trace and find out the truth of anything by history.â42 Nonetheless increased knowledge and understanding resulting from historical studies can be satisfying and rewarding processes in themselves.

In Search of a Tale: A Personal Account Kenneth W. Baldridge

The general outline of events of December 7, 1941, has been retold countless times through congressional investigations, personal accounts, books, and motion pictures. About six oâclock on that Sunday morning, 183 planes took off from a Japanese task force of six aircraft carriers, two battleships, and about two dozen other craft for the three-hundred-mile flight to Pearl Harbor. A second flight of 170 planes was launched from the carriers at 7:15 a.m., a few minutes before the first group had passed over at Kahuku Point. The object of both flights was to destroy the U.S. Pacific Fleet, especially the aircraft carriers Enterprise and Lexington, which fortunately for the U.S. were at sea. To carry out this objective safely, it was necessary for the Japanese to neutralize U.S. military air defenses at the bases at Kaneohe, Ford Island, Hickam Field, âEwa, Wheeler, and Bellows. Maps depicting the attack routes show that the first wave divided north of Oâahu: some planes went along the north shore toward Haleâiwa, while others paralleled the windward coast toward Kaneohe Naval Air Station. The squadron acted quickly and decisively. Before the day was through, approximately 3,500 Americans had been killed or wounded. The once proud U.S. Pacific Fleet was in shambles.

LÄâie in the early 1940s was largely a sleepy Mormon community, composed primarily of Hawaiians and some Samoans, near the northeast corner of the island of Oâahu. The people farmed, fished, and worked at various trades. The only building of any size was the beautiful, white Hawaiâi Temple, dedicated by President Heber J. Grant in 1919. What is the possibility that an attempted bombing might have happened? The odds against it are staggering. First, the raid was planned and conducted in great secrecy. Second, some versions describe the pilot as flying off on his own unauthorized bombing mission. Anyone who has served in the armed forces of either the United States or Japan finds this possibility difficult to accept. Third, the attacking planes bombed only confirmed military targets.2

The Original Kernel

Surely there must be some explanation for the alleged bombing story, I thought. Behind nearly every rumor there is a grain of truth that served as the original kernel from which the story grew. But the question was how to find that kernel.

Conclusion

After an extensive investigation, it is my recommendation that the tale be confined to the vast collection of Mormon legends and not be repeated as an actual faith-promoting incident. In the process, I occasionally found myself acting as an amateur folklorist as I traced variations in the stories and the different places where they surfaced. Japanese Military Aircraft and the Bombing Story

Because Kahawaii described the aircraft he saw that morning, a comparison can be made between his description and the three types of aircraft used by the Japanese at Pearl Harbor. Knowing the ordnance the planes carried and the specific missions and capabilities of each type of plane can also help determine which plane Kahawaii might have seen. There were two bomber types, the Nakajima B5N2, nicknamed the âKateâ by the Americans, and the Aichi D3A1, or âValâ as it was called by Americans. These bombers were escorted by the famed Japanese Zero (Zeke), or Mitsubishi A6M2 fighter plane, which was almost certainly not involved in an attempted bombing of the temple, for this airplane carried no bombs and was assigned only bomber protection and strafing duties.31

Kahawaii claims that he saw bomb bay doors under the fuselage of the Japanese plane. However, none of the Japanese carrier aircraft in 1941 had bomb bays. Rather, the bombs were affixed externally under the wings and to the fuselage of the aircraft involved in the attack. The Kate (type 97) bombers were the only planes armed with bombs that looked like torpedoes, but they carried only one bomb each. Their bombs were designed to be dropped from high altitudes in horizontal flight on American ships. These facts tend to rule out the Kate, since Kahawaii described the plane he saw as diving and carrying a torpedolike bomb, and Stout claimed the pilot dived toward the temple to bomb it.

The Val (type 98) dive bomber, with two crew members, is the more likely candidate for the attack. The Vals did not carry torpedo-shaped bombs but did dive to deliver their ordnance. However, none of the Vals in the first attack carried multiple bombs, so it would not be likely that one would have âa bomb left,â as Stout understood from Ogawa Sanâs story. None of the attack aircraft in the second attack carried torpedoes or torpedo-shaped bombs.

Bob Kahawaiiâs report of the bomb he saw is significant. It was ânot to[o] big, a smaller bomb, and long just like a torpedo.â One of the three types of Japanese aircraft involved in the Pearl Harbor attack carried ordnance roughly fitting Kahawaiiâs description. The Kate bomber carried a 1,760-pound bomb encased in a torpedo housing, but the bomb was designed for dropping on sea, not land targets. There was only one such piece of ordnance attached to each of the Kates in the first of the two attacks, so the pilot probably would not have had this type of bomb left when he returned to his ship. The Kates in the second wave that morning carried multiple bombs, but none of these was torpedo shaped. The details of Kahawaiiâs story indicate that the alleged bomber was part of the first wave of the Pearl Harbor attack.

Another of Kahawaiiâs details may be significant in helping investigate the veracity of his account. He recorded that the bomb was hanging down just as if it were on a hook, though it did not fall. Actually, the Japanese had to design and create special equipment for releasing their bombs, since at least two types of bombs dropped were specially designed for the Pearl Harbor attack. The release mechanisms were so new that they were still being installed and adjusted right up to the time the thirty-vessel Japanese task force reached Hitokappu Bay in the Kurrle Islands, the gathering point for the task force. Thus, a Japanese pilot may have had difficulty dropping his bomb because of mechanical failure, although no report of such has come forth.32

An Evaluation of the Storyâs Details

One of the central issues to consider is the designated targets of the Japanese attack. The primary targets for the Japanese aircraft were U.S. naval vessels, military air bases, and aircraft at these bases. Japanese leaders considered it vital to cripple the American fleet and aircraft on the ground. Japan wanted to operate for at least six months relatively unimpeded by American involvement as it pursued its conquest of the South Pacific and Asian rim. Nevertheless, Zero fighters did strafe homes, pedestrians, and cars.35 Moreover, the Shokaku and Zuikaku, the newest aircraft carriers in the Japanese fleet, had relatively untrained pilots. They were assigned only land targets that were considered large enough for inexperienced pilots to hit on their maiden combat missions.36 Certainly one of these pilots could have had his target obscured by smoke or clouds or have had a malfunction of his bomb release mechanism. No pilot would have returned to his ship and tried to land with a bomb attached and dangling precariously from its hanger. World War II planes customarily jettisoned unused bombs over water, if possible, and without arming the bombs, so they would not explode. Thus, in this respect, Stoutâs story fits reality better than Kahawaiiâs.

Another question concerns the assertion in Stoutâs journal that the pilot thought he would suicide dive, jisatsu, on his last try at the temple. The kamikaze phase of the war did not occur until late in 1944 and 1945. Stoutâs account thus appears, at first, to be an anachronism. However, Gordon W. Prangeâs brilliant thirty-seven year study of the Pearl Harbor attack reveals that Japanese pilots did discuss diving their stricken aircraft onto American targets, and some may have done so before 1944.37

Planes returning to their carriers were to use a rendezvous point twenty miles northwest of Kaâena Point, far from LÄâie.38 However, prior to the attack on Pearl Harbor, Japanese fighters had never taken offensive action more than a hundred miles from their base, and the planes had no homing devices.39 In the excitement and confusion of combat conditions, it is conceivable that a Japanese plane could have passed over LÄâie. The return to the carriers was much less orderly than the inbound flight. American radar sites could obtain no definite plot for the returning planes.40
