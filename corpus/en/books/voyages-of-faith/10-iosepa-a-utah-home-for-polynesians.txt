The story of Iosepa is an intriguing but often misunderstood chapter in Mormon history. Some have cast it as something of a tragedy: innocent, trusting islander Saints accustomed to a tropical and oceanic lifestyle were lured, because of their religious faith, into the midst of the harsh Great Basin desert. Then because of their almost nonexistent financial resources, they were stuck in Utah for nearly three decades as farm and ranch hands under the direction of haole (Caucasian) taskmasters. The story that Dennis H. Atkin reconstructs from primary sources is quite different. Sensitivity and generosity, rather than severity and greediness, characterized the actions of colony leaders and Church officials. First Presidency members Joseph F. Smith, after whom the colony was named (Iosepa is Hawaiian for Joseph), and George Q. Cannon, who was one of the first LDS missionaries in Hawaiâi, manifested a marked affection for the Polynesian Saints.

Twice in twenty years (1985 and 1998), Atkin shared information from his pioneering 1958 masterâs thesis and subsequent studies on this topic with the Mormon Pacific Historical Society. Below is an edited version that weaves together portions of both presentations.

One of the most interesting colonies founded in the settling of the western part of the United States was the Polynesian settlement of Iosepa, the Hawaiian name for Joseph. This Utah community, located about seventy-five miles southwest of Salt Lake City, in Skull Valley, Tooele County, was established and fostered by the Church. It was founded in 1889, after much of the Great Basin had already been colonized, and disbanded in 1917, after virtually all of its several hundred inhabitants had returned to Hawaiâi to assist with the building of the recently announced temple in LÄâie. Even though Iosepaâs quarter-century life span was filled with challenges, colony descendants look back with fondness on that period and hold annual reunions to commemorate it.

Hawaiian Saints Gather to Utah

Even though LÄâie had been established as a gathering place for Hawaiian Church members in 1865, some of the early Hawaiian converts wanted to gather to Zion along with converts from other parts of the world. Church leaders, however, did not encourage them to come. On the contrary, Hawaiian converts were discouraged by Church leaders from immigrating to Utah for two main reasons: (1) the Hawaiian government prohibited such emigration, and (2) the climate extremes in Utah were much harsher than that of the Pacific. The summers were hotter, the winters were colder, and the air drier than in the islands. Also, more effort was required to obtain food and other necessities of life in Utah. In 1891 the First Presidency wrote to the Minister of Foreign Affairs in Hawaiâi about the Church leadersâ long-standing sensitivity to the obstacles involved in immigration:

We think it can be truthfully stated that there has been no encouragement given to the native Hawaiian people of our faith to migrate to this country. For many years many of them have been urging our Elders to arrange affairs so that they could come; but there has been a feeling of reluctance entertained by us concerning their coming as we know how great a trial it might prove to them to live in a climate and in surroundings such as ours, so different in almost every respect to their position in their native Islands.1

Although laws prohibiting Hawaiian citizens from leaving their island home were not changed for some time, exceptions were made and occasionally Hawaiian Church members were allowed to go to Utah. In 1873 two Hawaiian boys traveled to Utah with William King and George Nebeker when they returned in 1873 from their missionary service.2 Then in 1882, eight Hawaiians (four adults and four children) accompanied Harvey H. Cluff, who had been recently released as mission president, and Elder J. G. Knell as they returned to Salt Lake City. A newspaper article reporting their arrival further stated:

There is a law in the Hawaiian Kingdom which prohibits the natives from emigrating to other parts of the world, but in consequence of the government [of Hawaiâi] having proofs before them that the only disposition of the community of Latter-day Saints toward the people is to benefit them, exceptions are made in the cases of those desiring to come to Utah.3

Around 1884 the Hawaiian government revoked its law prohibiting citizens from emigrating, and the Hawaiians wishing to go to Utah found it much easier to do so. Generally they traveled with returning missionaries and settled in the old Nineteenth Ward around Warm Springs in the northwest part of Salt Lake City. This was a logical choice since it was the home ward of Matthew Noall, Frederick Beesley, George Nebeker, and other former Hawaiian missionaries who were friendly to and supportive of the Polynesian community.

By mid-1889 approximately seventy-five Hawaiian Church members had migrated to Salt Lake City (fig. 1). Generally speaking, Polynesian immigrants were not well assimilated by the Caucasian population in Salt Lake City. The islanders usually could obtain only low-paying, manual-labor jobs and they were often unemployed part of each year. Few purchased or built businesses of their own. The Polynesians found themselves in an environment quite foreign to anything they had ever experienced.4

It became apparent to Church leaders that the islanders needed to have a permanent location where they could obtain year-round employment and control more of their own social environment.5 A plan was approved by the First Presidency to purchase a large ranch on which the Polynesians could establish a colony after the order of a âcompany town,â a place where they could have their own community, build their own homes, have year-round employment, retain their cultural identity, and adapt at their own pace to the general cultural milieu that surrounded them. This community was christened Iosepa.

Saints Find a Home in Utah

In May 1889, a land selection committee was appointed that consisted of three men who had been active in missionary work in the islands. These three men, Harvey H. Cluff, William W. Cluff, and Fred A. Mitchell, went to the Hawaiians residing in the Warm Springs area and presented the plan to acquire land for a colony. The Hawaiians sanctioned the plan and promptly appointed three from their own group to work on the committee: J. W. Kauleinamoku, George Kamakaniau, and Napeha.6

Early in June, the six men on the selection committee set out on their assignment. During the following two weeks, they inspected numerous farms and ranches offered for sale in Utah, Weber, Cache, and Tooele Counties. Because of the farmland to population ratio, good farming land was at a premium. Most people who owned fertile farm land did not want to sell it, and many who did not own good land wanted to buy.

The committee visited the choice locations they did find and wrote a report that was submitted to the First Presidency. The report included a description of each location and an evaluation of its suitability for the colony. Of the available choices, the 1,920 acres of the John T. Rich property located in Skull Valley, Tooele County, was by far the most desirable. This ranch had ample water and fertile land, and it was reasonably priced. There was more land nearby that could be obtained from the government. As the committee wrote in its report, the property included

extensive summer and winter range; cheap and abundant supply of fencing; extensive water supply; building materials easy accessible; mild climate; a large crop nearly matured more than necessary to supply [the Saintsâ] wants for the coming year; teams, wagons, harnesses, farming implements, cows, chickens, pigs, everything needful for their support.7

The day after the committee submitted its report, the First Presidency met with them and by unanimous vote accepted the committeeâs recommendation to purchase land in Skull Valley.8 The First Presidency wrote to William King, then mission president in Hawaiâi, informing him of the decision and expressing hope that the Skull Valley colony would âprove a success and become a mother colony after which others may be patterned when the time comes for the gathering of the remnants of the House of Israel who accept the Gospel on the isles of the Pacific.â9 The colony was legally incorporated as the Iosepa Agricultural and Stock Company, and a date was set for the Hawaiian Saints to move to Iosepa. Church officials hoped that the Iosepa Agricultural and Stock Company would become a self-sustaining entity, but until that time it was expected that the Church, as an institution, would help the colony and the company financially.

Some people who have visited Iosepa have questioned why this location was selected for the colony. In the summer it is hot and dry. Very little vegetation grows without irrigation. Other valleys in Utah appeared more productive. Although the winter weather was milder in Tooele County than in the other places under consideration, the winters were cold, especially when compared with the Pacific islands. The first winter the islanders spent in Iosepa (1889â1890), the temperature was especially cold. Yet, in the context of the times, Skull Valley offered the best situation available. According to historian Leonard Arrington:

The prime economic problem of Mormon Country in the late 1870âs and 1880âs was overpopulation. In every valley, there were signs that the continued flow of immigration, and the natural increase in population had filled up the land. . . . Projects to increase the supply of irrigable land were sponsored by ward, stake, and general church organizations. Here and there, from Blackfoot, Idaho, in the north, to Mesa, Arizona, in the south; from Manassa, Colorado in the east, to Southern Nevada in the west, available patches of land were reclaimed from the desert and put in the way of raising grains, hay, and vegetables.10

Hawaiians Settle in Iosepa

Most of the Polynesian Saints living in Salt Lake City decided to join the new colony. On the evening of August 26, 1889, the prospective colonists enjoyed a farewell feast prepared by the Salt Lake Nineteenth and Twenty-second wards. The next morning forty-five or fifty Hawaiians traveled by train to Garfield Junction.11 The First Presidency assigned members of the Tooele Stake the task of transporting them from Garfield Junction to their new home by teams and wagons. Before departing, the Hawaiian Saints entertained the local members with a program of music and dance. The following day, on August 28, 1889, the islanders were taken to Iosepa, the place that was to be the home of Polynesian Mormons in Utah for the next twenty-eight years (map 1). Harvey Cluff, the president of the Iosepa Agricultural and Stock Company, proclaimed August 28 to be Hawaiian Pioneer Day. In the evening, a special meeting was held that consisted of songs, prayers, and speeches of thanksgiving. Every year thereafter, August 28 was celebrated in Iosepa just as other Utahns celebrated July 24, the anniversary of the first Mormons entering Salt Lake Valley.

[caption] Map 1[/caption]

So eager were the colonists to get things underway that a survey of the townsite was started that first day by Fred A. Mitchell, assisted by Francis M. Lyman Jr., and completed two days later. Each block in the townsite was divided into four corner lots. The lots contained sufficient space for a home, garden, and barn and corral facilities for livestock. Family heads drew lots for these sites. If the lot suited the person who drew it, title to the land could be obtained by purchase. Shortly thereafter, to meet housing construction needs, the company bought a nearby saw mill for $1,497.75 from a man named E. Broad and employed him to operate it.12 The colonists also found it necessary to select a burial site. Cluff and a group of the Hawaiian elders selected an elevated plot on the northeast corner of the townsite for the cemetery. The next day, September 15, 1889, Iosepaâs first funeral was held after an elderly lady by the name of Makaopiopio died.13

As winter approached, the Iosepa colonizers settled down to the serious business of building their settlement and producing a livelihood in Skull Valley. The Iosepa Agricultural and Stock Company owned all the colonyâs land and livestock, except the lots that a few colonists had purchased and the teams or riding horses owned by individuals. The colonists received a daily or monthly wage from the company for their work. Necessities of life were purchased from the company or through its officials. In the beginning, a bookkeeping system was used in which the workers were credited with work performed and charged for goods purchased without the use of money. This system was used until after Thomas A. Waddoups became president of the colony in 1901. Shortly thereafter a scrip system was inaugurated, doing away with the laborious bookkeeping system. A Salt Lake City printing house printed a special series of paper scrip in 5Â¢, 10Â¢, 25Â¢, 50Â¢, and $1.00 denominations (fig. 2). This was used to pay the colonists for their labor, and, in turn, the colonists used it to pay for goods and services received. Occasionally, some colonists went to Salt Lake City to shop, and the company endeavored to have enough cash on hand to exchange for scrip at such times. Later, as the company became a paying venture, cash replaced scrip.14

Settlers Experience Homesickness

Some of the Hawaiians were not happy in Iosepa. A few wanted to return to Salt Lake City; others longed for Hawaiâi. Homesickness afflicted many, especially when the weather was cold or hot or when they had difficulty earning enough money to purchase what they needed. Supplying day-to-day needs was harder in Utah than in Hawaiâi. Fishing skills were not helpful in Iosepa, fruit was not simply plucked from naturally growing trees, and more clothes were required. A few of the Hawaiians became disillusioned and wanted to return to the islands.

When news of the dissatisfaction of some of the Hawaiians in Utah got back to Hawaiian government officials, money was appropriated by the Hawaiian Legislature to help any who wished to return to the islands but could not afford tickets. Government officials and representatives even went to Utah to encourage the Hawaiians to return. The first eight Hawaiians taken back from Utah by the Hawaiian government arrived in Hawaiâi on August 7, 1890, just one year after Iosepa was founded.15 However, in a letter written on October 1, 1890, from William A. Kinney, Hawaiâiâs Minister of Foreign Affairs, then in Utah to investigate matters, to David McKinley, Hawaiian Consul General stationed in San Francisco, it was reported that:

The majority of the natives prefer to remain in Utah though many wish to return.

None are destitute, or need be, as times were never better in Salt Lake than now and there is plenty of demand for labor. The body of the natives are over in Skull Valley, Church farming property, and only those are in Salt Lake who have broken away from the commands of the Church. These, of course, are the ones who wish to return. The many others will probably avail themselves of the chance to go home if they get it, who, until that time, do not wish to show their hands to the Church authorities. Home sickness is what makes them wish to return.

Some of the old time natives, who are thoroughly at home, declared, when they heard that the Government was going to send for them, that they would go to jail before they would comply. While these natives in Salt Lake are not destitute yet they donât get along very well and cannot raise enough money to return, and they are anxiously waiting the action of the Legislature which they think will send enough for them to get home on.16

Kinney reported that the governor of Utah Territory launched an investigation into the living conditions of the Hawaiians in Skull Valley. Despite the general satisfaction with life in Iosepa, Charles William Naâau offered a dissenting voice. He claimed that all the islanders were ordered to move to Iosepa and that nobody could go to Salt Lake City or any other place very far off unless they got a permit from the manager. Naâau himself wanted to go to Salt Lake City where he could earn cash âbut the President will not let me go, and the Manager watches us closely.â17 The investigator alleged that there were Hawaiians in both Salt Lake City and Iosepa who âhave been much disappointed and are very homesick, and if given full freedom would gladly return to the more congenial climate of the Sandwich Islands, except possibly a few who have become fanatical in their religious beliefs.â18 Governor Thomas sent this report to James O. Blaine, U.S. Secretary of State. Perhaps in an effort to present a balanced view, Thomas also included an editorial from the Deseret Evening News that refuted some of the ideas and allegations presented by the investigators:

Some of the native members of the Mormon Church desired to come to this Territory and received permission to do so. On their arrival they obtained work, chiefly in this city. They were well satisfied and other natives followed them. But it soon appeared that they would not rise above the ordinary laborers, unless some better opportunity than this city afforded was found for them. Several Elders who had been missionaries to the Islands joined in the formation of a company and purchased some land in Skull valley as a place for the Hawaiian Colony. The natives removed there and were pleased with the change.

A few of them, not more than ten or eleven, since the first arrival, desired to return to the Islands and went there. One who had been ex-communicated for improper conduct spread the reports of the dissatisfaction of the colony and thus the movement for an appropriation to take them back was started. . . .

There is no destitution among the natives in Skull Valley. Some of those who have returned were improvident and spent more than their income, so that they ran behind their brethren in the possession of âcreature comforts.â But they have all been supplied with necessaries and some luxuries through the kindness of the Elders having the matter in charge.

The Hawaiians, so we learn, are doing very well and are contented and prosperous.19

In attempting to facilitate the removal of a handful of Hawaiians to the islands, Kinney felt that his efforts were being hampered by actual or perceived statements of Church leaders to the Hawaiians, indicating that it would be sinful for the Hawaiians to leave Utah, and that if they did, God would be displeased. Kinney sought clarification from the First Presidency on the matter. In their reply, they thanked Kinney for his assistance on behalf of the Hawaiians and then explained:

The most that can be said in regard to our influencing these people to remain is that our people have encouraged them, inasmuch as they had come here, to make the best of their situation and to stay, assuring them that if they applied themselves to their labors they would be soon in a position of comfort and comparative independence.20

In a postscript to their letter, the First Presidency added,

As a further proof of what we have said concerning our feelings toward those who have returned, we may mention the fact that when Samuela Kii and Lima and their families left this country to return to the Islands a letter was given to them recommending them to the kind attention of the Presiding Elder at LÄâie. He was informed to this effect: âThey leave with our good feelings, although we think they are making a mistake. They should be treated kindly and be given a chance to work if they desire it and not be cut off.â21

Again in 1894, Hawaiian government officials sought to encourage the Hawaiian pioneers in Utah to return to the islands. In March of that year Cecil Brown, an agent of the Hawaiian government, came to Iosepa and offered to pay return fare to the islands for anyone who would forsake his or her Utah home and go back to Hawaiâi. Some seemed inclined to accept the offer.22 The next month, fourteen Hawaiians (five adults and nine children) left Utah, took a train to San Francisco, and boarded a ship for Hawaiâi.23

Later that summer, the Iosepa colonists received a letter from the Hawaiian Consul General in San Francisco offering free passage to Hawaiâi to all who would go. To some of the colonists, this was a command from their governmentâa command that must be obeyed; others, however, regarded their allegiance to their church as paramount to all others. The Sunday after the Consul Generalâs letter was received, President Cluff addressed the people on the gathering of Israel in the last days, a work that he said was then going on. He told them that they were the firstfruits of the gathering from the islands of the sea and that they were under the dominion of the Kingdom of God. The purpose of these remarks was to check the enthusiasm that stirred the colonists and made some of them desire to return to their island home. The talk had a profound effect upon the colonists; they unanimously gave up their desire to return at this time.24

A short time later President Cluff met with the First Presidency to discuss what the policy should be when any of the Hawaiians wished to return to Hawaiâi. The First Presidency stated that they should be free to return to their former home if they desired and added that they hoped the islanders would leave with good feelings and manifest an interest in the Lordâs work back in Hawaiâi. When the announcement was made that the Hawaiian Saints were free to come and go as they wished, some left Iosepa intending to enjoy âa trip to their native islands at the expense of their [former] Hawaiian government.â25 During the latter part of September, twenty-six Hawaiians had been brought into Salt Lake City, but after arriving in the city, ten changed their minds and decided to stay in Utah. The remaining group of sixteen was put together at considerable trouble to the Hawaiian government representatives. This caused Charles T. Wilder, Hawaiian Consul General in San Francisco, to say, âIn the future I would advise that the Government leave them [the Hawaiians in Utah] where they are, the fact is that they do not know what they do want, and as they are in need of nothing I consider that they are just as well off as they would be in Hawaiâi.â26 In December an article in the Deseret Evening News stated that âa few members of the colony accepted the [Hawaiian] government invitation to return to the Sandwich Islands. Several of these have written back, expressing the wish that they were here, and declaring their intention to return to the colony as soon as practicable.â27

From this time on, the Hawaiians felt more free to come and go as they wished. In 1898 the Hawaiian Islands became a territory of the United States, and thereafter its citizens could travel freely to the United States. During the years 1901 to 1917, when Thomas A. Waddoups was president of the Iosepa colony, a few more Hawaiians returned to the islands, but the number was small in comparison with the number who immigrated to the colony. Never during this time did the Hawaiian Territorial Government attempt to persuade them to return to the islands.28

Iosepa Achieves Success

The aim of the Iosepa Agricultural and Stock Company was to provide employment for the colonists, grow sufficient foodstuffs for the colony, and, if possible, produce cash crops to help meet the financial needs of the colony. Some of the necessities could not be raised on the land and had to be purchased. Cash was also needed to meet the yearly land payments. From the beginning, the colonists were successful in producing sufficient foodstuffs. In fact, at times they had reserves enough to last them for two or three years in the event of crop failure. However, the company did not begin to break even financially until after 1900. When they operated at a loss, Church finances made up the difference. During the 1890s, farmers and ranchers in the United States generally experienced financial difficulties, and those who bought livestock feed or boarded their animals at Iosepa had no money to pay their bill to the company. This compounded Iosepaâs problems. Gradually, as national conditions improved and the colonists learned proper farming and ranching procedures for the climate, the Iosepa Agricultural and Stock Company turned a profit. By 1911, Utah historian J. Cecil Alter could write:

Perhaps the most successful individual colonization proposition that has been attempted by the âMormonâ people in the United States, is the Hawaiian colony at Iosepa. . . .

There are 1,120 acres practically all in use and half as much more is being brought under the magic wand of the Hawaiian irrigator. . . .

The story of Iosepa is a story in itself. Suffice it to say that today the several hundred folks there have water in their houses just the same as we have in Salt Lake City, and a power plant will some time give them their electric lights. Their school and meetinghouses are as good as the best . . . and since they grow their own food and raise their own animals, they are better off than many farmers who have lived in this country all their lives. The âMormonâ people conceived the plan for them, and the Church made its perfection possible.

At a recent annual celebration . . . Lorenzo D. Creek, a government Indian official from Washington, who was studying the Indians in Tooele county at the time, rose before the great, Hawaiian, uniformed audience, after having been shown all over the place, and with much feeling said: âMy friends, if this is a sample of âMormonâ colonization work, the best thing the government of the United States could do, would be to assist them in every way possible.â29

As Alter mentioned, an example of the noteworthy improvements available in Iosepa by this time was their new water system. For the first ten or twelve years of the colonyâs history, culinary water was obtained from irrigation ditches that ran through the town. This was the usual practice in Utah towns during the first decades of their existence. Usually the water was dipped up and put in barrels early in the morning before sheep or cattle had a chance to drink from the ditches or pollute the water in any other way. The water was allowed to settle in the barrels for several hours before being used for drinking or cooking. In Iosepa, however, the main canal ran over very soft, powdery soil through which water could not be conveyed without difficulty and loss. This unsatisfactory system of obtaining culinary water was in use until about 1903, when Sister Waddoups contracted typhoid fever from impure water. The episode brought to the fore the immediate need for a better and more sanitary supply of water for culinary purposes.30 Soon thereafter, a tremendous water project was undertaken to divert the water from six or seven nearby mountain streams and several springs into huge cement-bottomed canals (fig. 3), gathering water from each source into one main pipe system. From that point, the water could be distributed for irrigation, culinary, and municipal purposes.31 The latter included installing a series of fire hydrants, a few of which survived until the late twentieth century. This $260,000 project, completed in 1908, was very successful in making water available for irrigation and providing each home with pure culinary water.32

Living conditions continued to improve during the next few years, and the yearly crop production and number of cattle fed increased. Will G. Farrell, president of the Iosepa Agricultural and Stock Company, was able to report in a 1915 Pioneer Day speech that in Iosepa:

A modern, well watered farm of over a thousand acres which furnishes the bulk of employment for the natives, and the pay roll averages more than $1,000 a month the year around. Everyone receives his pay every Saturday morning, as every Saturday afternoon is for recreation, leaving Sunday for reverence and rest. The cash sales of farm products last year amounted to $10,000, besides which feed was raised for 800 head of thoroughbred Shorthorn Durham cattle, which belong to the company. The cash sales of beef add another $10,000 to the income.33

The same newspaper article reported that James H. Wallis, state inspector of farm and town sanitation, visited Iosepa for three days as part of his regular duties. In a very earnest address, Wallis said that of the many towns he had inspected that year, âIosepa stood as a whole, the very highest in cleanliness, both in the streets and yards and in the interiors.â34 In addition, the Iosepa Agricultural and Stock Company offered prizes of $5, $15, and $50 for the properties most commended for cleanliness by Mr. Wallis. An elaborate judging system was worked out to have a fair evaluation.35

By the 1910s, Iosepa was successful in many ways. It was paying off financially. It sported clean and beautiful living areas, bedecked with flowers and fruit and shade trees. Iosepaâs population had gradually increased until over two hundred residents, young and old, called it home (fig. 4). All this represented a significant change from its beginnings more than twenty years before; when the original pioneers arrived, and for a number of years thereafter, they struggled with their new environment and a life alien to that which they had known previously. The redeeming factor for the Hawaiians was that they felt they were participating in the great gathering movement of their church and were near a temple wherein sacred ordinances could be performed for themselves and their dead ancestors. Their great sacrifices were made easier by the belief that they were obeying the will of God.

Life was also made easier by the love and support of Church leaders. Because of their missions to Hawaiâi, both George Q. Cannon and Joseph F. Smith spoke Hawaiian and were well acquainted with the customs and needs of Polynesians. A very special relationship existed between the First Presidency and the Church members at the Iosepa Branch. Unlike any other ward or branch, it did not work through stake, district, or mission leadership; instead, this branch reported directly to the First Presidency. The Churchâs historical files contain gentle and affectionate letters from members of the First Presidency counseling Iosepa colonists on personal matters and on interpersonal relationships, revealing the Presidencyâs concern for the Polynesian Saints. Throughout Iosepaâs existence members of the First Presidency regularly visited the branch.

Settlers Return to Hawaiâi

In the end it was the faith and devotion of the Hawaiian Saints that caused the dissolution of the colony. In 1915, President Joseph F. Smith, after returning from one of his frequent trips to the Hawaiian Islands, announced that the Church was going to build a temple in LÄâie. When the Hawaiians heard this, many resolved to return to the Islands. President Smith told Waddoups that if any of the Hawaiians wished to return to Hawaiâi it was agreeable with Church leaders for them to do so. Church funds would be used to pay transportation expenses for colonists who could not afford tickets.36

Soon a number were ready to leave. Their personal property was sold back to the company at a price arrived at by two disinterested persons. By summer 1917, virtually none of the Hawaiians were left to harvest the crops; Waddoups had to hire others to help him. That fall the ranch, including the townsite, was sold to the Deseret Live Stock company for $150,000.37 It remained, for many years, an integral part of their holdings. After Iosepa was sold, several of the buildings were moved to new locations while others were torn down and the materials reused. Traces of Iosepaâs uniqueness soon began to disappear, and in a few decades, people in Utah began to forget its existence.

Conclusion

Iosepa had been abandoned at its high point because the Hawaiian Saintsâ desire to return to their native land to help build the temple lured them away. A temple was being built for their people, and they wished to participate and do all they could toward building and sustaining it. They had participated in the gathering and had established a thriving colony in Utah, but now it was time to return to the islands. Those fortunate enough to be the descendants of these Polynesian pioneers in Utah should be proud of all that their ancestors endured and accomplished.

With the water turned off and its residents gone, the townsite of Iosepa soon returned to the desert it had been when the Hawaiians first arrived to make it âblossom as a roseâ (Isa. 35:1). The irrigated farmland was productive before the Hawaiians came, was productive while they were there, and reamins productive today. Recent decades have witnessed renewed interest in Iosepa. The descendants of its pioneer inhabitants and other interested parties have worked together to memorialize the site. The cemetery has been restored, a monument erected, and pavilions put up to shelter the hundreds who gather each year on Memorial Day weekend to pay homage and socialize. Once again the area resounds with music and activity, reminiscent of life there in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Visitors return home with a greater understanding of and appreciation for the Saints whose faith and determination made Iosepa a success.

The story of Iosepa is an intriguing but often misunderstood chapter in Mormon history. Some have cast it as something of a tragedy: innocent, trusting islander Saints accustomed to a tropical and oceanic lifestyle were lured, because of their religious faith, into the midst of the harsh Great Basin desert. Then because of their almost nonexistent financial resources, they were stuck in Utah for nearly three decades as farm and ranch hands under the direction of haole (Caucasian) taskmasters. The story that Dennis H. Atkin reconstructs from primary sources is quite different. Sensitivity and generosity, rather than severity and greediness, characterized the actions of colony leaders and Church officials. First Presidency members Joseph F. Smith, after whom the colony was named (Iosepa is Hawaiian for Joseph), and George Q. Cannon, who was one of the first LDS missionaries in Hawaiâi, manifested a marked affection for the Polynesian Saints.

Twice in twenty years (1985 and 1998), Atkin shared information from his pioneering 1958 masterâs thesis and subsequent studies on this topic with the Mormon Pacific Historical Society. Below is an edited version that weaves together portions of both presentations.

One of the most interesting colonies founded in the settling of the western part of the United States was the Polynesian settlement of Iosepa, the Hawaiian name for Joseph. This Utah community, located about seventy-five miles southwest of Salt Lake City, in Skull Valley, Tooele County, was established and fostered by the Church. It was founded in 1889, after much of the Great Basin had already been colonized, and disbanded in 1917, after virtually all of its several hundred inhabitants had returned to Hawaiâi to assist with the building of the recently announced temple in LÄâie. Even though Iosepaâs quarter-century life span was filled with challenges, colony descendants look back with fondness on that period and hold annual reunions to commemorate it.

Hawaiian Saints Gather to Utah

Even though LÄâie had been established as a gathering place for Hawaiian Church members in 1865, some of the early Hawaiian converts wanted to gather to Zion along with converts from other parts of the world. Church leaders, however, did not encourage them to come. On the contrary, Hawaiian converts were discouraged by Church leaders from immigrating to Utah for two main reasons: (1) the Hawaiian government prohibited such emigration, and (2) the climate extremes in Utah were much harsher than that of the Pacific. The summers were hotter, the winters were colder, and the air drier than in the islands. Also, more effort was required to obtain food and other necessities of life in Utah. In 1891 the First Presidency wrote to the Minister of Foreign Affairs in Hawaiâi about the Church leadersâ long-standing sensitivity to the obstacles involved in immigration:

We think it can be truthfully stated that there has been no encouragement given to the native Hawaiian people of our faith to migrate to this country. For many years many of them have been urging our Elders to arrange affairs so that they could come; but there has been a feeling of reluctance entertained by us concerning their coming as we know how great a trial it might prove to them to live in a climate and in surroundings such as ours, so different in almost every respect to their position in their native Islands.1

Although laws prohibiting Hawaiian citizens from leaving their island home were not changed for some time, exceptions were made and occasionally Hawaiian Church members were allowed to go to Utah. In 1873 two Hawaiian boys traveled to Utah with William King and George Nebeker when they returned in 1873 from their missionary service.2 Then in 1882, eight Hawaiians (four adults and four children) accompanied Harvey H. Cluff, who had been recently released as mission president, and Elder J. G. Knell as they returned to Salt Lake City. A newspaper article reporting their arrival further stated:

There is a law in the Hawaiian Kingdom which prohibits the natives from emigrating to other parts of the world, but in consequence of the government [of Hawaiâi] having proofs before them that the only disposition of the community of Latter-day Saints toward the people is to benefit them, exceptions are made in the cases of those desiring to come to Utah.3

Around 1884 the Hawaiian government revoked its law prohibiting citizens from emigrating, and the Hawaiians wishing to go to Utah found it much easier to do so. Generally they traveled with returning missionaries and settled in the old Nineteenth Ward around Warm Springs in the northwest part of Salt Lake City. This was a logical choice since it was the home ward of Matthew Noall, Frederick Beesley, George Nebeker, and other former Hawaiian missionaries who were friendly to and supportive of the Polynesian community.

By mid-1889 approximately seventy-five Hawaiian Church members had migrated to Salt Lake City (fig. 1). Generally speaking, Polynesian immigrants were not well assimilated by the Caucasian population in Salt Lake City. The islanders usually could obtain only low-paying, manual-labor jobs and they were often unemployed part of each year. Few purchased or built businesses of their own. The Polynesians found themselves in an environment quite foreign to anything they had ever experienced.4

It became apparent to Church leaders that the islanders needed to have a permanent location where they could obtain year-round employment and control more of their own social environment.5 A plan was approved by the First Presidency to purchase a large ranch on which the Polynesians could establish a colony after the order of a âcompany town,â a place where they could have their own community, build their own homes, have year-round employment, retain their cultural identity, and adapt at their own pace to the general cultural milieu that surrounded them. This community was christened Iosepa.

Saints Find a Home in Utah

In May 1889, a land selection committee was appointed that consisted of three men who had been active in missionary work in the islands. These three men, Harvey H. Cluff, William W. Cluff, and Fred A. Mitchell, went to the Hawaiians residing in the Warm Springs area and presented the plan to acquire land for a colony. The Hawaiians sanctioned the plan and promptly appointed three from their own group to work on the committee: J. W. Kauleinamoku, George Kamakaniau, and Napeha.6

Early in June, the six men on the selection committee set out on their assignment. During the following two weeks, they inspected numerous farms and ranches offered for sale in Utah, Weber, Cache, and Tooele Counties. Because of the farmland to population ratio, good farming land was at a premium. Most people who owned fertile farm land did not want to sell it, and many who did not own good land wanted to buy.

The committee visited the choice locations they did find and wrote a report that was submitted to the First Presidency. The report included a description of each location and an evaluation of its suitability for the colony. Of the available choices, the 1,920 acres of the John T. Rich property located in Skull Valley, Tooele County, was by far the most desirable. This ranch had ample water and fertile land, and it was reasonably priced. There was more land nearby that could be obtained from the government. As the committee wrote in its report, the property included

extensive summer and winter range; cheap and abundant supply of fencing; extensive water supply; building materials easy accessible; mild climate; a large crop nearly matured more than necessary to supply [the Saintsâ] wants for the coming year; teams, wagons, harnesses, farming implements, cows, chickens, pigs, everything needful for their support.7

The day after the committee submitted its report, the First Presidency met with them and by unanimous vote accepted the committeeâs recommendation to purchase land in Skull Valley.8 The First Presidency wrote to William King, then mission president in Hawaiâi, informing him of the decision and expressing hope that the Skull Valley colony would âprove a success and become a mother colony after which others may be patterned when the time comes for the gathering of the remnants of the House of Israel who accept the Gospel on the isles of the Pacific.â9 The colony was legally incorporated as the Iosepa Agricultural and Stock Company, and a date was set for the Hawaiian Saints to move to Iosepa. Church officials hoped that the Iosepa Agricultural and Stock Company would become a self-sustaining entity, but until that time it was expected that the Church, as an institution, would help the colony and the company financially.

Some people who have visited Iosepa have questioned why this location was selected for the colony. In the summer it is hot and dry. Very little vegetation grows without irrigation. Other valleys in Utah appeared more productive. Although the winter weather was milder in Tooele County than in the other places under consideration, the winters were cold, especially when compared with the Pacific islands. The first winter the islanders spent in Iosepa (1889â1890), the temperature was especially cold. Yet, in the context of the times, Skull Valley offered the best situation available. According to historian Leonard Arrington:

The prime economic problem of Mormon Country in the late 1870âs and 1880âs was overpopulation. In every valley, there were signs that the continued flow of immigration, and the natural increase in population had filled up the land. . . . Projects to increase the supply of irrigable land were sponsored by ward, stake, and general church organizations. Here and there, from Blackfoot, Idaho, in the north, to Mesa, Arizona, in the south; from Manassa, Colorado in the east, to Southern Nevada in the west, available patches of land were reclaimed from the desert and put in the way of raising grains, hay, and vegetables.10

Hawaiians Settle in Iosepa

Most of the Polynesian Saints living in Salt Lake City decided to join the new colony. On the evening of August 26, 1889, the prospective colonists enjoyed a farewell feast prepared by the Salt Lake Nineteenth and Twenty-second wards. The next morning forty-five or fifty Hawaiians traveled by train to Garfield Junction.11 The First Presidency assigned members of the Tooele Stake the task of transporting them from Garfield Junction to their new home by teams and wagons. Before departing, the Hawaiian Saints entertained the local members with a program of music and dance. The following day, on August 28, 1889, the islanders were taken to Iosepa, the place that was to be the home of Polynesian Mormons in Utah for the next twenty-eight years (map 1). Harvey Cluff, the president of the Iosepa Agricultural and Stock Company, proclaimed August 28 to be Hawaiian Pioneer Day. In the evening, a special meeting was held that consisted of songs, prayers, and speeches of thanksgiving. Every year thereafter, August 28 was celebrated in Iosepa just as other Utahns celebrated July 24, the anniversary of the first Mormons entering Salt Lake Valley.

So eager were the colonists to get things underway that a survey of the townsite was started that first day by Fred A. Mitchell, assisted by Francis M. Lyman Jr., and completed two days later. Each block in the townsite was divided into four corner lots. The lots contained sufficient space for a home, garden, and barn and corral facilities for livestock. As winter approached, the Iosepa colonizers settled down to the serious business of building their settlement and producing a livelihood in Skull Valley. The Iosepa Agricultural and Stock Company owned all the colonyâs land and livestock, except the lots that a few colonists had purchased and the teams or riding horses owned by individuals. In the beginning, a bookkeeping system was used in which the workers were credited with work performed and charged for goods purchased without the use of money. This system was used until after Thomas A. Waddoups became president of the colony in 1901. Shortly thereafter a scrip system was inaugurated, doing away with the laborious bookkeeping system. A Salt Lake City printing house printed a special series of paper scrip in 5Â¢, 10Â¢, 25Â¢, 50Â¢, and $1.00 denominations (fig. 2). This was used to pay the colonists for their labor, and, in turn, the colonists used it to pay for goods and services received. Occasionally, some colonists went to Salt Lake City to shop, and the company endeavored to have enough cash on hand to exchange for scrip at such times. Later, as the company became a paying venture, cash replaced scrip.14

Some of the Hawaiians were not happy in Iosepa. A few wanted to return to Salt Lake City; others longed for Hawaiâi. Homesickness afflicted many, especially when the weather was cold or hot or when they had difficulty earning enough money to purchase what they needed. Supplying day-to-day needs was harder in Utah than in Hawaiâi. Fishing skills were not helpful in Iosepa, fruit was not simply plucked from naturally growing trees, and more clothes were required. A few of the Hawaiians became disillusioned and wanted to return to the islands.

When news of the dissatisfaction of some of the Hawaiians in Utah got back to Hawaiian government officials, money was appropriated by the Hawaiian Legislature to help any who wished to return to the islands but could not afford tickets. Government officials and representatives even went to Utah to encourage the Hawaiians to return. Iosepa Achieves Success

The aim of the Iosepa Agricultural and Stock Company was to provide employment for the colonists, grow sufficient foodstuffs for the colony, and, if possible, produce cash crops to help meet the financial needs of the colony. Some of the necessities could not be raised on the land and had to be purchased. Cash was also needed to meet the yearly land payments. From the beginning, the colonists were successful in producing sufficient foodstuffs. In fact, at times they had reserves enough to last them for two or three years in the event of crop failure. However, the company did not begin to break even financially until after 1900. When they operated at a loss, Church finances made up the difference. During the 1890s, farmers and ranchers in the United States generally experienced financial difficulties, and those who bought livestock feed or boarded their animals at Iosepa had no money to pay their bill to the company. This compounded Iosepaâs problems. Gradually, as national conditions improved and the colonists learned proper farming and ranching procedures for the climate, the Iosepa Agricultural and Stock Company turned a profit. By 1911, Utah historian J. Cecil Alter could write:

Perhaps the most successful individual colonization proposition that has been attempted by the âMormonâ people in the United States, is the Hawaiian colony at Iosepa. . . .

There are 1,120 acres practically all in use and half as much more is being brought under the magic wand of the Hawaiian irrigator. . . .

The story of Iosepa is a story in itself. Suffice it to say that today the several hundred folks there have water in their houses just the same as we have in Salt Lake City, and a power plant will some time give them their electric lights. Their school and meetinghouses are as good as the best . . . and since they grow their own food and raise their own animals, they are better off than many farmers who have lived in this country all their lives. The âMormonâ people conceived the plan for them, and the Church made its perfection possible.

At a recent annual celebration . . . Lorenzo D. Creek, a government Indian official from Washington, who was studying the Indians in Tooele county at the time, rose before the great, Hawaiian, uniformed audience, after having been shown all over the place, and with much feeling said: âMy friends, if this is a sample of âMormonâ colonization work, the best thing the government of the United States could do, would be to assist them in every way possible.â29

As Alter mentioned, an example of the noteworthy improvements available in Iosepa by this time was their new water system. For the first ten or twelve years of the colonyâs history, culinary water was obtained from irrigation ditches that ran through the town. This was the usual practice in Utah towns during the first decades of their existence. Usually the water was dipped up and put in barrels early in the morning before sheep or cattle had a chance to drink from the ditches or pollute the water in any other way. The water was allowed to settle in the barrels for several hours before being used for drinking or cooking. In Iosepa, however, the main canal ran over very soft, powdery soil through which water could not be conveyed without difficulty and loss. This unsatisfactory system of obtaining culinary water was in use until about 1903, when Sister Waddoups contracted typhoid fever from impure water. The episode brought to the fore the immediate need for a better and more sanitary supply of water for culinary purposes.30 Soon thereafter, a tremendous water project was undertaken to divert the water from six or seven nearby mountain streams and several springs into huge cement-bottomed canals (fig. 3), gathering water from each source into one main pipe system. From that point, the water could be distributed for irrigation, culinary, and municipal purposes.31 The latter included installing a series of fire hydrants, a few of which survived until the late twentieth century. This $260,000 project, completed in 1908, was very successful in making water available for irrigation and providing each home with pure culinary water.32

A modern, well watered farm of over a thousand acres which furnishes the bulk of employment for the natives, and the pay roll averages more than $1,000 a month the year around. The cash sales of farm products last year amounted to $10,000, besides which feed was raised for 800 head of thoroughbred Shorthorn Durham cattle, which belong to the company. The cash sales of beef add another $10,000 to the income.33

The same newspaper article reported that James H. Wallis, state inspector of farm and town sanitation, visited Iosepa for three days as part of his regular duties. In a very earnest address, Wallis said that of the many towns he had inspected that year, âIosepa stood as a whole, the very highest in cleanliness, both in the streets and yards and in the interiors.â34 In addition, the Iosepa Agricultural and Stock Company offered prizes of $5, $15, and $50 for the properties most commended for cleanliness by Mr. Wallis. An elaborate judging system was worked out to have a fair evaluation.35

By the 1910s, Iosepa was successful in many ways. It was paying off financially. It sported clean and beautiful living areas, bedecked with flowers and fruit and shade trees. Iosepaâs population had gradually increased until over two hundred residents, young and old, called it home (fig. 4). All this represented a significant change from its beginnings more than twenty years before; when the original pioneers arrived, and for a number of years thereafter, they struggled with their new environment and a life alien to that which they had known previously. The redeeming factor for the Hawaiians was that they felt they were participating in the great gathering movement of their church and were near a temple wherein sacred ordinances could be performed for themselves and their dead ancestors. Their great sacrifices were made easier by the belief that they were obeying the will of God.

Life was also made easier by the love and support of Church leaders. Because of their missions to Hawaiâi, both George Q. Cannon and Joseph F. Smith spoke Hawaiian and were well acquainted with the customs and needs of Polynesians. A very special relationship existed between the First Presidency and the Church members at the Iosepa Branch. Unlike any other ward or branch, it did not work through stake, district, or mission leadership; instead, this branch reported directly to the First Presidency. The Churchâs historical files contain gentle and affectionate letters from members of the First Presidency counseling Iosepa colonists on personal matters and on interpersonal relationships, revealing the Presidencyâs concern for the Polynesian Saints. Throughout Iosepaâs existence members of the First Presidency regularly visited the branch.

Settlers Return to Hawaiâi

In the end it was the faith and devotion of the Hawaiian Saints that caused the dissolution of the colony. In 1915, President Joseph F. Smith, after returning from one of his frequent trips to the Hawaiian Islands, announced that the Church was going to build a temple in LÄâie. When the Hawaiians heard this, many resolved to return to the Islands. President Smith told Waddoups that if any of the Hawaiians wished to return to Hawaiâi it was agreeable with Church leaders for them to do so. Church funds would be used to pay transportation expenses for colonists who could not afford tickets.36

Soon a number were ready to leave. Their personal property was sold back to the company at a price arrived at by two disinterested persons. By summer 1917, virtually none of the Hawaiians were left to harvest the crops; Waddoups had to hire others to help him. That fall the ranch, including the townsite, was sold to the Deseret Live Stock company for $150,000.37 It remained, for many years, an integral part of their holdings. After Iosepa was sold, several of the buildings were moved to new locations while others were torn down and the materials reused. Traces of Iosepaâs uniqueness soon began to disappear, and in a few decades, people in Utah began to forget its existence.

Conclusion

Iosepa had been abandoned at its high point because the Hawaiian Saintsâ desire to return to their native land to help build the temple lured them away. A temple was being built for their people, and they wished to participate and do all they could toward building and sustaining it. They had participated in the gathering and had established a thriving colony in Utah, but now it was time to return to the islands. Those fortunate enough to be the descendants of these Polynesian pioneers in Utah should be proud of all that their ancestors endured and accomplished.

With the water turned off and its residents gone, the townsite of Iosepa soon returned to the desert it had been when the Hawaiians first arrived to make it âblossom as a roseâ (Isa. 35:1). The irrigated farmland was productive before the Hawaiians came, was productive while they were there, and reamins productive today. Recent decades have witnessed renewed interest in Iosepa. The descendants of its pioneer inhabitants and other interested parties have worked together to memorialize the site. The cemetery has been restored, a monument erected, and pavilions put up to shelter the hundreds who gather each year on Memorial Day weekend to pay homage and socialize. Once again the area resounds with music and activity, reminiscent of life there in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Visitors return home with a greater understanding of and appreciation for the Saints whose faith and determination made Iosepa a success.
