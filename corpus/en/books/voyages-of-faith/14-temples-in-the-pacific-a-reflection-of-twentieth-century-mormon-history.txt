In and of itself the construction of Latter-day Saint temples in the Pacific is significant. Yet this dramatic achievement takes on added meaning when it is viewed as a reflection of broader developments during the twentieth century. Throughout most of the nineteenth century, Latter-day Saint converts had gathered to Zion, flocking to the centers of Mormon colonization in North America. As that century drew to a close, however, the supply of land for settlement in the Mormon Corridor approached exhaustion, and the United States was plagued by economic depression. In this setting, and consistent with revelations given earlier through Joseph Smith (D&C 101:20â22; 115:17â18), Church leaders began discouraging the Saints from gathering to America. Instead, they instructed the faithful to stay and strengthen the Church in their own lands. President Lorenzo Snow, who served at the dawning of the twentieth century, stressed the Churchâs worldwide mission. Impressed with the need to take the gospel to all the world, he appointed Apostle Heber J. Grant to open a mission in Japan and also looked forward to carrying the gospel message to places such as Russia and Latin America.

David O. McKay and the New Zealand Temple

During the 1950s, David O. McKay presided over an accelerated internationalization of the Church. Once again this trend was reflected in temple building. Temple and Genealogy Work

For centuries the Pacific Islanders had kept genealogies in various forms, such as wooden carvings in New Zealand or oral traditions that were memorized and passed down from generation to generation. During the early years of the twentieth century, Abraham Fornander, a Hawaiian historian, convinced the people to record their oral legends. The results of his work were published in several large volumes. âFor twenty years,â commented Susa Young Gates, âthis Hawaiian genealogist and antiquarian has been at work on the preparation of these volumes; and now, with the completion . . . of the Hawaiian Temple, comes the publication of this master work for the people of that land. Pacific Saints, as groups and individuals, also felt the responsibility to collect records to form the basis for vicarious temple service. In French Polynesia, for example, when the government archives building burned, vital records were charred and soaked. Relief Society sisters salvaged the precious documents, separated the sheets, and spread them out on the floor to dry. The records were subsequently alphabetized and microfilmed. Now the Family History Center on the Tahiti Temple grounds has over 1,400 rolls that are frequently consulted for both legal and genealogical purposes. As high as 40 percent of the names used at the temple were submitted locally. Elder Gordon B. Hinckley recalled a faithful sister who brought her precious genealogical record to the New Zealand Temple:

A little woman came with her husband. They were evidently of extremely modest circumstances. She held in her hand a straw basket, and in the basket a Book of Remembrance, the record of her people, laboriously gathered. To the temple she brought that record, out of the love born of the gospel, that those of her people who had gone before might enjoy the rich blessings of which she bore testimony.44

Temple and Genealogy Work

For centuries the Pacific Islanders had kept genealogies in various forms, such as wooden carvings in New Zealand or oral traditions that were memorized and passed down from generation to generation. During the early years of the twentieth century, Abraham Fornander, a Hawaiian historian, convinced the people to record their oral legends. He translated them and found that some of the genealogies extended back to the time of Christ. The results of his work were published in several large volumes. âFor twenty years,â commented Susa Young Gates, âthis Hawaiian genealogist and antiquarian has been at work on the preparation of these volumes; and now, with the completion . . . of the Hawaiian Temple, comes the publication of this master work for the people of that land. Pacific Saints, as groups and individuals, also felt the responsibility to collect records to form the basis for vicarious temple service. In French Polynesia, for example, when the government archives building burned, vital records were charred and soaked. Relief Society sisters salvaged the precious documents, separated the sheets, and spread them out on the floor to dry. The records were subsequently alphabetized and microfilmed. Now the Family History Center on the Tahiti Temple grounds has over 1,400 rolls that are frequently consulted for both legal and genealogical purposes. As high as 40 percent of the names used at the temple were submitted locally. Elder Gordon B. To the temple she brought that record, out of the love born of the gospel, that those of her people who had gone before might enjoy the rich blessings of which she bore testimony.44
