Today I would like to suggest some guidelines for those of you who are involved in writing the history of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints in the Pacific. As the Church becomes more international, it will become increasingly important to write the history of Latter-day Saints in their homelands. By reconstructing these peopleâs lives, we give their heirs a sense of their LDS heritage as well as provide real models for their own lives, models with whom they can identify.

Reasons to Study Mormon History

Before I offer suggestions about writing local histories, I suppose I should start out by explaining why the study of Latter-day Saint history in general is important. Let me suggest four reasons. First, the study and writing of our history will help us better understand the Lord. For example, it will tell us more about the settings and circumstances of his revelations. It will provide examples of how the Lord works through men and women and what he expects of us. It will tell us more about the Saintsâ relationship with the Lord during the progress and problems of the Church and of its membership, so we can learn how and why changes have taken place.

Second, the study and writing of history will help strengthen Church unity by building in all of us a sense of community and pride in our past. It will give us a sense of who we are, where our cultural and spiritual roots lie, as well as where our biological and social roots originate. It will give us a sense of appreciation for our Latter-day Saint ancestors. Studying Church history is like studying the scripturesâit gives us a society against which to compare or contrast our own. We can compare ourselves with members of other religious or social cultures, but it is not the same as looking at people who have lived with the restored gospel. The Jews were a covenant people, and they made mistakes. The Nephites and the Lamanites were a covenant people, and they made mistakes. Our Latter-day Saint ancestors were a covenant people, and they made mistakes. We have the advantage of being a covenant people who follow all these cultures, and we can learn from the way they honored their covenants and from their failures. This will help us see our problems in perspective and remind us to be humble about our own achievements.

Third, studying history helps give us a knowledge of the past so that we can be informed when discussing our religion with others. It will give us accurate information to counter distorted histories and misunderstandings about the Church; it will provide accurate information to counter the arguments of apostates and others who border on apostasy. In short, it will help us be informed as we discuss the Restoration with those not of our faith.

And fourth, we study and write history because the Lord commands it. On April 6, 1830, the very day the Church was organized in this dispensation, the Lord commanded the Prophet Joseph Smith to arrange for the keeping of a record of the Churchâs history (D&C 21:1). Joseph appointed Oliver Cowdery to do this, and thus Oliver served as the first Church historian and recorder. Eleven months later, on March 8, 1831, John Whitmer was commanded by revelation to âwrite and keep a regular historyâ of the Church (D&C 47:1). In a later revelation, Whitmer was instructed to âcontinue in writing and making a history of all the important things which he shall observe and know concerning my churchâ (D&C 69:3). Other revelations attest to the Lordâs insistence that the Churchâs documents be preserved and that histories be written. With the encouragement of the prophets, the Church has fully complied with these commandments. From Oliver Cowderyâs first history (later published in the Latter Day Saintsâ Messenger and Advocate), to the most recent works of the General Authorities, the Historical Department of the Church, and others, Latter-day Saint historians have prepared and published histories of events and personalities from each stage of the Churchâs development that are important to the Church and its members.

Added to this record of following the Lordâs admonitions about keeping the Churchâs history, President Spencer W. Kimball has counseled us to keep our personal and family histories. These instructions apply to all members, in whatever part of the world they reside and under whatever government they make their living. Church and family history is a record of our brotherhood and sisterhood in Jesus Christâa history that brings us home to an understanding of the Lordâs kingdom and how we fit into it.

The Value of Local Histories

When the Historical Department of the Church was organized in 1972, we looked over our written history and identified areas that had been neglected and that needed further attention. We realized very quickly that we should give encouragement to studies of ethnic groups and their experiences in the Church; studies of Latter-day Saints in their local settings; studies of ordinary men, women, and family groups; studies of local leaders; and studies of the workings of Church programs at the local level.

Now I am going to suggest something that may seem to be hereticalâsomething that may seem ridiculous. I am going to suggest that it is more important to study and write local history than it is to study and write general history. What I am suggesting is that it is more important for you to do Hawaiian history than general American history, New Zealand history rather than history of the British Commonwealth, and so on. This is true of Church history as well. It is more important to do stake and ward history than general Church history. It is more important to do the history of your family than the history of the family in general. As I say, this seems ridiculous, but I firmly believe that one who concentrates on Hawaiâiâs history can make more progress toward understanding the aspirations, experiences, and achievements of mankind than one who has elected to study the history of the United States.

Only at the local level can the historian come to grips with reality in its most elemental forms. Focusing on the local allows the historian more intimate knowledge than studying the general. Too much history has been written from a detached perspective, from the important documents, from the ivory towers as it were. History needs to come from the grassroots, from where the people live. Many of the significant administrative steps the Church has taken had their beginning at the grassroots level. Local innovations and suggestions were brought to the attention of Church leaders who then felt inspired to use them as the basis for Churchwide programs such as the welfare program, the Primary, the Young Womenâs program, the Relief Society, and so on. While many Church members have felt that there is little room for individual initiative in a Church directed by God and with prophets at its head, positive achievements have taken place when Church members did not wait to be âcommand[ed] in all thingsâ but did âmany things of their own free willâ (D&C 58:26â29).

As the Church grows, the saga of the localityâof the ward, the stake, the region, and the individual Latter-day Saintâtakes on greater significance. Traditionally, our general Church history is too abstract and too impersonal. When history comes from the grassroots, from where the people live and worship, the individual can identify personally with the general Church experience.

Suggestions for Church Historians

As you go forward with your work, let me offer a number of suggestions that might be helpful in compiling and writing the history of the Church in the Pacific. First, give due importance to the work of the sisters. Letâs not have histories that give lists of bishops but omit lists of Relief Society presidents. Letâs include descriptions of the activities of the womenâs organizations as well as those of the priesthood. It is incorrect to start out with the notion that since men hold all the important policy-making positions, they are the ones who determine the course of events. Although the priesthood holders have certain key leadership offices, the brethren are not exclusively responsible for everything that happens in the Church. We must avoid a male interpretation of Mormon history. Those who spend a substantial amount of time going through the materials in the Church Archives find themselves gaining a new appreciation of the important and indispensable role of women in Church historyânot to mention gaining new insights from viewing Church history through the eyes of women. The historical studies of what Latter-day Saint women have done thus far reveal them to be strong individuals with tremendous varieties of temperaments, talents, and sensitivities. Further biographical studies of these women will help our sisters in charting their own lives without feeling constrained to mold themselves into existing stereotypes. Real models should be more useful than simply ideals.

Second, in our writing we should give due emphasis to the Churchâs intellectual, social, and cultural accomplishments. There is a tendency for us to remember the tangible, the material, and the visible simply because we can see them and because they have had greater survival value. We have tended to measure the accomplishments of earlier members by such durable achievements as the construction of canals and dams, temples and meetinghouses, homes and cooperative stores. But our forebears also made contributions in thought, in poetry, in music, in games and dancing and recreation, in human relations, and in education. These contributions are more difficult to discover and to trace but they are nevertheless there, and we should give them sufficient attention.

Third, in our writing we must acknowledge that not every program and organization proceeded smoothly. This is illustrated by Lance D. Chaseâs talk on the Hawaiian Mission crisis of 1874 (included in this volume). Our histories should make us aware of some of the problems, obstacles, objections, and difficulties Church members have faced. It would be especially instructive to know the particular problems of applying Church procedures and programs in the Pacific because of the differing cultural and social backgrounds among the people.

Fourth, we should give due attention to the so-called âordinaryâ members of the Church. An ideal example of this is Carl Fonoimoanaâs paper on his grandfather Opapo (included in this volume). We should be concerned with both the obscure and the famous, the deacons as well as the General Authorities. Actually, one may get a more accurate reflection of the impact of the gospel on the lives of members by studying the humble, the unambitious, or the devoted ward worker. The âordinaryâ member provides a richer field for understanding the functioning of the Church in the lives of people. The unexpected variety of people, the complexity and the uncoached elements of their character, demonstrate the richness of the gospel plan and the manner in which it can serve all men and women.

Fifth, we need to recognize the complexity of people, especially in biographical and family histories. We get a mistaken view of humanity by watching certain television shows. A half-hour show alone, or even an hour-long show, cannot develop a full-rounded personality in that time; the writer can exploit only one facet of a personâs character. And so he or she presents one person as compassionate, another as cruel, still another with the single motive of wanting more money, and a fourth who wants to take to bed every pretty woman he meets. But people are not that simple. The person who finds it hard to resist an opportunity to take advantage of another may be hardworking and kind to the unfortunate. The person who cannot resist an opportunity to make money may love his or her family, may be generous, and may have courage. A whole series of qualitiesâgood and badâmay exist in the same person. And sometimes these qualities are contradictory. Historians are obligated to give some indication of this diversity, this complexity, this contradictory nature of historical persons. I am not suggesting anything that might serve to malign beloved ancestors or leaders. It is, of course, distressing to think that the composer of a beautiful song was less than a completely beautiful person, or that the author of a lovely poem may have been stingy and small. I am merely suggesting that we acknowledge that everybody has weaknesses. If we are conscious that the people we respect have faults as well as achievements, we may come to believe that personal achievements may be possible even with our own defects.

My sixth point is that we should tell a straightforward story, not bogging down the narrative with attempts to moralize. Latter-day Saint writers have a tendency to moralize. Maybe we got into that habit by having to give talks in church or perhaps by hearing the constant moralizing of parents, seminary teachers, and missionaries. The secret of good historical writing is to stick to the point and to cut text whenever you can. There is a tendency for us, especially those who are new at history, to look upon our writing as something of a miracle, to be amazed that we are able to put words on paper at all. When they are on paper, out of our own brain if not straight from heaven, we look upon them as sacred. We cannot bear to sacrifice one of them. The best historians, having cultivated the ability to write, have learned to cut with fortitude. Each of you, as you write, will hit upon a quotation, a thought, that seems so exceptional that to cut it is worse than having a tooth extracted. It is then that you must accept the advice of another reader that you respect. If editorializing takes us away from the story we are trying to tell, it should be cut.

Finally, I hope you will, through your interviews and writing, convey the lovable spirit of Pacific peoples: their faith, their sense of humor, their playfulness, their physical prowess, their love of nature, their generosity, their practicality, and their happy disposition. Caucasian missionaries who return from this area tell of this special spiritâand speak of it longingly, as if one cannot find it duplicated in the continental states. We need not just rely on the reports of the so-called white missionaries, but the stories, the tales, the characterizations of the Pacific peoples themselvesâillustrations of this spirit from their own lives. For this purpose we need to interview bishops, Relief Society presidents, Sunday School teachers, and others. Relatively speaking, we do not have much in the Church Archives reflective of the Pacific perspective, either in past records or current ones.

Actually, one may get a more accurate reflection of the impact of the gospel on the lives of members by studying the humble, the unambitious, or the devoted ward worker. The âordinaryâ member provides a richer field for understanding the functioning of the Church in the lives of people. The unexpected variety of people, the complexity and the uncoached elements of their character, demonstrate the richness of the gospel plan and the manner in which it can serve all men and women.

Fifth, we need to recognize the complexity of people, especially in biographical and family histories. We get a mistaken view of humanity by watching certain television shows. A half-hour show alone, or even an hour-long show, cannot develop a full-rounded personality in that time; the writer can exploit only one facet of a personâs character. And so he or she presents one person as compassionate, another as cruel, still another with the single motive of wanting more money, and a fourth who wants to take to bed every pretty woman he meets. But people are not that simple. The person who finds it hard to resist an opportunity to take advantage of another may be hardworking and kind to the unfortunate. The person who cannot resist an opportunity to make money may love his or her family, may be generous, and may have courage. A whole series of qualitiesâgood and badâmay exist in the same person. And sometimes these qualities are contradictory. Historians are obligated to give some indication of this diversity, this complexity, this contradictory nature of historical persons. I am not suggesting anything that might serve to malign beloved ancestors or leaders. It is, of course, distressing to think that the composer of a beautiful song was less than a completely beautiful person, or that the author of a lovely poem may have been stingy and small. I am merely suggesting that we acknowledge that everybody has weaknesses. If we are conscious that the people we respect have faults as well as achievements, we may come to believe that personal achievements may be possible even with our own defects.

My sixth point is that we should tell a straightforward story, not bogging down the narrative with attempts to moralize. Latter-day Saint writers have a tendency to moralize. Maybe we got into that habit by having to give talks in church or perhaps by hearing the constant moralizing of parents, seminary teachers, and missionaries. The secret of good historical writing is to stick to the point and to cut text whenever you can. There is a tendency for us, especially those who are new at history, to look upon our writing as something of a miracle, to be amazed that we are able to put words on paper at all. When they are on paper, out of our own brain if not straight from heaven, we look upon them as sacred. We cannot bear to sacrifice one of them. The best historians, having cultivated the ability to write, have learned to cut with fortitude. Each of you, as you write, will hit upon a quotation, a thought, that seems so exceptional that to cut it is worse than having a tooth extracted. It is then that you must accept the advice of another reader that you respect. If editorializing takes us away from the story we are trying to tell, it should be cut.

Finally, I hope you will, through your interviews and writing, convey the lovable spirit of Pacific peoples: their faith, their sense of humor, their playfulness, their physical prowess, their love of nature, their generosity, their practicality, and their happy disposition. Caucasian missionaries who return from this area tell of this special spiritâand speak of it longingly, as if one cannot find it duplicated in the continental states. We need not just rely on the reports of the so-called white missionaries, but the stories, the tales, the characterizations of the Pacific peoples themselvesâillustrations of this spirit from their own lives. For this purpose we need to interview bishops, Relief Society presidents, Sunday School teachers, and others. Relatively speaking, we do not have much in the Church Archives reflective of the Pacific perspective, either in past records or current ones.

Conclusion

I think of Solomona Piâipiâilani (fig. 1). We donât know exactly when he was bornâsometime around 1800. This brother first received the gospel from George Q. Cannon in 1851 and was soon afterward ordained an elder. He was a fluent speaker in his native Hawaiian tongue, a close and careful reasoner, a thorough student of the scriptures, and a conscientious man. He was among those who gathered to PÄlÄwai, on the island of LÄnaâi, in 1854. When LÄâie was purchased in 1865 and established as a gathering place for the Saints, he was among the first to gather and labor on the plantation there. In 1879, his wife having died, he decided to emigrate to Utah, and he took his granddaughter along. He acquired a home in the Salt Lake Nineteenth Ward and made a modest living. He went to the Endowment House to make his temple covenants and was one of the first Hawaiians to receive his endowments. He had a thorough knowledge of the Bible and the Book of Mormon, and he made regular trips to the temple in Logan, Utah, to do temple work on behalf of his kindred dead. Finally, around the ripe old age of ninety-one, he died, still in Iosepa. At his funeral, President Joseph F. There must be dozens of others about whom we could say similar things. One sign of the vitality of the restored gospel is its capacity to produce strong peopleâpeople of faith and commitment and talent. I think of Mele Vea (also known as Mary Ashley), a native Tongan who later lived in Fiji. She was baptized a member at age fourteen and became a woman of tenacious faith, a pioneer of the Church both in Tonga and Fiji. I think of Helen Sekaquaptewa, the great Hopi woman whose life is eloquently told in Me and Mine;1 Faith Okawa Watabe, an American-born Japanese woman whose marriage linked her to the âpioneerâ Watabe family of the Churchâs first real expansion into Japan; Lucile Bankhead, the faithful black member whose long wait for temple blessings was finally rewarded by the glorious announcement of June 9, 1978. Finally, there was Jonatana H. Napela, a Hawaiian judge on Maui, who met George Q. Cannon in 1851 and was frequently mentioned in his diary. Napela was baptized and ordained an elder, was a fine speaker and reasoner, and was a person of standing and influence in the community. He was also a charming and intelligent man who helped the ministry in many ways, assisted with translating the Book of Mormon, healed the sick, and in other ways showed the spirit of Jesus.2

Let me close by saying to those who are interested in studying Mormonism in the Pacific that we wish you well in all your endeavors to write history. We offer also our facilities to help you to the extent that we can. What you are proposing to do is important to you, to your wards and stakes and regions, and to the Church. May the Lord bless you in your efforts and all of us who are engaged in similar endeavors.
