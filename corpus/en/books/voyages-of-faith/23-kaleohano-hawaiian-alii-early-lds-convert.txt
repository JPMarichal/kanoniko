The Hawaiian monarchy was formed when Kamehameha I, paramount chief of the âbig islandâ of Hawaiâi, united the Hawaiian Islands by conquest in 1810. Eighty-three years later, the monarchy ended when Queen Liliâuokalani (fig. 1) was overthrown in 1893. During this span of time, family life in Hawaiâi underwent significant changes as it was transformed from the native âohana (family) to the Euro-American, Christian pattern. The frequency of terms and phrases referring to family in the Hawaiian language attests to the historic significance of the âohana among the islanders.1 Economic shifts in both land systems and trade combined with diseases and social changes introduced by Christian missionaries caused the traditional Hawaiian family to undergo this drastic transformation. By the late 1840s, pressure from haoles (Caucasians) had also brought an end to the old land system. That system, while feudal in nature and providing only temporary land tenure, was well suited for the island situation where land was limited. Under the new system of individual ownership, land became a commodity to be bought and sold. The use and access rights so necessary for life in the old way ended, and eventually the Hawaiians were replaced as landowners by those who could afford to purchase or otherwise acquire this commodity.2 The âohana was now almost completely broken up since its operation had depended so much on free access to land. Newly married couples might no longer settle in the same ahupuaâa (land division) as the rest of the family. The old cooperative, mutually supportive interaction among family members was more difficult. The Hawaiian monarchy was formed when Kamehameha I, paramount chief of the âbig islandâ of Hawaiâi, united the Hawaiian Islands by conquest in 1810. Eighty-three years later, the monarchy ended when Queen Liliâuokalani (fig. 1) was overthrown in 1893. During this span of time, family life in Hawaiâi underwent significant changes as it was transformed from the native âohana (family) to the Euro-American, Christian pattern. The frequency of terms and phrases referring to family in the Hawaiian language attests to the historic significance of the âohana among the islanders.1 Economic shifts in both land systems and trade combined with diseases and social changes introduced by Christian missionaries caused the traditional Hawaiian family to undergo this drastic transformation. By the late 1840s, pressure from haoles (Caucasians) had also brought an end to the old land system. That system, while feudal in nature and providing only temporary land tenure, was well suited for the island situation where land was limited. Under the new system of individual ownership, land became a commodity to be bought and sold. The use and access rights so necessary for life in the old way ended, and eventually the Hawaiians were replaced as landowners by those who could afford to purchase or otherwise acquire this commodity.2 The âohana was now almost completely broken up since its operation had depended so much on free access to land. Newly married couples might no longer settle in the same ahupuaâa (land division) as the rest of the family. The old cooperative, mutually supportive interaction among family members was more difficult. Moreover, fewer children were born, and foreign diseases wiped out the population by the hundreds and thousands.
