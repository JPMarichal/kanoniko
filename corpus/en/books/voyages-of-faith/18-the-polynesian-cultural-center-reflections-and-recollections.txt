Several prior institutions and activities paved the way for the PCC. Foremost was the successful monthly hukilau (fishing with a seine) operated by the local LÄâie wards. The Critics

Though sentiment has been overwhelmingly positive, the PCC has had its share of criticism. The predominant concern in the early years was the welfare of the students, because it was feared that in focusing on entertainment skills they might be diverted to seek employment in the tourist industry of WaikÄ«kÄ« and abandon their educations. Some critics felt that the Center kept Polynesians âin their placeâ and exploited them for its purposes. It was feared that the marketing of the studentsâ natural social ability and cultural skills would change them from Polynesians in the heart to Polynesians âby design only.â

Fears were expressed concerning the distance of LÄâie from Honolulu, the ability of little LÄâie and LÄâieans to rise to the challenge of sustaining a long-term, entertainment-based cultural village, and the possibility of no tourist interest in a cultural site. Many Church members became very vocal with their opinions. They felt the Church had no business in a commercial venture; they supposed the temple would play second fiddle to the PCC; they felt that the Center would be a detriment to the spirituality of the LÄâie Saints. They worried about the impact of coffee drinking, smoking, and non-Church dress standards on the students and community. Some local Hawaiians feared that the PCC might bring an increase in population to the small windward community; they expressed a wariness of âinvasionâ and being crowded out. Lastly, for many years there was a sentiment in the community that the PCC was something that had been railroaded through without the knowledge or input of the local people who would be intimately involved.

Vernon Hardisty came to the Center in August 1968 and left in March 1975. He was a retired lieutenant colonel with a strong background in planning military operations. Hardisty began the expansion of the Center with new construction on the original orientation building, which later became the Gateway Restaurant and a new administration building. Cravens served until 1982. His first year fulfilled some of these fears. In restructuring his new management team, he dismissed over a dozen employees. On the other hand, Cravens had dreams for the Polynesians, and he tried to fulfill them during his seven years of leadership. He wanted Polynesians to live up to their strong faith and spirituality. He wanted both the tourists and the locals to see a Polynesian life beyond weaving mats and climbing coconut trees. He wanted Polynesians to be integrated into a gospel lifestyle rather than just a cultural one. Before Cravensâs tenure, the Board of Directors essentially managed the Center. As director, Bill Cravens presented to Pacific Islanders the reality of a Polynesian corporate leader. Throughout its history, the Center has been led by visionary men with a range of skills and talents who brought their particular expertise to the job at the times when it was needed. Conclusion

The PCC has achieved a number of impressive results. First, it has helped preserve the material culture and traditions of Polynesia. Many college students have come from parts of the Pacific where the way of life is changing, and in some cases disappearing, in the face of modern technology and ideas. Second, the Center has been a great missionary tool, stimulating many to investigate the Church. It has also produced much goodwill for the Church among governments worldwide and provided fertile ground for cultivating international friendships. Third, the PCC has contributed significantly to the local community by providing full-time employment for adults, thus reducing the need to commute an hour or two each way to Honolulu for work. Fourth, the PCC has functioned as an educational center for tourists, for the workers themselves, for community families, and for academic researchers. The Center has become a place where Polynesia can be studied and its culture perpetuated.

The comments of Edward Clissold, long-time supporter of the Center and original member of the Executive Board, provide an appropriate conclusion:

I have nothing but commendation for the people that are in charge now and what they are doing. I think if I were in charge, Iâd probably do some things a little differently, but I canât be critical because I wouldnât go back to the old ways, the old dreams, and the old plans. These (ways, dreams, plans) are so much more forward-looking and greater than the ones we had, that I can do nothing but support them.32

For those of us who have had a long association with the Polynesian Cultural Center and who put aside passing dissatisfactions, it is easy to feel a genuine affection for the âgood old PCC.â This affection may focus on a person, a particular activity, or a special experience. However produced, it is real and lasting. The PCC is like family, and like family, it will always be a part of us.

The Forerunners

Several prior institutions and activities paved the way for the PCC. Foremost was the successful monthly hukilau (fishing with a seine) operated by the local LÄâie wards. Publicized throughout the islands, the event was highly recommended by the Hawaiâi Visitors Bureau. It included the actual hukilau, a lÅ«âau meal, and entertainment on the beach. The Hawaiâi Temple certainly had an impact on the PCCâs history. It was a major tourist attraction for around-the-island bus tours. Highlighting the songs and dances of Polynesia, the show had previously been performed in Honolulu and had received very favorable reviews.

A final element in the background of the PCC was the CCH. Once the college was built, the pressure was on to ensure its continued existence. Financial means had to be provided to help students complete their college educations. With very few job opportunities in LÄâie and on Oâahuâs north shore, the proposed PCC would become the primary employer of CCH students and would enable them to meet their financial needs. As part of CCH, the institute was charged with exploring the idea of having the local community build and operate a series of Polynesian villages. The instituteâs involvement, however, was primarily academic, and the villages were intended to be used for cultural research.

The Critics

Though sentiment has been overwhelmingly positive, the PCC has had its share of criticism. The predominant concern in the early years was the welfare of the students, because it was feared that in focusing on entertainment skills they might be diverted to seek employment in the tourist industry of WaikÄ«kÄ« and abandon their educations. Some critics felt that the Center kept Polynesians âin their placeâ and exploited them for its purposes. Many Church members became very vocal with their opinions. They felt the Church had no business in a commercial venture; they supposed the temple would play second fiddle to the PCC; they felt that the Center would be a detriment to the spirituality of the LÄâie Saints. They worried about the impact of coffee drinking, smoking, and non-Church dress standards on the students and community. Some were confused about where the membersâ Church obligations ended and the cultural and economic needs began. There were also those who loved the old hukilaus and wished for their continuance. âWhy spend money on a new thing?â they asked, âWhy not improve something that already works?â

Some local Hawaiians feared that the PCC might bring an increase in population to the small windward community; they expressed a wariness of âinvasionâ and being crowded out. In addition, during most of the PCCâs history, there have been strong community perceptions of a lack of long-term career opportunities available to Polynesians or locals at the top levels of the PCC management because from its establishment these positions have been filled predominantly by mainland Caucasians. Lastly, for many years there was a sentiment in the community that the PCC was something that had been railroaded through without the knowledge or input of the local people who would be intimately involved.

Vernon Hardisty came to the Center in August 1968 and left in March 1975. He was a retired lieutenant colonel with a strong background in planning military operations. Hardisty began the expansion of the Center with new construction on the original orientation building, which later became the Gateway Restaurant and a new administration building. Cravens served until 1982. His first year fulfilled some of these fears. In restructuring his new management team, he dismissed over a dozen employees. Throughout its history, the Center has been led by visionary men with a range of skills and talents who brought their particular expertise to the job at the times when it was needed. Conclusion

The PCC has achieved a number of impressive results. First, it has helped preserve the material culture and traditions of Polynesia. Many college students have come from parts of the Pacific where the way of life is changing, and in some cases disappearing, in the face of modern technology and ideas. Second, the Center has been a great missionary tool, stimulating many to investigate the Church. It has also produced much goodwill for the Church among governments worldwide and provided fertile ground for cultivating international friendships. Third, the PCC has contributed significantly to the local community by providing full-time employment for adults, thus reducing the need to commute an hour or two each way to Honolulu for work. Fourth, the PCC has functioned as an educational center for tourists, for the workers themselves, for community families, and for academic researchers. The Center has become a place where Polynesia can be studied and its culture perpetuated.

The comments of Edward Clissold, long-time supporter of the Center and original member of the Executive Board, provide an appropriate conclusion:

I have nothing but commendation for the people that are in charge now and what they are doing. I think if I were in charge, Iâd probably do some things a little differently, but I canât be critical because I wouldnât go back to the old ways, the old dreams, and the old plans. These (ways, dreams, plans) are so much more forward-looking and greater than the ones we had, that I can do nothing but support them.32

For those of us who have had a long association with the Polynesian Cultural Center and who put aside passing dissatisfactions, it is easy to feel a genuine affection for the âgood old PCC.â This affection may focus on a person, a particular activity, or a special experience. However produced, it is real and lasting. The PCC is like family, and like family, it will always be a part of us.
