Having lived for many years in China, Pulitzer Prize-winning author Pearl Buck had ample opportunity to observe closely the attitudes and behavior of Christian missionaries serving far from home among people they too often misunderstood and failed to appreciate. âI have seen the missionary narrow, uncharitable, unappreciative, [and] ignorant,â she wrote.

Conclusion

After Cluff reached Salt Lake City in July 1874, he and George Nebeker met, by appointment, with the General Authorities to discuss the situation in the Hawaiian Mission. The site selected was Iosepa in Skull Valley, west of Salt Lake City. When the Iosepa site was ready for settlement, Cluff and his family moved to the town.

Most of the story presented here was taken from Cluffâs and Mitchellâs point of view.29 That of the Kahana community itself has yet to be told. Choirs from Kahana frequently sang at the semiannual conferences in LÄâie, and the two Mormon communities remained in close and frequent contact. Clearly, relationships did not suffer for long.

Having lived for many years in China, Pulitzer Prize-winning author Pearl Buck had ample opportunity to observe closely the attitudes and behavior of Christian missionaries serving far from home among people they too often misunderstood and failed to appreciate. âI have seen the missionary narrow, uncharitable, unappreciative, [and] ignorant,â she wrote.

Unfortunately, our own Latter-day Saint missionaries have sometimes acted as offensively as those described by Ms. Buck. The overzealous and intolerant attitude of Mission President Frederick Augustus Herman Frank Mitchell (fig. 1) provoked in 1874 what has been called the âmost serious affair that has transpired in the Hawaiian mission save that of Walter Gibson.â2 Known as the âKahana incident,â this event led to the creation of a separate gathering place for Mormons at Kahana, caused a serious but temporary rift among the Saints, and resulted in the early release of the mission-plantation president. Fortunately, the lasting effects have been negligible; only a tiny chapel and a few moldering gravestones remain at Kahana to remind us of a once-thriving Mormon community.3 I have no desire to grind any axes or reopen any wounds, but I hope that my research into the practices, personalities, and conditions that led to this break-off movement will increase our understanding of what happened in this chapter of Hawaiian Mission history.

Conclusion
