Hyrum L. Andrus, Anticipations of the Civil War in Mormon Thought ( 1966)

Warnings to Flee From the Wrath to Come

Awareness of coming civil war figured largely in the westward movements of the Mormons. They were to "declare by word and by flight that desolation shall come upon the wicked." [^1] Early in 1831, two revelations warned them that warfare would come to their "own lands" and commanded that even then they should gather "out from the eastern lands...into the western countries." [^2] With a note of sarcasm, Rev. John A. Clark reported that converts in New York and New England "would forthwith place their all into a wagon, and wend their way to the 'promised land,' in order, as they supposed, to escape the judgments of Heaven, which were soon to be poured out upon the land." [^3]

Mormon movements westward were correlated with their effort to build a New Jerusalem in Jackson County, Missouri. Success in this venture was to depend upon certain factors, one of which was their obedience to Zion's law—a new socio-economic program upon which they were to build their holy city. But even before they located its site a revelation warned that by failure to live Zion's law they would be "cut off" from their anticipated "inheritances." [^4] And before the site was dedicated another revelation declared:

Ye cannot behold with your natural eyes, for the present time, the design of your God concerning those things which shall come hereafter, and the glory which shall follow after much tribulation. For after much tribulation come the blessings.... The hour is not yet, but is nigh at hand. [^5]

"Remember this, which I tell you before," the revelation admonished, "that you may lay it to heart, and receive that which is to follow." [^6]

Again, in September, 1831, a revelation declared that "the rebellious shall be cut off out of the land of Zion, and shall be sent away." [^7] But despite these warnings, dissension arose, and in January, 1833, the Prophet wrote, from Kirtland, Ohio, stating that if Zion would not purify herself so as to be approved in all things, God would "seek another people." [^8] The following July, mob elements came against the Saints in Missouri. When they had been driven from their lands, Joseph Smith explained:

I have always expected that Zion would suffer some affliction, from what I could learn from the commandments which have been given. But I would remind you of a certain clause in one which says, that after much tribulation cometh the blessings.... But how many will be the days of her purification, tribulation, and affliction, the Lord has kept from my eyes. [^9]

A few months later, as the Saints were gathering to other areas of Missouri, the Prophet chastized them for their waywardness, implying that if they would obey Zion's law they might yet establish themselves and in time return to Jackson County. Otherwise they "would be driven from state to state, from city to city, from one abiding place to another." [^10] Said he:

You say I am a Prophet. Well, then, I will prophesy, and when you go home write it down and remember it. You think you have been badly treated by your enemies; but if you don't do better than you are doing, I prophesy that the state of Missouri will not hold you. Your sufferings have hardly commenced. [^11]

Building the New Jerusalem upon the law of Zion was held to be a necessary factor in establishing a lasting peace in America. If the Saints failed to do their part, God would allow their enemies to drive them from their lands and the judgments they would receive would thus be justified. Much depended upon the establishment of Zion's law, and God would hold the Saints largely responsible. But there was also another side of the issue. The attitude the gentiles (people of European extraction [^12]) took toward the project was considered equally as important as a factor in establishing the holy city. If the gentiles would embrace Zion's law, the Book of Mormon stated, the New Jerusalem would be built with their assistance, as a blessing unto them. Otherwise the "gospel" would be taken "from among them" and severe judgments would follow. [^13]

Since Americans in general were not disposed to accept the claims of the newly revealed gospel, Orson Pratt repeatedly commented that "the Book of Mormon would never have been fulfilled if the Saints had not left the gentiles as a people," and migrated to the Great Basin. [^14] Thus students of Mormonism were not disturbed when the Saints were driven from Missouri and the building of their sacred city was indefinitely postponed. Pratt, for one, reasoned from the revelations "that it would be very many years before Zion would be redeemed." [^15] It was with peculiar meaning that the Prophet also wrote, "Do not think that our hearts faint as though some strange thing had happened unto us, for we have seen and been assured of all these things beforehand." [^16]

During their movements westward, the Saints accepted certain obligations toward their fellow Americans, including the responsibility to teach them Zion's law of peace and union, to warn them of coming judgments, and to gather the obedient "out from Babylon." In January, 1838, John Smith wrote to his son of the latter tasks, stating, "There is a very fierce and terrible war close to our doors, and the righteous will scarcely escape, saith the Lord; therefore, they should assemble themselves together with all convenient speed and be diligent in informing their neighbors, etc." [^17]

To better facilitate the work of teaching, warning, and gathering the people, Kirtland, Ohio, was retained as "a place of preparatory gathering," even though the body of the Church had for some time moved westward. Don Carlos Smith, the Prophet's brother, wrote from Kirtland, in January, 1838:

...there is none that embrace the Gospel, but has some friends and relatives whom they wish to warn thoroughly in order that their skirts may be clean (and truly the blood of their friends will be upon them unless they do their duty), and they have a better opportunity to warn their friends by gathering to this place, than they otherwise would.... [Meanwhile,] it is indispensably necessary that the first Elders...should go where it will be more for the upbuilding of the kingdom of God, and also to prepare a place for the Saints against the day of trial, when the hot displeasure of God shall be upon the wicked, etc. [^18]

Even before the Mormons left Ohio and Missouri Joseph Smith was laying plans to move the Church to the Rocky Mountains. [^19] In many ways the building of Nauvoo, Illinois, was another temporary gathering place, where the Saints could carry out their responsibilities to the world while adding numbers to their cause. [^20] But problems arose in initiating these policies. To some at Kirtland the westward vision grew dim, and others in Illinois even contemplated moving back. William Law was counseled by revelation not to take his family back to Kirtland, for God had "a scourge prepared for the inhabitants thereof." [^21] That same year Hyrum Smith declared it to be the will of God that those in Kirtland "come away." Said he, "Therefore pay out no moneys, nor properties for houses, nor lands in that country, for if you do you will lose them, for the time shall come, that you shall not possess them in peace." [^22]
