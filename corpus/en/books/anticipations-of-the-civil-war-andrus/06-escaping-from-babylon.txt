Hyrum L. Andrus, Anticipations of the Civil War in Mormon Thought ( 1966)

Escaping from Babylon

The mob murder of Joseph and Hyrum Smith at Carthage, Illinois, with the subsequent expulsion of the Latter-day Saints from the United States, signaled the nation's failure to meet one of its most vital obligations to justice. The death of the Prophet and his brother, said Brigham Young, "sealed up the damnation of the United States, not of individuals, but of the nation." [^1] Orson Spencer explained further that it was the "word of the Lord" on the eve of the Carthage tragedy that if their enemies began "to shed blood, the sword shall waste the blood of the nation." [^2] Orson Hyde likewise emphasized that "the mouth of the Lord" had decreed that no "mortal arm" could then "save the American nation from wrath and indignation, tribulation and anguish." [^3] As he wrote, the Mormons were being driven from their homes and lands in Illinois. And while they trudged westward, he cited the Book of Mormon edict: "Behold the wrath of God cometh upon the people of this land when the cup of their iniquity is full." Hyde then explained one further stipulation to the coming of judgment:

But the cup of their iniquity is not full until they cast out the Saints. Americans! do you set the seal of your approbation to the act of driving out the Saints? If you do, I tremble for your fate. [^4]

When the Mormons bound up the testimony, sealed up the law and headed West, Brigham Young explained, "The word of the Lord to us was: 'Gather up my people, and flee to the mountains, and hide yourselves, and there wait until you shall see the hand of the Lord made bare, and the wrath of the Almighty poured out upon the wicked nation that has consented to the death of my prophets.' " [^5] But one further duty devolved upon them before they left. Letters were written to the governors of all the states, requesting an asylum within their respective borders. These requests were refused "either by silent contempt or a flat denial in every instance." But Brigham Young later explained, "We did this to deprive them of the chance of saying at the day of judgment, 'You could have had an asylum with us if you had applied for it.' " [^6]

At the time of the Exodus, Mormon authorities made every effort to remove all their people to the West. Brigham Young addressed an epistle to the scattered Saints, reminding them that "the spirit of prophecy had long since portrayed in the Book of Mormon what might be the conduct of this nation towards the Israel of the last days." Said he:

You all know and have doubtless felt for years the necessity of a removal provided the government should not be sufficiently protective to allow us to worship God according to the dictates of our own conscience.... The ranklings of violence and intolerance and religious and political strife that have long been waking up in the bosom of this nation, together with the occasional scintillations of settled vengeance, and blood guiltiness cannot long be suppressed. And deplorable is the condition of any people that is constrained to be the butt of such discord and revolutionary materials. The direful eruption must take place.... We therefore write unto you beloved brethren, as wise men that will forsee the evil and hide yourselves until the indignation be overpast. [^7]

Similar epistles were written by other Mormon authorities throughout the nation. From Peterborough, New Hampshire, J. C. Little wrote, April 6, 1846, to the Saints in the Eastern and Middle States, repeating the above admonitions and concluding, "We, in the name of Jesus, call upon you all to give heed to this counsel, before it is too late." [^8] As Orson Pratt left the East to join in the exodus, he published a farewell address from New York City, exhorting the Saints to "depart for the Rocky Mountains." Four days later a conference was held in that area, at which he encouraged "a union of action" that the prophecies of the Book of Mormon might "be fulfilled." [^9] The following resolution, among others, was then passed by the assembled Saints:

Inasmuch as the people and authorities of the United States have sanctioned such proceedings [as mob action and the denial of their rights], without manifesting any disposition to sustain us in our constitutional rights, but have rejected our many petitions to judges, governors, and presidents for the last twelve years, having hardened their hearts like Pharaoh of old, against the cries of the fatherless and the widow—That we now cease our cries, wipe away our tears, and prepare ourselves to "enter into our chambers and shut our doors about us for a little season until the indignation be overpast." Therefore,...

Resolved, that the Church in this city move, one and all, west of the Rocky Mountains between this and next season, either by land or water; and that we most earnestly pray all our brethren in the eastern country to join with us in this determination, and carry it out effectually. [^10]

Emphasis continued to be placed upon gathering the Saints, after church headquarters had been established in the Great Salt Lake Basin. "It will be as much as the Saints can do to gather out," Heber C. Kimball observed, as he spoke of coming judgments. [^11] In January, 1853, Orson Pratt commenced the publication of The Seer, at Washington, D. C., where he was sent to encourage the gathering and to write in the interest of the Church. In the new organ's "Prospectus," he reviewed the unsuccessful efforts made by the Saints to obtain their constitutional rights and urged all who remained to "flee to the mountains for refuge," as "the Lord's controversy for his people" was near. [^12]

There was little doubt in Mormon circles as to the outcome of future events after the Prophet's death. By the summer of 1844 the coming of the Civil War was to them a virtual reality. In 1847 the Millennial Star requested that it be published abroad that God had "decreed and sworn that he will feed the nation with speedy judgments." [^13] Again, the Mormon periodical declared that "a little stronger draught from the unflinching hand of the Northern Puritan, or from the elastic hand of the Chivalrick Southerner" would sever the union. [^14] Brigham Young tersely declared in 1849 that "God Almighty will give the United States a pill that will puke them to death," then added: "and that is worse than lobelia." [^15] And Orson Hyde went beyond the point of dogmatic assertion when he stated in 1858 that just "so sure as the storms of the mountains burst and hurl their fury upon the Twin Peaks of the Wasatch range, just so sure is the storm of Jehovah's wrath about to burst upon the nation and people of the United States." When Eastern papers countered with some "ludicrous editorials" on what "Prophet Orson" had said, he reaffirmed his statement in a letter to the Missouri Republican. [^16]

By 1857, Heber C. Kimball was confident that in the course of ten years God would "play with this nation as he did with Pharaoh, only worse." [^17] Brigham Young likewise looked to a near commencement of these events. When misrepresentations sent to the East to stir popular feeling against the Mormons, coupled with the desire of certain southern politicians to deplete the federal store of arms, resulted in an army being sent against the Saints, in 1857, Brigham Young reasoned that if they could "avert the blow for another season," people in the States would probably "have enough to attend to at home, without worrying the Latter-day Saints." [^18] To Captain Maurice Van Vleit of the United States Army, he declared that he cared not how many troops might be sent against the Mormons. When the Captain inquired if he had counted the cost, he replied, "Yes, for this people I have; but I cannot estimate it for the United States; for if they actually persist in their present tyrannical course, before they get through they will want to let the job to sub-contractors." Later he added, "They do not know the captain of the armies of Israel; and although they profess to believe in him, they do not realize that he is about to hold a controversy with them for their iniquity." [^19] The Mormon leader declared emphatically that "the coming of that army was the entering wedge to split the Government of the United States in pieces, and that soon." He even sent word to Congress "that it would prove the opening wedge for the struggle of war between the North and the South." [^20]

Though positive and at times even dogmatic, the Mormon attitude was more sorrowful than belligerent toward what they saw. Several times Joseph Smith advised: "Never be anxious for the Lord to pour out his judgments upon this nation; many of you will see distress and evils poured out upon this nation till you will weep like children." [^21] He lamented in 1835: "Oh, that I could snatch them from the vortex of misery, into which I behold them plunging themselves, by their sins; that I might be enabled by the warning voice, to be an instrument of bringing them to unfeigned repentance." [^22] With few exceptions this attitude characterized the feelings of the Mormon people. From the Great Salt Lake Basin they wrote to friends in the East, "We'll salt the Union for you, if you can't preserve it in any other way." [^23] With a note of deep sorrow, Brigham Young said in 1863:

If they had known Joseph Smith in his true character, and acknowledged him, he would have lead this nation long before now, and this dreadful war would not have prevailed throughout the land; but now it will be death upon death, and blood upon blood until the land is cleansed. I pray to God to save the innocent, the righteous, and the honest poor, and let those who desire to shed the blood of each other fall first in this deadly strife. [^24]

John Taylor agreed that the Civil War would not have occurred had the nation "been under the rule and dictation of a Prophet, governed by the principles of light and intelligence...from heaven." [^25] As it was, the Saints felt that God had directed them in their afflictions and had overruled the acts of men, making them "the manifest monuments of his mercy and care." [^26] In the words of Brigham Young, the Rocky Mountains provided "the best place on the earth for the Latter-day Saints." There "the Lord designed to hide His people." The fact that they had been brought to that area was "one of the greatest blessings that could have been visited upon" them. [^27]

The Mormon people more correctly discerned the signs of the times than any of their contemporary Americans. Joseph Smith had even anticipated them and their outcome. With reference to a revelation in 1831 that called the Saints "to go to the western countries," Wilford Woodruff acknowledged, "When it was given no man among us knew anything about Salt Lake or the Rocky Mountains; but it has been fulfilled before our eyes." [^28] In faith the Saints accepted the dictates of their Prophet; and they were not disappointed. When hostilities finally commenced between the North and the South, John Taylor, a convert of 1836, epitomized the Mormon sentiment when he said, "I knew as much as twenty-five years ago that these events would transpire, and I had just as much assurance of it then as I have today." [^29] This prophetic insight adds interesting and unique elements to the story of the Civil War.
