Hyrum L. Andrus, Anticipations of the Civil War in Mormon Thought ( 1966)

The Mormon Experience

As early as 1823, when the Era of Good Feelings was in full bloom, Joseph Smith was apparently aware that he and his future associates would be victims of slander and abuse in the era of conflict ahead. He claimed that he was then informed by a heavenly messenger that falsehoods would be circulated "to destroy" his reputation, while persecution would "rage more and more." [^1] To such extent would this be true, the Book of Mormon declared, that "the blood of the saints" would "cry from the ground" against their assailants. [^2] Here was an awesome forecast of such scenes as the Haun's Mill Massacre, the governor's exterminating order against the Mormons in Missouri, the martyrdom of the Prophet and his brother Hyrum, and, finally, the expulsion of the Saints from the United States.

As expected, a flood of insinuations, misrepresentations, and slanders was sent out against Mormonism from every quarter. It was observed that "every foolish thing that a giddy headed generation could invent" was used to oppose the new movement. [^3] When Jared Carter commenced to investigate the Mormon claims, he "was told all sorts of stories concerning Joseph Smith and the golden Bible." [^4] From Virginia, George A. Smith wrote, "All the foolish stories that malice and enmity can invent are industriously circulated against the truth." [^5] Meanwhile, in Massachusetts there were those who "published many evil things ... about the cause and refused to insert any answers." [^6] Commented a Mormon editor on the prevailing situation. "No sooner has a slander been put in circulation, (by no matter who,) than it has been seized with eagerness, and trumpeted throughout the world of Christendom by every sect and party." [^7] The Mormon organ later described the conditions to which converts were subject, explaining that, while their "former friends may have been strong advocates of civil and religious liberty," they would now deprive the Saints of the freedom of speech and the right to hold public office. [^8]

A widespread disregard for law and justice was the natural fruit of the times. It was estimated that between 1835 and 1860 the South experienced over 300 lynchings. [^9] And, measured by the experience of the Saints, the North was not without its disorders. While preaching the Mormon faith in Pennsylvania, in 1836, Erastus Snow was mauled and beaten by a mob with a justice of the peace at its head, who threatened further bodily injury if Snow failed to leave the country. The Mormon elder appealed to his rights under the law. "But they cared not for civil law," he reported; "they said they would take the Lynch law." [^10] In New England, Orson Hyde and Samuel H. Smith were forced to flee for their lives after preaching but a short time in Providence, Rhode Island. [^11] Daniel Stephens described a mob with blackened faces, that yelled like savages, threw stones, broke in windows, and knocked down some of the Saints. [^12] And Jonathan Crosby, Jr., wrote from Wendell, Massachusetts: "We are like sheep among wolves who are howling on every side; and the same spirit is manifest here that was shown toward our brethren in the west; and they have even ventured so far as to trample on the laws of the land." [^13]

In the West, the Quincy Whiq, at Quincy, Illinois, publicly ordered all Mormons to "depart from this neighborhood," threatening that if they would not go "other measures will be resorted to soon." [^14] When a group of Mormon converts in neighboring Hancock Country was threatened with violence, the militia was called out, but it refused to protect the Saints and retreated "in the face of a gentile mob." [^15] Another small group of Mormons tried to attend a camp meeting conducted by the Reverend Peter Cartwright, but they were ordered off the premises and told not to return. "If you do," Cartwright threatened, "you will get Lynch's law." [^16] In Ohio, John Murdock reported that certain elements of opposition came "out with mob spirit in battle array against us, being set on by Priests, Doctors and Lawyers." [^17] When Parley P. Pratt attempted to preach at Mentor, his antagonists locked him out of the church, procured a drum, a fife, and several eggs; and when he yet persisted in preaching, they marched back and forth in front of him and threw the eggs in cadence. A stranger present approached a local deacon and inquired, "Is this the way you worship in this country?" "Oh, no, Sir," replied the deacon, "that man is a 'Mormon."' Said the inquirer, "His talk is very reasonable." "Yes," mused the deacon gravely, "but he is a 'Mormon,' and we do not intend that he shall preach here." "He appears very cool," commented the stranger. "Yes," came the reply, "he is used to it, he has been in such scrapes before." [^18]

Mormon persecution stemmed primarily from religious issues. But in areas where the Saints had gathered in large numbers, the priests, as Brigham Young put it, started "pinching the 'Mormons' from behind the politicians." [^19] And thus the conflict was intensified. By such accumulated opposition the Saints were driven from Jackson Country, Missouri, and finally en masse from the state. In the meantime, disrupting circumstances had resulted in a mass evacuation of the Saints from Kirtland, Ohio, and adjacent areas.

Open hostilities subsided for a time after the Mormons arrived in Illinois, but again mobocracy gained the ascendancy, and the sentiment became prevalent that the Saints must be "crushed," with or without law. They were informed that it would "be in vain" for them to oppose the "public will" and that they should leave the state before "the last extreme is resorted to—THAT OF FORCE." [^20] This attitude, with the mob murder of Joseph and Hyrum Smith and the atrocities that were later visited upon the Mormon people, was justified in the minds of such influential men as the Reverend Peter Cartwright by the comment that "no redress at law" could cope with the problem. [^21] But others less moved by prevailing sentiment took a clearer view of the matter. The editor of the Mississippian, published at Rock Island, Illinois, about seventy miles from Nauvoo, viewed the "horrible picture with utter disgust and lothing" and declared that it was time for every citizen and officer, regardless of the cost, to impartially "stand forward and defend, maintain, and obey the laws." [^22] Another non-Mormon writer also referred to the reckless disregard for the lives and property of others and concluded: "I have been more than shocked at the unreflecting portion (which is a large majority) of the conductors of the public press in our community, for giving a wide-spread publicity through their columns of the most incendiary, exciting and exaggerated accounts of the rumored proceedings of the contending parties, thereby flaming into flame the already burning firebrands of discard and strife." [^23] These trends, Joseph Smith maintained, had to be checked else they would lead to civil war.
