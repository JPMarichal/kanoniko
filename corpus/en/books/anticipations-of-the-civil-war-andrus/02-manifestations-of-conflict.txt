Hyrum L. Andrus, Anticipations of the Civil War in Mormon Thought ( 1966)

Manifestations of Conflict

Several conflicting forces were manifest in American society during the second quarter of the nineteenth century. The desire for reform and perfectionism was strong; and many placed renewed emphasis upon the reality of spiritual fact. But the period also witnessed the development of social conflict that finally overshadowed the more noble desires of men and bathed the nation in blood and strife.

Students of American history associate the signs of conflict to which Joseph Smith pointed with the rise of those sectional forces that culminated in the Civil War. The manifestation of these new portending powers deep within society evidenced a withdrawal of social union, accompanied by the growth of hatred and animosity. For example, Ralph Waldo Emerson later reported that, while "the thoughtful and virtuous" manifested "a deeper belief and reliance on spiritual facts, there was in all the practical activities of New England ...a gradual withdrawal of tender consciences from the social organization." [^1] Hezekiah Niles also noted the shifting sentiment toward conflict. In the first week of September, 1835, he clipped over five hundred items reporting acts of violence and disorder from newspapers throughout the country. "Society seems everywhere unhinged, and the demon of 'blood and slaughter' has been let loose upon us," he exclaimed. "The character of our countrymen seems suddenly changed." [^2]

The Messenger and Advocate had earlier observed that "every mail brings new accounts of mobs and riots." The Mormon organ then warned that "a people that profess to be Christian must maintain the dignity they have assumed and proclaimed to the world, by righteous acts, or else they must fall and fester in their own infamy." [^3] Again, in 1842, a Mormon chronicler reported that "riots and mobs are multiplying in the land at this time." [^4] In 1855 George A. Smith observed that half the columns in newspapers were taken up with "accounts of murder, suicide, plunder, bloodshed, and every other species of crime." Smith, the Prophet's cousin, held that these circumstances existed because "the people have rejected the truth, and therefore the light of truth has ceased to shine in their hearts." He admonished the people to read the revelations of Joseph Smith "and see how rapidly they are fulfilling, and observe how partyism and constant wrangling are seizing the human mind, and how tremendously they will contend with each other, and sustain one another in lies, and speak evil of those who are good." [^5] Brigham Young also spoke in the terminology of the Spirit by observing, in 1854, that God was "operating with this nation, and taking away their wisdom, until by-and-by they will get mad and rush to certain destruction." [^6] Again he emphasized that the Spirit was being "withdrawn from the people." Consequently, they would "whet the knife" and cross the Mason-Dixon line "to destroy each other, and the sword and fire will be prevalent in the land." [^7]

The seeds of dissension were ubiquitous, and after 1830 they developed rapidly. Religiously, new winds were blowing over America, and quarrels, contentions, and slanders characterized the churches. Even the peaceful Quakers and the newly formed Unitarians were not exempt. [^8] Among northern reformers the trend toward radical abolitionism began to develop, while the tone of the southern defense of slavery markedly changed. When the positive and radical cry of the abolitionist was scunded in the North, the reaction in the South became equally extreme and uncompromising as emotion reacted to emotion. [^9]

The incipient sectionalism gained its strength from several sources, among which was a widespread hatred and animosity among the people. "Many a time in America have I been conscious of that pang and shudder which are felt only in the presence of hatred," Harriet Martineau reported, as she observed the animosity Americans expressed toward "fellow citizens in distant sections of their country." [^10] She also witnessed the existence of a lying, insinuating attitude in many circles. [^11] Newspapers during the period were often little short of being blackmailers and blackguards, [^12] particularly in reporting on minority groups who did not conform to prevailing opinions. Earlier there had existed a wide-spread interest in new ideas and a general willingness to discuss even the most radical opinions. But with the change of temper, Emerson observed that for non-conformity "the world whips you with its displeasure." [^13] And Alexis de Tocqueville declared that "in America the majority raises formidable barriers around liberty of opinion," so that he who went beyond accepted bounds was exposed to continual persecution where "those who blame him criticize loudly," while those "who think as he does keep quiet and move away without courage." [^14] The prevailing trends were manifest in different ways in various sections of the country. John Taylor, a prominent figure in the Mormon movement, explained:

In the East, they do it [express their hatred and animosity] with their tongues; they do not use the bowie knives, pistols, and rifles so much as in the West and in the South; but a spirit of rancour, animosity, and hatred seems to be engendered in their very midst. Every man's hand is against his neighbour. [^15]

Examples of disregard for the character and rights of others in that day are numerous. When the Reverend Theodore Parker delivered his noted address, "Transient and Permanent," there arose such a howl of disapproving voices that few of his colleagues in the ministry dared defend him. A similar attack was made upon Bronson Alcott's Conversations. But even worse was the reaction to "The Laboring Classes," Orestes A. Brownson's penetrating essay. The Whigs used it as political cannon fodder against Brownson's colleagues, the Democrats. After trying unsuccessfully to call Brownson off, the Democrats disowned him. One writer explained apologetically that the Massachusetts Democrats were no more responsible for Brownson than the Whigs were for Mormonism; and the Methodist Quarterly Review accused him of all that was subversive, including free love.

William Lloyd Garrison, representing the more radical segment of society, gave his fellow citizens ample opportunity to test their willingness to sustain constitutional rights. Martineau witnessed mob action against Garrison in 1835 and later spoke to several prominent citizens about the incident:

An eminent lawyer at Boston was one of the next to speak upon it. "O, there was no mob," said he, "I was there myself, and saw they were all gentlemen. They were all in fine broadcloth."

"Not the less a mob for that," said I.

"Why, they protected Garrison. He received no harm. They protected Garrison."

"From whom, or what?"

"O, they would not really hurt him. They only wanted to show that they would not have such a person live among them."

"Why should not he live among them? Is he guilty under any law?"

"He is an insufferable person to them."

"So may you be to-morrow. If you can catch Garrison breaking the laws, punish him under the laws. If you cannot, he has as much right to live where he pleases as you." [^16]

As further examples of such disruptions, Martineau listed the abolition riots at New York and Cincinnati; "the burning of the Charleston Convent; the hangings by Lynch-law at Vicksburg; the burning alive of a man of colour at St. Louis; [and] the subsequent proceedings there towards the students of Marion college." [^17] Here was a partial, but fearful, list.
