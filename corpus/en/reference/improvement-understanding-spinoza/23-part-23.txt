Baruch Spinoza, On the Improvement of the Understanding ( 1662)

Part 23

(1) In order that the whole matter may be put in a clearer light, I will make use of a single illustration as follows.

(2) Three numbers are given—it is required to find a fourth, which shall be to the third as the second is to the first.

(3) Tradesmen will at once tell us that they know what is required to find the fourth number, for they have not yet forgotten the rule which was given to them arbitrarily without proof by their masters; others construct a universal axiom from their experience with simple numbers, where the fourth number is self-evident, as in the case of 2, 4, 3, 6; here it is evident that if the second number be multiplied by the third, and the product divided by the first, the quotient is 6; when they see that by this process the number is produced which they knew beforehand to be the proportional, they infer that the process always holds good for finding a fourth number proportional.
